Last week, the White House announced the withdrawal of Lance Schroyer, President Trump’s nominee to direct Immigration and Customs Enforcement. No explanation was offered, though Schroyer, a former Oklahoma state trooper and a confidant of Secretary of Homeland Security Markwayne Mullin, had been criticized for lack of experience in immigration and in leading large organizations.

In some ways, a vacancy atop ICE is normal: The agency hasn’t had a Senate-confirmed leader since the day Trump took office in 2017. But the lack of a permanent director is especially notable right now because of the emphasis Trump has put on immigration and the major operations ICE has undertaken during his second term.

In the absence of a director, leadership has been split between an acting director, the so-called border czar Tom Homan, the secretary, and the Trump senior adviser Stephen Miller; the administration’s tactics have been volatile, changing often and abruptly. Among many other scandals, ICE agents keep shooting people, some of them American citizens. The department has struggled to recruit and train qualified agents to conduct the mass deportations Trump wants. The vacuum at the top has allowed loose cannons such as the Border Patrol officer Gregory Bovino to (temporarily) seize huge authority. Probably no director could calm all of these problems, but the lack of a Senate-confirmed leader cannot help instill discipline and organization.

This is not an isolated case. Trump has stridently attacked what he calls the “deep state,” the corps of permanent civil servants whom the president and some of his aides believe are either layabouts or liberal saboteurs, who, regardless, are determined to bog down his agenda. Using this dubious claim as a basis, the administration has struck serious blows against the civil service. Hundreds of thousands of employees have been fired or pushed out, protections have been eliminated, and there are plans to convert many positions into political appointments.

Yet layered above the supposed deep state is what we might call the shallow state: the class of employees who are appointed by each new administration, some subject to Senate confirmation and others not. By intention or negligence, Trump has also left the shallow state riddled with vacancies. The Partnership for Public Service tracks 823 top appointed roles and finds that Trump hasn’t even nominated people for 244 of them. Some of the most notable vacancies are for jobs that would have a central role in implementing Trump’s priorities.

The most glaring case is the Pentagon. The United States is currently sinking deeper into a war with Iran. The president cannot consistently define objectives; no one can explain how or when the war might end, with or without U.S. victory, and the global economy is trembling. But the Defense Department is dealing with this growing crisis with many major jobs open. The department has no secretary of the Army, since Dan Driscoll resigned after feuding with Secretary Pete Hegseth. The Army also has no chief of staff, its top position, after Hegseth forced out General Randy George; senators of both parties are skeptical of Christopher LaNeve, whom Hegseth wants to replace George. Hegseth has ordered a 20 percent reduction in the number of four-star positions, so that allotted positions are left open. The U.S. reportedly faces a serious shortage of munitions, which will take years to fix, but Trump has no assistant secretary for acquisition and no nominee for the role.

The rest of the national-security and foreign-policy apparatus is also understaffed. The National Counterterrorism Center, which coordinates federal-government efforts to fight terror, has had no director since Joe Kent resigned in protest of the Iran war (not that Kent, a conspiracy theorist with fringe views, was a wise choice to lead NCTC in the first place). Despite being embroiled in a war in the Persian Gulf, the U.S. has no ambassadors to the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Kuwait, or Iraq; it also doesn’t have an ambassador in Yemen, where a rebel group has grabbed some control over another waterway. The U.S. is treating Venezuela as a de facto colony but has no ambassador in Caracas. The president wants to end the war in Ukraine, but he has not sent an ambassador to either Kyiv or Moscow, preferring to rely on the envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, his son-in-law.

To be sure, some of the vacancies in the executive branch are a result of Trump’s desire to eliminate or hamstring bodies such as the U.S. Agency for International Development, the Department of Education, and the Federal Election Commission. But plenty of other posts remain unfilled despite their jobs being directly connected to top Trump priorities. Since Todd Blanche was promoted to attorney general, leading the conversion of the Justice Department into a political weapon, Trump has not named a replacement to serve as deputy attorney general. He has proposed major changes to the 2030 census, but the Census Bureau does not have a director. Likewise, he wants to reorganize the National Park Service, but no one has been nominated to lead it. The administration is wrestling with serious water shortages in western reservoirs, but since it withdrew its nominee to head the Bureau of Reclamation, which oversees dams, a year ago, no new commissioner has been named.

The Trump administration is, I have written, pushing a kind of big-government conservatism. Trump’s agenda seeks to wield the power of the state more aggressively and intrusively than any other Republican presidency in memory. Some of those efforts have been chillingly effective, but Trump’s undermining of both the shallow and the deep states means that his government struggles to execute even on his most cherished initiatives. That’s bad news for Trump’s agenda, but it might be good news for democracy.

Egypt’s Warning to Israel

When Israel’s leaders gathered in Jerusalem on September 26, 2023, to mark the 50th anniversary of the Yom Kippur War, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu described the surprise assault by Syria and Egypt as a cautionary tale, one of missed intelligence warnings and self-delusion. “We do not underestimate any threat,” he said.

Roughly two hours before Netanyahu took the stage, a chartered Cessna jet linked to Egypt’s intelligence service had touched down at Ben Gurion Airport, about 30 miles away. The jet rolled to a stop in an isolated section of the airport used for sensitive diplomatic and security missions. Abbas Kamel, Egypt’s intelligence chief and one of the region’s most influential security officials, had come to Israel on a most sensitive mission.

Hamas, the Muslim Brotherhood–aligned terrorist group that controlled Gaza, appeared to be mobilizing for a major attack, Kamel said, according to Israeli, Egyptian, Arab, U.S., and European officials who were briefed about his visit after October 7. The window to prevent a disaster was closing. He recommended that the Israeli government grant Hamas economic concessions to avert conflict. His message, according to one former Israeli official later briefed on the visit, was: If you don’t change course, Gaza is going to blow up in your faces.

Kamel didn’t provide Israeli officials with a date or specific information on what turned out to be a multipronged attack on Israel less than two weeks later, on October 7. And it’s not clear exactly with whom he met. The Egyptian plane remained on the ground in Israel for 67 minutes, according to an Israeli-airport-authority flight log, before returning to Cairo. (Independent flight-tracking applications confirm the plane’s journey.)

What the Israeli officials did with Kamel’s warning remains an open question three years after the most devastating surprise attack in Israel’s history. But officials from seven different countries I spoke with said that Kamel’s visit should have set off alarms across the top ranks of the Israeli government. Netanyahu attended a classified briefing following the Yom Kippur War commemoration, according to his public schedule, and soon after approved some economic concessions to Hamas. Officials who spoke with me stressed that the mere presence of the Egyptian intelligence chief in Israel should have demonstrated the urgency of the issue. “The messenger was the message,” a former Israeli official told me. “There is no world,” this official said, where top Israeli officials meet with the head of Egyptian intelligence “and Bibi is not aware.”

Netanyahu’s office, in the days after the attack, denied that he’d received any warnings, directly or indirectly, from Egypt over the situation in Gaza. Spokesmen for Netanyahu and Egyptian President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi didn’t respond to my repeated requests for comment when I asked about Kamel’s visit. The then-head of Netanyahu’s national-security council, Tzachi Hanegbi, denied to me this week that there had been a visit from Kamel or an Egyptian warning of any kind.

Kamel’s 2023 visit, details of which have not been previously reported, underscores what dozens of sources have told me: that the Israeli government was repeatedly told of threatening activity by Hamas in the lead-up to October 7.

A United Nations envoy who was in the Middle East at the time publicly warned of a latent conflict that could bring “grave consequences” while privately scrambling to defuse tensions between Israel and Hamas. The president of the United Arab Emirates, Mohamed bin Zayed, called Netanyahu to warn about the possibility of an imminent attack, after being briefed on intelligence findings from Egyptian and Palestinian sources, according to U.S. and Arab officials with knowledge of the call. Earlier this month, Israel’s Haaretz reported the leaders’ call; I confirmed with multiple officials that the call took place. Netanyahu denied the report and has threatened to sue Haaretz. The UAE didn’t respond to a request for comment.

[Read: Netanyahu just admitted he’s unfit to lead Israel]

Current and former Israeli, Arab, and U.S. officials tell me that they all experienced the same phenomenon in 2023: an arrogance on the part of Netanyahu bred by the deep-seated belief that Israel had the upper hand against Hamas. “It seemed clear that, for whatever reason, the West Bank was their priority,” a former senior U.S. official involved in talks with the Israeli government told me. “They were confident—even cocky—that they had the situation under control.”

The Hamas attack, 11 days after Kamel’s visit, resulted in the deaths of approximately 1,200 people, primarily civilians, and the abduction into Gaza of 251 hostages during coordinated raids on communities, military bases, and an outdoor music festival. Israel launched a crushing retaliatory campaign that killed more than 73,000 Palestinians in Gaza, the majority of whom were civilians, according to the territory’s Health Ministry. Hamas’s leadership and thousands of combatants have been killed. The attacks on Israel and the Gaza war that followed produced one of the more devastating humanitarian tragedies of the 21st century.

The Netanyahu government’s stunning lack of preparation before the October 7 invasion is perhaps the most important issue in next month’s Israeli election. Netanyahu, who has been prime minister longer than any other politician in Israel’s history, is facing the most serious challenge of his career.  

Many Israelis, including Netanyahu’s main rival, the former military chief of staff Gadi Eisenkot, have demanded an independent state inquiry into the military and intelligence failures in the run-up to the October 7 attacks. But Netanyahu has continuously refused to set up the kind of independent inquiry that followed past security failures, including the Yom Kippur War. The prime minister has instead attempted to establish a politically appointed commission of inquiry, which he said would include opposition representatives. The attempt was highly controversial; opposition leaders have refused to advance the proposal over concerns that the probe would favor Netanyahu’s government. While the wait for an inquiry continues, dozens of current and former Israeli, Egyptian, Arab, European, and U.S. officials have spoken with me, and their accounts help tell the story of what went wrong that fall.

Since Egypt and Israel signed the Camp David peace accords, in 1978, their relationship can be described as, at best, a cold peace. But the two nations have a common desire to subdue the threat of Hamas and other militant groups in Gaza, which borders both countries. Intelligence sharing between Egypt and Israel has thrived, particularly since al-Sisi became president, in 2014—even when their diplomatic relationship founders.

Most of the intelligence reports in the weeks before the October 7 attacks, including Egypt’s, pointed to the renewal of conventional attacks—such as rocket fire—by Hamas and other armed factions in Gaza. They also warned of a spillover of violence into the West Bank. But intelligence sources inside Gaza conveyed to Egypt a more ominous picture, leading Egypt and the UAE to flag to Israel the prospect that something unprecedented was in the works. “For Kamel to go to Israel” for a single hour, an Egyptian official told me about the September 2023 visit, “meant that al-Sisi himself felt: You need to go there and tell them yourself.”

The Israeli government’s focus, however, was elsewhere. The country was already in the grip of its deepest domestic crisis in decades. For months, Israel had been consumed by Netanyahu’s effort to overhaul the judicial system, a constitutional confrontation that was protested by hundreds of thousands and opened deep fissures within the military and within society. And a surge in Israeli-Palestinian violence in the West Bank had already made 2023 the deadliest year in decades for West Bank Palestinians, with Israeli military raids, Palestinian attacks, and settler violence contributing to the combustible atmosphere. Israel surged military personnel and resources into the territory. Biden-administration officials sought to ease tensions in the West Bank while other international and regional negotiators focused on Gaza.

[Read: Israel can’t postpone its reckoning forever]

Israel established a comprehensive land, sea, and air blockade of Gaza in 2007, when Hamas seized control of the territory, and crossings at Egypt’s border with Gaza, in Rafah, have been tightly restricted since. Freedom of movement became virtually impossible for the 2.1 million people who lived in the 141-square-mile territory. Israel and Hamas had last fought a war there in May 2021. That 11-day conflict ended in a cease-fire—brokered, notably, by Kamel, among others. Hamas appeared to have little interest in abandoning the uneasy truce. But international observers picked up on signs that the situation inside Gaza was changing. Long before the summer of 2023, one international negotiator in the region told me, contacts were reporting that “something was cooking,” though the details were vague and the signals confusing.

By August, tensions in Gaza had clearly heightened, in part because Qatar froze deliveries of Egyptian fuel that it had been sending to Gaza since 2021. Hamas resold the fuel and used the proceeds to help pay public-sector workers, a key mechanism by which it maintained control in the Strip. Hamas called for demonstrations at the Erez border crossing over Qatar’s delayed aid, as well as other lingering disputes like the treatment of Palestinian prisoners. The crossing is the primary pedestrian gateway between Israel and Gaza and an especially important one for Gazan workers. Israel responded to the protests in mid-September by closing the crossing.

Roughly 18,000 Gazans had permits to work in Israel. After those people were barred from going to work, “the escalation started to come much faster,” one negotiator told me. The Arabic outlet Rai al-Youm, citing Palestinian sources, reported on September 26 that Hamas had conveyed to the Egyptian government that Gaza would explode over the Erez closure. That same day, Kamel’s plane flew to Israel.

Negotiators from Egypt, Qatar, and the United Nations scrambled to maintain calm. Hamas officials responded with what one international negotiator described as a broad “shopping list” of demands, including more permits to work inside Israel and the restoration of the fuel-for-public-salaries arrangement with Qatar. After the talks concluded in the final days of September, Hamas agreed to pull people back from the border fence and to try to ease tensions. Israel agreed to reopen the Erez crossing and to restore a medical-supply line into Gaza, adding that it would consider increasing the number of work permits.

Still, the situation remained turbulent. On September 27, 10 days before the attack, Tor Wennesland, the UN’s special coordinator for the Middle East peace process, left Gaza with a stark warning: “The situation inside the Strip is dire and we must avoid another conflict that will have grave consequences for all,” Wennesland wrote on X. A day later, the new arrangement appeared to be taking hold. European diplomats who visited Erez watched as Gazan workers streamed back into Israel.

But negotiators told me it later became clear that Hamas was using the talks as a ploy to buy time while finalizing its attack plans. Documents recovered by Israel from Gaza revealed that Hamas regarded the 2021 conflict as a triumph, and in its aftermath began planning for a breathtakingly large operation. A detailed memorandum from August 2022, apparently handwritten by Yahya Sinwar, then the leader of Hamas in Gaza, laid out much of the operational plan that the group would put into effect the following year. Even as international mediators focused on issues such as work permits and fuel deliveries, believing that these could defuse tensions, Hamas continued its relentless preparations for a massive attack. U.S. and Israeli military analysts told me Israel misinterpreted Hamas’s intentions, concluding that the group had no desire for military confrontation.

Kamel’s visits to Israel are rare. His last known trips were in May and August 2021, following that year’s Israel-Hamas war. After Kamel’s September 2023 trip, Egypt continued to raise the alarm up until two days before the attack, according to U.S., Egyptian, and Israeli officials with direct knowledge of those communications. American and Israeli officials told me it is unclear whether Kamel’s message could have been understood as foretelling the unprecedented attack that followed. Most of the warnings that Israel received in 2023 contained no date, target, or method of attack. But they were frequent and persistent.

Around the same time, Netanyahu received the call from bin Zayed, whose country had normalized relations with Israel only three years earlier. In the call, bin Zayed reportedly sought to convey his assessment that Sinwar was planning a major attack. Like Kamel, bin Zayed urged Netanyahu to make economic concessions to Gaza. But Netanyahu seemed unmoved, two Arab officials familiar with the call told me.

UN negotiators, U.S. officials, and international groups sensed that tensions in Gaza were exceptionally high, but told me they hadn’t been aware of any particular plans for an attack. On September 28, the CIA issued an internal report about a possible Hamas escalation in Gaza, reportedly based in part on foreign intelligence services. U.S. officials told me that report described the possibility that Hamas would launch rockets into Israel over several days. A second report, dated October 5, built on the first but was more analytical than revelatory. Neither detected rehearsals for a coordinated, multipronged attack like the one Hamas carried out two days later.

On October 5, a message from Egypt to Israel also reiterated concerns that the situation was untenable, U.S., Egyptian and Israeli officials said. It provided no date or methodology, but conveyed urgency about a possible attack, the officials said. Egypt also shared its assessments with several other nations in the region, including Saudi Arabia, a senior Gulf Arab official told me. Jordan’s intelligence chief also conveyed concerns to his Israeli counterparts, according to Arab officials, that the situation had grown potentially explosive, especially in the West Bank, and needed to be addressed swiftly. (Spokespeople for Jordan’s government and intelligence service didn’t respond to a request for comment.)

Biden-administration officials told me that neither Egypt nor the UAE shared such warnings with them ahead of October 7. U.S., Egyptian, Arab, and Israeli officials gave me a range of reasons why. Several people told me the warnings were too vague to warrant sharing with the U.S. Other officials, including those from the region, told me it was up to Netanyahu’s government to share the findings with Washington. (U.S. officials I spoke with dismissed this explanation, suggesting that if Egypt or Abu Dhabi had a pressing warning for Israel, Washington should have heard about it from them.) Several months after the attack, CIA Director William Burns was told, almost in passing, about both Kamel’s visit to Israel and bin Zayed’s call, but was not told about the specifics of their outreach, according to U.S., Israeli, and Arab officials.

[Read: Netanyahu doesn’t want the truth to come out]

Two days after the attack, as Israel took stock of the tragedy and its military campaign in Gaza began, Netanyahu’s office rejected press reports that he’d received any outreach from Kamel or Egypt. “The Prime Minister has neither spoken, nor met, with the head of Egyptian intelligence since the formation of the government, neither directly nor indirectly,” his office said on X. But the distinction between direct contact and intelligence passed through aides and other officials would become central to the debate over whether Israel could have done more to anticipate the attacks. On October 11, then–House Foreign Affairs Committee Chair Michael McCaul told reporters after a classified briefing for lawmakers that Egypt had warned Israel. “We know that Egypt had warned the Israelis three days prior that an event like this could happen,” McCaul said. “I don’t want to get too much into classified, but a warning was given. I think the question was at what level.” (McCaul didn’t respond to my repeated attempts to discuss his comments.)

In the months that followed, Israeli officials described a broader failure to interpret the signals coming from Gaza. In leaked recordings of briefings to bereaved families and residents obtained by Israel’s Channel 12, Herzi Halevi, then the chief of staff of the Israel Defense Forces, described the military establishment as having been “lulled to sleep.” In a separate leaked recording, Halevi told local council chiefs that before the attacks, he had regarded Gaza “as the last border that needed attention.”

Halevi resigned from his position in March 2025, and that June, the head of the Israel Security Agency, Ronen Bar, resigned under pressure over intelligence failures linked to the October 7 attacks. Hanegbi, the head of Netanyahu’s security council, was replaced in October 2025. Kamel, who left Egypt’s General Intelligence Service in October 2024, is now an adviser and special envoy for al-Sisi.

Since the October 7 attacks, Israel not only launched a crushing retaliation in Gaza, but later opened military fronts against Lebanon’s Hezbollah and, this year, a war in Iran. It’s impossible to know what would have happened if the warnings from Kamel and others had been heeded at the time, but it’s hard to imagine that Israel wouldn’t have been better prepared. As it was, the attacks caught the Israeli security services flat-footed. Reserve forces weren’t immediately available after kibbutz communities were attacked, leaving them defended primarily by local, lightly armed civilian volunteer forces from about 6:30 a.m., when the attacks began, to 1:30 p.m., when significant IDF reinforcements arrived.

Netanyahu said in a campaign video clip last month that Israeli security officials failed to immediately wake him on the morning of October 7. “In the lead-up to October, there were so many warning signs, so many indications, that an attack was imminent,” Netanyahu said, without offering any specifics. “Had they woken me up, everything would have looked different. That is the story of October 7th—that is the story.”

Today, Gaza remains in ruins and violence continues, despite efforts by the Biden and Trump administrations to secure a cease-fire. An April assessment by the UN, the World Bank, and the European Union estimated that $71.4 billion would be needed over the next decade for Gaza’s recovery and reconstruction. The great majority of its population remains displaced. Meanwhile, the trauma of October 7 lives on for Israelis. And bereaved families from that day continue to search for answers about how Hamas was able to engage in a devastating attack on Israel that appeared to catch the government completely by surprise.

The Show That Always Chased Ratings, No Matter the Cost

To Catch a Predator may have presented a simulation of reality, but it was just convincing enough for viewers. The show’s objective was to apprehend people suspected of child sex abuse—which it did by staging sting operations. Its host, Chris Hansen, and his collaborators at Dateline NBC would sparsely furnish the entryway of some rented suburban home; an adult actor posing as a teenager would open the door for a stranger he or she had been chatting with online; and Hansen, who worked as a Dateline correspondent, would lurk in another room, waiting for the right moment to present himself to the houseguest and laconically intervene. His catchphrase was “Have a seat,” which he would utter while inviting his latest subject to take stock of the nightmare unfolding around him: He was suspected of intending to have sex with a minor, Hansen would explain, and now they were on candid camera—the police waiting just outside.

Hansen’s somewhat maniacal pursuit of what he considered justice was detailed in last year’s very solid documentary Predators, which explored the legal and ethical limits of To Catch a Predator’s blockbuster public-shaming approach. The new movie Primetime, a fictionalized look at the host’s meteoric rise and sudden implosion, also has a critical edge, but it’s much more interested in the weird version of real life that Hansen’s show created. The drama, from the director Lance Oppenheim, plunges the viewer into a sordid vision of early-2000s news entertainment—flashy, shallow, flimsy. And it revolves around an energetic Robert Pattinson performance that always feels a little sweaty and desperate to please the audience.

Primetime is a beautiful nightmare—and might feel seductive to anyone who wanted to work in the world of gaudy TV journalism in 2004. That’s when the first To Catch a Predator segment premiered on NBC. It was followed by another, in 2005, before airing as a more regular series from 2006 to 2007. When the program ended, it did so amid a legal accusation of wrongdoing (the case was settled) and questions about its ethics. But during the years it ran, popular culture portrayed Hansen as a righteous crusader. His method—ensnaring alleged sex offenders in online traps meant to expose their dark proclivities, before turning them over to law enforcement—made for satisfying TV, mixing the trashiness of Jerry Springer–style talk shows with the aesthetics of supposed hard-news journalism.

[Read: Jerry Springer explained it all]

Casting Pattinson, a true movie star, as Hansen should stack the deck in the character’s favor: He dazzles on-screen. The actor has long been drawn to playing nervy oddities or handsome devils with some insecurity simmering beneath the surface. He portrays Hansen as sharklike and occasionally charming, unable to drop the facade he’s created for his show. His smirk is frozen, his voice affected. He seems to be constantly preening for cameras that aren’t always there. Pattinson’s performance comes across as fake in just the right ways—it’s an impersonation of someone who was himself living as an impersonator.

Oppenheim primarily works as a documentarian, and has long been drawn to the anonymous-feeling tract housing and chintzy suburbs that To Catch a Predator made its domain. His documentary Some Kind of Heaven was about a group of retirees living in The Villages, a master-planned neighborhood for senior citizens; Spermworld explored the unregulated online marketplaces for sperm donation, for which people meet in motels to arrange DIY insemination. In Primetime, the director’s first narrative feature, the Dateline crew infiltrate similar types of communities to wreak gleeful havoc in the name of ratings. But the movie doesn’t condemn their efforts so much as it tries to understand how Hansen’s thirst for success infected everyone around him. (The real Hansen has criticized the film, declaring it “detached from reality.”)

The most captivating storyline, in fact, is that of a decoy performer. The aspiring Hollywood actor Dan Plumb (played by Skyler Gisondo) takes the gig because he wants a union card, then quickly finds himself dangled in front of suspected sexual predators with little warning. Gisondo’s performance is as sweet and guileless as Pattinson’s is intense and unsettling; Dan has mighty career ambitions, but he sadly fails to grow beyond his seemingly eternal youth and the specific role Hansen has cast him in. Caught between them is the show’s producer Andie (the incredible Merritt Wever), who suppresses any qualms about her professional ethics in search of the show’s next target.

Oppenheim imagines the tale of To Catch a Predator as a story of America. This is apt, considering how many stories about this country involve people pursuing success at any cost—one that typically ends up being very high. Primetime eventually careers toward disaster (drawn from the real-life incident that ended the show’s run), but any viewer can see that coming. More fascinating to behold is Hansen’s delight as he gets closer and closer to that grand catastrophe.

The moment I knew Resident Evil had me was the ammo check. The protagonist of the director Zach Cregger’s new film is a civilian, a medical courier named Bryan (played by Austin Abrams) who’s been tasked with driving a special package over to nearby Raccoon City in the snow. Quickly enough, strange things start to happen: He almost runs over a woman who seems to be sick with a mysterious virus; as the situation disintegrates, he finds himself fending her off with a dead police officer’s pistol. Amidst all the chaos, Bryan slides out his gun’s magazine to count how many bullets he has left. Confirming how many rounds are in the chamber before venturing further into the darkness is a key aspect of the video-game series that Cregger is adapting. But rarely is that ever shown on the big screen.

Indeed, the seven Resident Evil films that were made before this one (all but one of which starred Milla Jovovich) took the opposite approach. The characters toted comical varieties of automatic weapons, possessed supernatural abilities, performed amazing vehicular stunts, and pumped thousands of bullets into waves of zombies. The new film’s comparative groundedness is perhaps why Cregger’s initial pitch for a fresh iteration of Resident Evil, one of the best-known video-game properties, resonated with Constantin Films, which holds the rights. “I like the idea of somebody with my skill set and my attitude going into this world,” Cregger told me in an interview of that storytelling choice—that is, turning an unassuming young man whose primary concerns include his girlfriend and his sneakers into a mutant-slaying hero.

The director chose not to draw from a specific entry of the game series, which debuted in 1996. Cregger is best known for his first feature film, Barbarian, which announced him as a surprising new talent in the horror world, and for his follow-up, Weapons, which won an Oscar. For his current film, capturing the stressful sensation of actually playing the game was important to him. “Resident Evil is so simple and so inherently cinematic,” Cregger said, noting that he himself plays it “because the game design and the pacing and the dread is so effective. That, to me, is the heart and soul of those games. It is the feeling you get when you walk into a new environment, and you only have two bullets.” Managing resources, making every shot count, and navigating a world where a monster could jump out from behind any corner—they’re fundamental to the feel of this world, he said.

Resident Evil helped create the wildly popular gaming genre known as survival horror, which mixes suspense and action to hook the player. But as with almost any sprawling franchise, it has become burdened by years of continuity. Cregger solves this problem by ignoring most of the backstory for his version. The essential premise is still there: A pharma-bio conglomerate, the Umbrella Corporation, has mistakenly unleashed something called the “T-Virus,” transforming those who are infected into monsters that Bryan must combat in the hopes of survival. The source material usually foregrounds members of law enforcement, most of whom have been featured in past screen adaptations, but Cregger chose to focus on an original character. Bryan fills the role of the average person behind the controller, who’s entered a hostile world barely able to defend himself—forcing him to translate into reality what he’s learned by playing video games.

The result is a rollicking, often ridiculous viewing experience, as Bryan hits some lucky shots early on but also makes some rookie mistakes. In one sequence supercharged with video-game logic, he foolishly discards a ladder that could have obviously helped him escape an attic he’s stuck in; in another, he evades a zombie attacker by simply running in circles around an overturned car. As he progresses, Bryan levels up his arsenal, coming across a shotgun and then an automatic weapon. Cregger’s homage to a nasty element of later Resident Evil installments (namely the fourth, which he has called one of his all-time favorite games) is especially amusing: Executing a headshot to kill an enemy can actually be detrimental, as some new tentacled horror might sprout from the neck wound.

Bryan’s situation is so absurdly terrifying that laughing as it escalates is inevitable. Humor is, of course, the best way to defuse tension or deflate shocking gore; that’s especially true in moments such as Bryan confronting an infected child or a maternity ward full of mutated babies. Cregger started his career as one of the members of the sketch group the Whitest Kids U’ Know, and his previous movies have a darkly funny edge. He also admitted that the Resident Evil games themselves have “a camp to them that’s undeniable.” Yet Cregger seemed to regret the amount of humor he’d deployed in his take—and that was despite the fact that he’d already pulled some jokes out in the editing process.

He had run out the clock, having “turned the movie in at, like, 11:59 p.m. a week before it hit theaters,” he said. Still, he recounted, while watching the finished film, he was thinking, “I could have lost that; I could have cut that.” He’d also done plenty of big edits, other than just culling lines; the climax, in which Bryan makes it to his destination (a fortified Umbrella lab working on a cure to the virus), in particular changed drastically over time. The scene is filled with comedic actors playing frazzled lab attendants, and initially came across as more outwardly zany. “I had to go and make some really painful cuts to some really great performances and funny, clever things that people were doing in that lab and try and keep it hard-core,” Cregger said. Given the audience’s emotional investment in Bryan’s journey up to that point, however, he realized that they would “not really be in the mood to be chuckling at clever quips that the lab dudes are telling.”

To me, though, the humor matches the odd and dissonant tone of some video games, and is thus part of the appeal. The way the film moves through scenes that feel akin to “levels” without feeling schematic was also impressive. Bryan’s first major challenge is at the site of a car crash, after which he trudges to an abandoned house nearby; then he moves through the streets and sewers of Raccoon City, before making it to the lab. Cregger replicates in a way that feels organic the Resident Evil games’ tendency to move players from place to place quickly.

It became obvious how much of a Resident Evil super-fan Cregger is as he referenced detailed aspects of the games that inspired him. (The fairly straightforward path Bryan’s journey takes was another Resident Evil 4 homage, for instance.) Indeed, the entire project came about when he was asked by the film’s producer Roy Lee, who had worked on the Minecraft movie, whether Cregger would ever be interested in adapting a video game. “The only one I could think of would be Resident Evil,” he said, because he felt that no film had quite yet captured the spirit of playing it. Nevertheless, he faced backlash online from fans for eschewing the franchise’s established characters and plot—a sense of skepticism from a community that often sees their favorite games clunkily translated to the big screen.

Cregger was unsurprised. “I was expecting, as D-Day was approaching, I’m gonna get shredded,” he said. “I was already getting shredded by the people who were supposed to like the movie. So, I was just like, well, this is just gonna be the humbling that I am due for.” Instead, the film has been a colossal success for the Resident Evil franchise, earning strong reviews and grossing more in its opening weekend—upwards of $60 million domestically—than any prior adaptation made in its entire U.S. box-office run.Theatergoers, who now comprise multiple generations of video-game fans, have responded to the film’s atmosphere; they’ve never encountered cinema that so closely resembles their side of the player equation. The connection to the source material does matter to Cregger, of course: “The one thing that I really hate is the accusation that I’ve been getting that I just want to make a movie and slap Resident Evil on it,” he said. “This was always supposed to be Resident Evil.” But adapting the franchise doesn’t have to mean engaging with the lore—it can be as simple as capturing a feeling.

Dario Amodei, Sam Altman, and Elon Musk don’t agree on much. Each started their AI company—Anthropic, OpenAI, and xAI, respectively—out of spite at competitors’ greed and recklessness; each is certain that he alone can be entrusted to build superintelligence safely. But in the midst of the AI panic that has seized the country over the past two weeks, the three CEOs found one point of consensus: the introduction of “embedded evaluators,” or outside experts, to sit inside AI companies and monitor their safety practices. Both Amodei and Altman made express commitments to integrating such evaluations inside their companies. Musk followed with a quote-post stating simply, “Dario is right.”

Yet the details of such a system for regulating AI remain vague. How would such a setup work? An unknown number of outside evaluators would be assigned desks to sit at, computers to use, and NDAs to sign. Then, they might seek out risks: Evaluators could spend their days testing whether unreleased models trained for drug discovery can be altered to design viruses too. They might audit organizational practices: Are employees cutting corners on safety in the rush toward the launch of a new model? The evaluators will then write up what they find, and hopefully those reports will see sunlight. Amodei has promised editorial independence, but with significant asterisks: carve-outs for “security-sensitive, legally privileged, commercially sensitive, or third-party confidential information.” Anthropic, OpenAI, and xAI did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

[Read: America’s Hypocritical Take on Intellectual Property]

As for these experts, it seems likely that they’ll come from small nonprofits, as well as large firms. That includes the tech-consulting giant Accenture, with which Anthropic plans to fund a billion-dollar partnership, and the nonprofit METR, which led the investigation of OpenAI agents’ hack of Hugging Face. After that hack, OpenAI granted METR exclusive access to agent transcripts, data sets, and interviews with OpenAI staff. The resulting report revealed concerning levels of cheating and deception among the agents.

On-site evaluation has precedent in other high-risk industries such as aviation and banking. The FAA delegates some safety certifications to engineering experts inside airplane manufacturers, whereas the Federal Reserve stations its own supervisors inside banks to enforce regulation. “OpenAI and Anthropic are like Citibank: They pose systemic risk to the globe, so you have examiners,” Brad Carson, a former U.S. representative who is now the president of the AI-governance nonprofit Americans for Responsible Innovation, told me.

Yet Amodei’s proposal is still far from a comprehensive AI-auditing regime. As long as safety evaluations remain voluntary, the independent organizations selected by the companies will get as much access as the companies allow to evaluate the specific risks that the companies choose. That doesn’t add up to true independent oversight, and at least some METR employees seem to agree. “None of the work that we’ve done so far passes my bar for an ‘audit’ of an AI company or its systems, and was definitely not ‘regulation’ in any meaningful sense (whether bank examiner–like or otherwise),” Charles Foster, a policy staffer at the organization, wrote on X, speaking in his personal capacity. Recall the Facebook Oversight Board, a legally independent entity created with the ambitious aim to adjudicate the platform’s thorniest speech questions—but which has largely proven powerless to make major decisions that cut against Meta’s interests.

Third-party evaluators must walk the line between holding their corporate hosts accountable and not saying anything that gets them booted from the premises. The same access to Slack channels and lunchroom chatter that helps an evaluator conduct whole-organization safety audits can lead to them succumbing to the same blind spots as their host. Here, the 2008 financial crisis offers hard lessons. A Senate investigation found that the New York Fed’s internal culture had been “excessively deferential,” where supervisors feared speaking up about the issues they were supposedly regulating. Moreover, credit-rating agencies were motivated to offer lenient terms to win business from banks. These conflicts of interest are exaggerated when evaluators—like Accenture—are funded directly by the companies. “It doesn’t really seem tolerable to let AI companies pick providers that they have hundreds of millions of dollars of business with to be the primary cops on the beat,” Nathan Calvin, the general counsel of the AI-advocacy group Encode, told me.

Then there’s the question of whether the evaluators and the companies they’re asked to monitor are too intertwined. METR, a 43-person organization based in Berkeley, has partnered with the leading AI labs to measure model misalignment since 2023. It does not accept payment or donations from AI companies or employees. Still, many METR staffers come from the same circles as the people they audit, and some have known one another socially and professionally for years. “The AI space is not that large,” Jacob Steinhardt, a Berkeley computer-science professor and the CEO of Transluce, a San Francisco–based AI-evaluation nonprofit, told me. “If you say no former lab employee can ever evaluate any AI model, you are ruling out a pretty broad and important set of expertise.” Those longtime relationships have helped small nonprofits gain access to corporate data, yet they now challenge the organizations’ perceived neutrality. “THESE are the people we’re trusting to beat China in AI? Give me a break,” posted House Majority Leader Steve Scalise, quoting a New York Post cover describing METR employees as “The Woke Wizards of A.I.”

Embedded evaluators are surely preferable to the status quo of no oversight at all. The public will learn more about AI incidents when there are watchdogs around—as it has from METR’s Hugging Face report, or Transluce’s recent report on OpenAI agents attempting to hack into a government website. Yet the evaluators will lack teeth and trust until backed by government authority: to license a wide-ranging group of expert organizations, to mandate frontier-AI developers to bring them in, and to set the minimum safety and transparency standards that they must meet. “You need serious oversight,” Gillian Hadfield, a professor of AI governance at Johns Hopkins, told me. “Are we making sure that the entities that are doing this work are qualified? Have we got ways of establishing and maintaining their independence? Do we have a threat that says, ‘If you don’t do this well, you’ll lose your license’?” The worst-case outcome is a race to the bottom: where auditors compete on leniency instead of quality, winning big contracts in return for hasty sign-offs.

Meanwhile, it seems that at least some sort of regulation is approaching. In the face of escalating public backlash, internal employee activism, and fears of near-term AI extinction events, both Anthropic and OpenAI have declared support for various forms of state and federal legislation around third-party audits. In a recent podcast, Ben Horowitz—whose venture firm has been a major voice against AI regulation—also seemed to embrace a private-auditor approach, saying that the government “is very good at setting the rules” around unintended model behaviors, whereas “a very competent private company” should evaluate compliance. The Overton window in Silicon Valley is shifting. But if the stakes really are existential, hiring a few extra inspectors falls painfully short—especially when the details of the arrangements are this slippery.

It all feels too little, and too late. Some politicians, facing an angry and frightened public, have turned to more aggressive asks, such as nuclear-style treaties with China and a ban on superintelligence itself. Two years ago, California Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed a state bill that included mandated third-party audits, among other safety requirements, after a fierce lobbying campaign by OpenAI and Andreessen Horowitz. (Anthropic was an exception, expressing conditional support.) Last week, Newsom—sensing the changing tides—issued an executive order to require onsite auditors and accelerate the development of an “AI kill switch.”

President Trump still has his foot on the gas pedal. “The only control or ‘guardrails’ that AI needs is a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT,” he posted last week. In June, the administration told CAISI, the federal government’s internal AI-evaluation unit, to stop publishing its reports. Trump’s accelerationism—informed by his rapport with ballroom sponsor and NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang—likely dooms federal AI-safety regulation for now.

It’s nice to see some AI-industry leaders agreeing that someone else should rein them in. And if implemented well, embedded evaluators are a smart technocratic move. But after years of developing the technology, lobbying against guardrails, and causing multiple accidents, AI leaders shouldn’t be surprised if their preferred—and valid—policy solution ends up being met with disappointment, and a heap of skepticism.
