A Very Bad 24 Hours for Crypto

Last night, hackers stole approximately $6.3 billion from Binance, the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange. The whole thing reportedly took about 20 minutes. It was the biggest theft in the history of the sector, and also probably the biggest financial heist of all time, topping the $1.5 billion ByBit hack of 2025 and Sadam Hussein’s $1 billion robbery of the Central Bank of Iraq in 2003. Binance says that about half of the stolen funds have been “frozen”—meaning that they’re effectively banned from being used in future transactions. North Korean hackers are thought to have been responsible.

In the hours before the attack, though, a smaller heist was roiling a different corner of digital finance: Yesterday afternoon, at least $2.6 million was reportedly drained from Polymarket accounts. This, too, was a crypto theft. Polymarket’s international platform, which until last month hosted the majority of the company’s trading volume, runs on blockchain technology. But the attacks had different origins, and there’s no evidence that the two are connected. Binance is saying that its hack may have involved a compromised laptop, as well as “additional points of breach”; thieves took money that was stored on the exchange. On Polymarket, hackers exploited a flaw in a third-party provider, allowing them to access users’ accounts. It was the firm’s second security lapse since June.

Both platforms say they plan to cover the losses in full. But in crypto, getting your money back isn’t a guarantee. Despite the technical differences and massive disparity in scale, yesterday’s hacks are a reminder that crypto-backed platforms of all kinds can still come with fundamental risks.

Unlike Kalshi, which has a single U.S. exchange for its domestic and international customers, Polymarket’s operations are split down the middle: Polymarket.com and Polymarket.us are two separate exchanges. When you make a bet on the U.S. site, your trade is settled in dollars. On the international site, everything is settled in crypto. The distinction matters. Both traditional financial firms and blockchain-backed systems face immense cybersecurity challenges—and the crypto industry has done a lot of growing up since 2022—but with crypto, customers typically have less recourse when things go wrong. Although deposits at U.S. banks are technically IOUs, they tend to be really good IOUs. If you ask for your money, you can be pretty confident that it will be there. If the bank fails, you’re guaranteed to get some of it back, thanks to the miracle of FDIC insurance.

Polymarket, like Binance, isn’t a bank, and its international platform isn’t overseen by the American regulatory system. (Both companies’ independent U.S. platforms, which are subject to onshore regulations, were entirely unaffected by these attacks.) Users may get their money back this time, as the firms have promised, but that’s not always the case—not all companies have the funds to repay their customers. They’re not necessarily obligated to, either.

Polymarket’s ties to crypto go back to its founding, in 2020, a moment when the industry was seen as a kind of Wild West. Early on, users could make bets on the site only if they were willing to ditch old-school money for digital tokens. Polymarket didn’t even have a domestic, non-crypto platform until late last year—and until very recently, it was seeing less volume than the international site. (The demographics for crypto and prediction markets still have some overlap too.)

This isn’t the end for Polymarket, and it’s certainly not the end for crypto, which has lately proved itself to be incredibly resilient. (Bitcoin is down a few thousand dollars since last night, but it hasn’t tanked nearly as much as you might expect after a disruption of this scale.) Still, the timing is unfortunate for the company. As I wrote on Wednesday, next week’s election coverage will put prediction markets in front of even more people. Users who have lost confidence in the company may end up switching over to Polymarket’s main competitor, Kalshi; users who aren’t familiar with prediction markets will have yet another reason to avoid them. Americans are already broadly skeptical of these firms’ push into politics. Polymarket’s link to crypto—and to the chaos of the past 24 hours—could further entrench that distrust.

Many a heavy-handed metaphor has been constructed on the claim that Washington, D.C., is built on a swamp. This hoary cliché is also, for the most part, untrue—but the neighborhood Foggy Bottom, home to the State Department, really is former marshland.

This is perhaps fitting. Stories of decadence, deviousness, and ineptitude within the current Trump administration are a dime a dozen, even at current inflation rates. The Justice Department acts as a private law firm for the president; the Department of Health and Human Services seems dedicated to undermining both; the Hegseth Pentagon is breaking globes, both physically and metaphorically. Yet the State Department has escaped some of the taint that has attached to other departments and agencies. This may be because the exploits elsewhere are so blatant, and because Secretary of State Marco Rubio has a reputation as one of the more serious figures in the administration.

Whatever the reasons, the State Department may deserve more opprobrium than it has received. One of the guiding philosophies of the second Trump presidency, imported from Project 2025, is that personnel is policy: The success of an administration depends on who staffs it. State shows how this can go very wrong.

Perhaps holding Kimberly Guilfoyle, the ousted ambassador to Greece who was allegedly selling access, against the department would be unfair. After all, she was a political choice, likely selected as a thank-you from the president whose campaign she assisted, and to whose son she was previously engaged.

But other appointments cannot be so easily explained away. One example is Darren Beattie, whose title is senior adviser for Western Hemisphere affairs. He has particular interest in policy toward Brazil, where the administration has worked to assist the right-wing movement associated with the Bolsonaro family; he is also president of the U.S. Institute of Peace, on which Trump has scrawled his own name. Beattie’s career shows the difference between the first and second Trump administrations: When his speech to white nationalists was revealed during the first, he was fired. In the second, he was welcomed back and given a plum role.

One of his colleagues now is Nate Hochman. Once seen as a rising star of mainstream conservatism, Hochman went from National Review to the 2024 presidential campaign of Ron DeSantis, from which he was fired for sharing a video featuring Nazi imagery. After a pit stop working for Senator Eric Schmitt of Missouri, who has regularly made ethnonationalist comments (yes, he’s also the guy with the Jack Smith gaffe), he landed at the State Department, where he is reportedly Rubio’s chief speechwriter. This might explain why the secretary’s recent speech in Athens was so obsessed with supposed threats to the West.

The overlapping politics does not appear to be coincidental. Jeremy Carl, who has warned that “white culture” is imperiled, missed out on a chance to join Beattie and Hochman only when his Senate hearing in February completely derailed over offensive old tweets and his inability to explain his own views. The department now has an “Office of Remigration,” using a white-nationalist shibboleth for ethnic cleansing.

Some of the State Department’s hires are questionable less for ideological than for personality reasons. Britt McHenry, a former ESPN personality who then deployed culture-war takes to become a mid-tier MAGA figure, joined the State Department last month as a press adviser, but her time there was short: She was resigned this past Friday after throwing a drink at a fan during a Washington Wizards game.

Another recent departure is Paolo Zampolli, an old Trump hanger-on most famous for introducing the president and first lady. Zampolli snagged a State Department appointment as special envoy for global partnerships, focused on cultural affairs, but then quickly began meddling in issues that were not in his remit. (Not that he was aware what his remit was: “I didn’t even read the notification” to Congress about his role. “I don’t know about the notification,” he told Politico, saying he took direction from Trump.) Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau’s attempts to control Zampolli produced what Politico described as “a monthslong fight” conducted via “emails that at times included more than a dozen administration officials.” Shortly after that scoop, Zampolli was fired.

Zampolli isn’t the only one inattentive to cultural diplomacy. Officials have been rampaging against a painting in the embassy in the Dominican Republic by the artist Kehinde Wiley, who is best known (perhaps not incidentally) for painting President Obama’s official portrait. Trump officials have referred to it as “very woke,” “aesthetically terrifying,” and a lesbian picnic. (The piece is much less titillating than this might suggest: It depicts four people posing over one of Wiley’s trademark floral patterns.)

Are any top officials at the State Department above this chaos? Yes, but maybe not for long. My colleague Vivian Salama reported earlier this year on the deserted halls of the department, and The Washington Sun last week reported that leaders are working to promote political loyalists but squeeze out senior foreign-service officers, the most experienced members of the nonpolitical career staff trained in foreign relations. “The State Department is trying to get people to quit,” one officer told the Sun. Sometime soon, we might get a chance to learn what happens when diplomacy is fully in the hands of the least diplomatic people imaginable.

When a Movie Hinges on a Kid’s Performance

The film Josephine opens with one of the most upsetting sequences in any movie this year. In it, the titular 8-year-old girl accidentally witnesses a woman being raped at a park near her home. In subsequent scenes, the filmmaker Beth de Araújo, who based the incident on an episode she experienced in childhood, carefully explores how the event affects Josephine and her family, and sheds light on larger questions: how parents can struggle to make sense of a child’s trauma, and how attempts to protect young people can yield more pain.

Josephine is anchored by Mason Reeves, the actor who plays Josephine, who was 8 at the time of filming. Her performance sensitively conveys her character’s mixture of confusion, frustration, fear, and curiosity. Reeves is now 10, and she’s been enthusiastic, de Araújo told me, about acting in another project soon. De Araújo would rather she didn’t. “I hope and I believe Mason had a great experience on this,” she told me in September, “and I hope she also puts it aside for a while.”

De Araújo is not the only director both working with children on films that have challenging themes and aiming to keep those young stars out of the spotlight. Cristian Mungiu, the writer-director of Fjord, has a similar wish for the adolescent actors who, in his film, give remarkably grounded performances as the children of a devout couple accused of child abuse. “I’m trying to encourage them to think twice before they consider acting as something they want to do for their lives,” Mungiu told me.

The filmmakers’ hesitation is understandable. Children who grow up on sets are vulnerable to being physically and emotionally exploited, as numerous documentaries and memoirs have made clear. And the emotional toll of subsequent fame can lead to troubled adulthoods, as the recent deaths of former child stars including Hayden Panettiere and RJ Mitte have helped demonstrate. But these concerns may be of particular importance when it comes to children appearing in movies with dark subject matter, such as Josephine and Fjord. Zuri Pryor-Graves, an intimacy coordinator and a former therapist for child actors, told me that young performers immersed in intense environments often fail to notice when they’re overworked. Tight production timelines and the scrutiny of an adult crew isolate them from normal childhood experiences, including coming of age as themselves, rather than as their characters. “They’re in development stages where they’re learning who they are,” Pryor-Graves said. “Being able to create that separation is not something that’s built into a production schedule.”

[Read: The uncomfortable truth about child abuse in Hollywood]

In an op-ed for The New York Times in August, the former child actor Alison Pill echoed that concern: “At a time when kids are supposed to be forging our own identities, child actors have ours chosen for us by agents, casting directors and the public.” Pill called to end such work altogether. “The greatest storytellers should find a way to capture the world we live in without child actors,” she wrote, “for all our sakes.”

Yet that idea seems impossible. Thoroughly adult stories that hinge on young performances are nothing new, and this year has seen a spate of such projects. Apart from de Araújo and Mungiu’s films, the drama Gentle Monster depicts a man who gets arrested for distributing child pornography; his 9-year-old son’s perspective becomes pivotal. This fall also brings the return of the Hunger Games franchise, and with it another round of youngsters fighting to the death, as well as the debut of Club Kid, a drama about a hard-partyer who discovers he has a 10-year-old son. And Primetime, a film dissecting the rise and fall of the reality-television series To Catch a Predator, features cameos from former child stars including Alex Winter and Jonathan Lipnicki. Their portrayals of some of the alleged pedophiles the TV show targeted flip the script, underlining how unprotected they themselves were as children.

The directors I spoke with about this topic all agreed that young performers need extra care and attention. But the trouble with working with children is that no single method exists to ensure their healthy adulthoods. The question facing Hollywood, then, is whether the industry can ever truly place their youngest actors’ needs first—perhaps to the point of scrapping a film no matter the budgets, schedules, and egos involved. Projects involving child actors should move forward, de Araújo said, only if directors are genuinely confident those actors are capable of handling the pressure of being on a set. “If you don’t find them, you don’t find them,” she said. “You have to go back to the drawing board and start from scratch.”

Let’s say you’re a child actor. In most cases, you’re accompanied by a supervisor on set who coordinates your schedule so you don’t exceed your legal work hours. You’re shuffled from hair and makeup to wardrobe, to rehearsal, to set. You spend time studying with tutors. You’re surrounded by adults, whose attention you find intimidating or perhaps special; either way, you don’t want to be the problem. “Kids in front of the camera have a higher propensity for creating a bit of a false self,” Fran Walfish, a child psychologist based in Los Angeles, told me, “because they want to please everybody.”

Marie Kreutzer, the writer-director of Gentle Monster, aimed to avoid this scenario. When she was casting for Johnny, the boy whose relationship with his father gets scrutinized, she made sure to “audition” the parents of her finalists. She talked with the caregivers at length about their reasons for allowing their kids to be on camera in a mature drama. Their level of enthusiasm for getting their children cast, she told me, could be telling. “When you have difficult conversations like that in the beginning, you immediately get a very good feeling about them and how they are as parents,” she explained. After the then-7-year-old Malo Blanchet was cast as Johnny, his parents asked whether he wanted them around while he filmed. Kreutzer said Blanchet declined because having them there seemed stressful.

On set, Kreutzer tried to keep Blanchet from becoming overly attached to crew members. “Being the center of attention of a whole film crew for a while can really do something to you as a person when you are young,” Kreutzer said. She pointed out that the separation anxiety that comes at the end of filming can be disorienting, no matter a person’s age. “We even get that wrong, you know?” she said. “When I’m shooting a film and I wrap, I always miss the people.”

[Read: How Daniel Radcliffe outran Harry Potter]

Every filmmaker I spoke with discussed the importance of creating distance between young actors’ normal life and the grown-up stories they help tell. Mungiu, of Fjord, calls his younger cast members “interpreters” rather than “actors,” and said he aims to give them simple, direct instructions about how to portray their characters. “We shouldn’t project our personalities as adults on children,” he said. Kreutzer said she wanted to explain the story of Gentle Monster to Blanchet in terms he would understand by offering him, as she put it, “a part of the truth.” She spoke with Blanchet’s parents about what he’d be able to handle; together, they agreed to describe Gentle Monster to him as a film about a mom and a dad having a hard time separating, a concept he grasped because he had friends whose parents had divorced.

Josephine, with its story rooted in a traumatic sexual-assault case, required similar discretion, especially because Reeves is in almost every scene. De Araújo told me she worked closely with Reeves’s parents to come up with a plan that would let Reeves avoid undergoing the same emotional turmoil as Josephine, while still understanding her character’s arc. She and Reeves mapped out Josephine’s emotions by revisiting Reeves’s own memories; if Reeves had to portray Josephine being angry, she’d recall the time her sister ruined a drawing she made, for example. De Araújo hoped that this would protect the 8-year-old from being too immersed in the particulars of her character’s story. In addition, Reeves saw only portions of the script, was not on set for the filming of the opening scene, and was told that the movie boils down to “Josephine sees something bad and it makes her scared, and then the film is about Josephine overcoming her fear and being stronger,” de Araújo said.

De Araújo also pushed for Reeves’s needs to come first. If she was hungry, the filming schedule would adjust so she didn’t have to wait until the scheduled lunch break to eat. If she was tired, they’d rethink what to shoot. “A child’s going to act like a child,” de Araújo explained. “Because of that, you need to be paying such close attention to where they are emotionally.”

Even so, no strategy can guarantee a safe future. Child-labor laws largely don’t engage with the emotional complications child stars can face. The Coogan Law, passed in 1939, serves as the basis for most legislation pertaining to young performers, including proposals recently introduced seeking to protect the rights of child influencers—the latest type of child star. But the Coogan Law, like most of its successors, focuses exclusively on compensation, meaning that there are few if any enforceable safeguards around a young performer’s mental well-being.

And there is no legal fix for the fact that once a film hits theaters, the children in it will be exposed to a level of scrutiny that can be overwhelming. Young performers can easily get pigeonholed into archetypes that then shape their budding personal identities. As Pill wrote in her essay, “It is difficult, if not impossible, to develop an independent sense of self when what feels like the entire world pillories you for deviating from your type and heaps praise on you when you conform to it.”

To de Araújo, that’s a dilemma that’s remained largely unresolved. “I don’t think child acting is necessarily the problem,” she said. “I think it’s then the press and the fame that they get around it.” There is no way to control how an audience responds to children on-screen—to prevent fandoms from going overboard, or to limit the public conversations around child actors’ work. No laws dictate how their caregivers raise them. There are only guidelines for productions and parents, which can in many ways amount to guesswork about how a child will turn out.

For now, Reeves seems thoroughly content with all things Josephine. She’s been traveling the world—Berlin! Toronto! Los Angeles!—on the film festival tour with de Araújo. On her Instagram page, she posted an essay she put together for a fourth-grade assignment, titled “Acting Is the Best Job!!!!!!!!!!” (That’s 10 exclamation points.) “Acting is the best job,” she writes, “because you can learn different skills and do new things, you can meet new people and last but not least Acting is FUN!!!” It’s easy to understand why she wants to get back on set. Rooting for that to happen, though, is harder.

AI hacking swarms are poking holes across the internet at such a rate that some cybersecurity professionals have dubbed this month “Black October.” The AI-cybersecurity professional Alex Stamos argues that it’s time to be proactive rather than reactive, and that might mean rewriting the code for the internet in newer programming languages that could make all of our online systems and personal information safer. He joins the Galaxy Brain host Charlie Warzel to explain why that’s not as big of a deal as it sounds, and how we can all protect ourselves in the meantime.

The following is a transcript of the episode:

Alex Stamos: What we have to do, as people who work in cybersecurity, is: We have to shorten the Dark Age. We need to make sure the Library of Alexandria doesn’t burn, right? We have to do everything possible to make this as short as possible. And from my perspective, the best way to do that is to be proactive, not reactive, here. And to be proactive we can’t just wait for these bad things to happen; we have to intentionally re-architect things.

Charlie Warzel: It’s late October. There’s an upcoming election. There’s a World Series. There’s this endless string of bracing news events. But there’s another crisis looming as well. The digital infrastructure that powers our entire on- and offline lives is, according to the people who protect it, more vulnerable than ever. The internet as we know it is a very fragile ecosystem. So much of it is built off of code that’s written by humans—that is, by nature, full of flaws or bugs. Some of our critical digital infrastructure, it’s built off of decades-old technology that’s running on ancient code languages.

The internet is a marvel. But it is also, like any massive human endeavor, imperfect and full of vulnerabilities. One way to look at the history of cybersecurity is as a race between people who are looking to exploit all those vulnerabilities and the people who can fortify our systems and keep them secure.

But in recent months, AI has threatened to change the dynamics of that race. AI agents—which are cheap, relentless, and highly capable hacker armies—are proving excellent at finding vulnerabilities in code. Sometimes, oftentimes, in places that humans had never thought to look. And the result has been this series of high-profile cybersecurity incidents, each one adding to this feeling that we’ve entered a new era.

The rate of disclosures of incidents has been so intense that in recent weeks, cybersecurity professionals are referring to this month as Black October. Last week, the public found out that AI agents found vulnerability in PHP, which is a coding language that affects 70 percent of the internet, that could allow hackers to gain access to almost any server. On top of PHP, there have been disclosures in major software products like Node.js, which is software that’s used across countless important webpages and services on the internet. This is stuff that powers everything from supply chains to health-care companies.

And the fear is that what we are seeing here is not some kind of aberration, but the beginning of this new normal—this period of time where everything from our power grids to our personal data may be uniquely vulnerable.

And so I wanted to understand exactly how unprecedented this Black October moment really is, and what it means for the state of the internet and the possibilities of catastrophic events in the future. Alex Stamos is the chief information security officer at the AI company Cognition, and the former chief security officer at Facebook. He is not only on the forefront of this current crisis, but he’s also somebody who understands how massive digital organizations respond to the demands of an increasingly insecure internet.

I’m Charlie Warzel, and this is Galaxy Brain.

[Music]

Warzel: Alex Stamos, welcome to Galaxy Brain.

Stamos: Hey, thanks for having me, Charlie.

Warzel: So we’re in the middle of what feels right now like this explosion of either AI-powered hacks or cybersecurity disclosures. Right now, people in your industry, and colloquially on places like X are referring to it as Black October. Pretty ominous. Can you walk me through what has changed fundamentally in the last two weeks?

Stamos: Yeah. So we’re just kind of—this has honestly been a slow burn. But what’s been really interesting in this month is that finally attackers are getting the benefit from AI; where they have the ability to use AI to automate what we call kill chains. So you know, this is a term we have stolen from the military—where, when the military says “kill chain,” they mean to actually kill something. In the cyber world, those are the steps you take to break into a network and to do something.

And traditionally, you could automate some parts of a kill chain. But there’s often human steps. And just in the last several months, we’ve gotten to the point of where AI models—and especially the open-weight models, the models that you can run locally on hardware you run yourself, and even commodity hardware like these new Mac Studios that you can buy—are good enough that basically every step of the kill chain can be completely automated with AI. And so that has really changed the game here—where you can get rid of any human bottleneck. And that has completely changed the economics and the speed at which attackers can move.

Warzel: So I’ve heard of this being talked about as a “disclosure wave,” meaning that it’s a disclosure of potential vulnerabilities and not a wave of attacks. But what’s the truth to that?

Stamos: Yeah, I don’t think that’s true. We’ve actually had a wave of attacks, but it’s way before this October. We’ve had a building wave of attacks over the last year or so as attackers have automated their kill chains. There’s a good thing happening, which is—these vulnerabilities have existed for decades, right? We have, you know, years and years and years of human-written code that is not reliable, that is not safe. That is often written in languages that are not what we call memory safe and type safe, that are written in computer languages that were not fundamentally easy to write safe code in. Languages like C and C++.

And unfortunately, it turns out that human beings writing code in those languages—it’s very hard to make systems that are safe in the presence of these superhuman bug-finders. And so what’s happening is we are now burning through all of these bugs that were written over two decades, and they are being found and discovered and patched. But the problem is, once those patches are released, actually getting them fixed in the wild takes a while. And so bad guys have a window during which they can then exploit them. And over the last year, we have seen that exploitation happening. Sometimes manually, and more and more automatically.

And so yes—we have seen waves and waves of exploitation. It’s not being covered as much, but it is extremely hard if you have a security emergency on a Friday evening. And it’s often on a Friday evening; unfortunately, that’s how it often seems to work out. It is very hard to get professional incident responders on the phone, because everybody is booked up pretty far out right now.

Warzel: So all of this news, as an outsider, is really bracing to just see come across the timeline. I don’t think it’s very clear for a lot of us without the expertise to evaluate some of these claims.

The one that I saw the other day that I wanna bring up here is the reports that the cloud service from Japan’s GMO Internet Group, which is used also by financial traders, had been hacked. And in one report that I saw circulating, the CEO of GMO suggested that the internet as we know it could be on the verge of collapse due to this pressure.

When we hear something like that, how are we supposed to evaluate that kind of claim? What is the state of the internet right now?

Stamos: Oh, so to say the internet is going to collapse, I think, is hyperbole. The fundamental infrastructure of the internet is holding up fine, right? Like the internet—the routers, the switches—there have been bugs in the fundamental systems that move traffic, but those have been fixed.

I think what is happening, however, is we have lived through a period where you have been able to assume that if you use a service, that your data is going to be safe. That assumption was never correct, it turns out. Right? The systems were never safe. It’s just that we lacked the number of skilled attackers to take advantage of the fundamental insecurity of these systems.

And what is happening now is the bugs are being found. Sometimes by the bad guys, often by the good guys. But in the period in which it takes for the bugs to be fixed, the bad guys have an opportunity to take advantage of that period of time, of that lag of fixing them. And then the bad guys—the skill sets. You know, I don’t know if you’re an English soccer/football fan, but everybody gets this metaphor now ’cause they watch Ted Lasso. But you know, right, so the English football leagues—if you do really well, you get sent up, right? If you do poorly, you get relegated; you get sent down.

Well, what’s happening is: Every attacker is now getting sent up a league, right? And so you know, it used to be the Premier League was just a handful of really big countries—so the United States and our allies—called the Five Eyes. So the United States, the U.K., Canada, Australia, New Zealand, is called the Five Eyes. Russia, China, Israel, a couple other Western nations were up there at the top. And then you had the next tier—which was like Iran, North Korea, a couple other second-tier countries. And then you had the really good independent professional commercial groups.

And what’s happening is everybody’s going up a league, because of AI. Which in some ways is bad for the people at the top, because they’re losing their premier status. That everybody else is kind of joining them. The skill sets that used to be really unique are becoming much less unique because of AI. And so I think that’s what we’re seeing now. That skills like taking a patch from Microsoft and reverse-engineering it and turning it into exploit code? Maybe 150, 200 people used to be able to do that five years ago. Now, a 19-year-old in St. Petersburg can do that with a M5 Mac Ultra. That’s kind of—and that’s really crazy.

Warzel: There’s a striking anecdote that I’ve seen floating around: that the PHP vulnerability that, you know, affected 70 percent of websites on the internet was independently discovered by someone using a frontier model, using the prompt “Find bad vulnerabilities in PHP.” And I think that sort of speaks to that idea, right? That everyone is elevated; everyone has these skills they didn’t have. But if people without even real basic knowledge of these systems or skills can do this, how is this sustainable for, you know, cybersecurity?

Stamos: Yeah. So what’s going to have to happen—like you said, what’s happening is, you have these bugs that will be found by somebody with access to a preview version of a cyber model. We do this thing called “coordinated disclosure,” where sometimes if you have a really big bug like that, it used to be you could wait 90 days. But in those 90 days, the models might get better, that everybody has access to, that you can’t wait, right? Because other people can just find the bug.

So the way we’re gonna get through this is—I think what we’re gonna have to do is we’re gonna have to rewrite a bunch of code. So I actually gave a talk earlier this year where I posited two scenarios, which is the good and the bad scenario. And the good scenario was that there was a finite number of bugs, and we were just burning them down. And we would asymptotically approach. As new models came out, you’d have fewer and fewer. And the bad scenario was every time we have a new model, you get the equal number of bugs.

Unfortunately, we’re closer to the bad scenario still. Which is: Every time one of these new models comes out, we still have a huge number of incredibly complex memory-management flaws. And so I think for these really complex code bases, we’re going to have to refactor them. We’re going to have to rebuild them in languages like Rust, where you have the ability to, one, there’s entire classes of bugs they don’t have. And then you also have the ability to effectively have kind of contracts where you say “I am designing it to have these properties,” and you can mathematically prove that it has those properties.

There’s a thing called formal verification. And you don’t have to do true formal verification, which is really hard, and it would’ve been basically impossible to do this with humans. Or incredibly expensive. But with LLMs, you can. And so I think we’re just gonna have to basically completely rewrite stuff. And that is actually going to be cheaper with AI than it’s going to be to do 1,000 bugs every six months. We should just redo these things.

Warzel: But let’s talk about that. Like how long does something like that take, a rewriting the code of the internet?

Stamos: Well, less time than you might think. I think that’s actually one of the things that’s changed with AI. So I work at this company called Cognition now, and we have a product called Devin. And we work with companies, and we have these examples; like, we worked with Mercedes-Benz, and we rewrote one of these apps of theirs that would’ve taken months and months for humans to do. And we did it in like eight days. So I think actually this is one of those beliefs that software engineers have—that it’s impossible to do this. And it’s actually quite plausible.

Warzel: I’ve also heard you say, and maybe these are the same thing, but I’ve heard you say that we’re entering a dark age of cybersecurity. Is that what you mean by that? And that sounds really kinda ominous.

Stamos: Yeah. I mean, we are. I’ve gotten a little crap for this, and maybe that’s deserved. But like, the Dark Ages—we came out of them, right? So first off, the Dark Ages were only for part of the Earth. Like, the Dark Ages happened in Europe, and things were, like, fine in China. You know, things were great. They had a flourishing civilization. But in Europe, we had Greek and Roman civilization, and then things got kind of rough for a while. And we forgot about a lot of things. But then we had the Enlightenment, and we had the Renaissance. And so my point is that what we have to do, as people who work in cybersecurity, is: We have to shorten the Dark Age. We need to make sure the Library of Alexandria doesn’t burn, right? We have to do everything possible to make this as short as possible.

And from my perspective, the best way to do that is to be proactive, not reactive, here. And to be proactive is: We can’t just wait for these bad things to happen. We have to intentionally re-architect things. We have to intentionally rewrite things. We have to intentionally change how we do cybersecurity. We have to intentionally change how we build the networks. We have to intentionally change how we build security teams.

Right now, we’ve been very reactive. We still do patching like we did five years ago. Most security teams are still built like they were built five years ago. Like: The jobs are the same, the defensive structures are the same, how we do incident response is the same. And I think that’s a problem. And when I say “shorten the Dark Age,” what I mean is, we’ve kind of gotta actually utilize AI to proactively—we’ve gotta to make this better. Otherwise, we’re just going to try to patch-patch-patch our way through it. And that’s not gonna work.

Warzel: In the scenario you’re describing—rewriting things with today’s better code—do we just end up, if we do that, in the same situation later? When more powerful AI models find these new vulnerabilities? Or that the code, you know, allows for these, like, superhuman-style bugs that we are incapable of kind of comprehending? Is that a possible scenario?

Stamos: I don’t think so. I mean, it’s hard. Now we’re getting to, like, metaphysics, when you talk about like these superhuman capabilities. But I don’t think so. I think if we start rebuilding things using languages that eliminate these entire classes, and then you start using some level of formalism, then it’s much less likely that’s true. Certainly what we’re doing right now is not working, right? I just think it’s total malpractice to write anything in C and C++. Any company that is shipping code that is written in C++ in 2027 is, from my perspective—that is just completely inappropriate.

Warzel: And for the people listening who aren’t as familiar, the reason for that is just ’cause it’s an old, outdated language. And you just don’t wanna have your systems be running off that. It’s easier to exploit all that, right?

Stamos: Right. Because the language C++—C and C++ are languages from the 1980s. The ’70s actually, and 1980s. And in fact, in the original book for the C programming language—which I was taught at Berkeley out of this book, in the original printing—there was a security flaw in the book that was written by the original authors of the language, right? Kind of famously. You can make mistakes, because these languages are what’s called low level. And you have to intentionally decide how you talk to the processor, and how you handle memory.

And so a lot of the kind of fundamental flaws that we see that get exploited these days is about deciding how you manipulate memory, the memory of the computer. You say, “I want to allocate memory. I wanna deallocate memory.” And the bugs that we see, like the PHP bug, these are memory-allocation errors.

In modern languages, these things are handled for you automatically. And in really modern languages, like Rust, you have the ability to say not just it handles the memory for you, but you can effectively say to the compiler—the software that takes what you say and then turns it into the language the computer actually executes. You can say, “This is what I intend. This is what I want to happen.” And so there’s double-checks, triple-checks to make sure bad things don’t happen.

Now, that would’ve been totally improbable in the 1980s, ’cause computers weren’t fast enough. It would’ve made the computers way too slow. But computers are incredibly fast now, and so these languages are much slower. But who cares? Because computers are spectacularly fast. We have way, way more speed, and so we sacrifice a little bit of speed for a lot more safety. And it would’ve been completely improbable to rewrite Windows, to rewrite Linux, to rewrite the Cisco kernel code—to rewrite all of the stuff that powers all of the electricity, the light that you and I, that’s shining on us. All of the little processors that power the hydroelectric plant that’s—you know, ’cause you’re in the Pacific Northwest, you’re probably getting hydropower from Washington and Oregon and British Columbia. I’m getting wind power and solar power in California.

Those systems are from PLCs [programmable logic controllers]; from Siemens and Schneider Electric and companies like that. That’s all written in C code. That stuff could be rewritten in Rust now. It just means you have to pay to replace that stuff. It will cost money, but that is worth it for our society to continue to operate over the next decade. And I think that’s worth it, but we have to proactively decide as a society to do that.

Warzel: So let’s talk about that. You obviously mentioned, like, power-grid systems. Some of this very foundational infrastructure, running off of some of this old stuff. We need to do something about it. You also said early on that people are overwhelmed, right? That there’s so much happening. Do we have the resources to do this in an orderly fashion? To respond to the level of the threat right now? Because I think what I am seeing is these concerns from people—perhaps they’re just engagement farming on places like Twitter, but they’re talking about vulnerabilities to the power grids. They’re talking about vulnerabilities to these very critical pieces of infrastructure.

Stamos: I mean, what do you mean by the resources? Certainly, so, one—Twitter’s not real life. I think that has to be one of the most important things that we have to remember, right? Like, the capability absolutely exists, because modern AI systems have the ability to do this. What we haven’t done is created the incentive structure. First, for the people who run the power grid to put the pressure on their vendors, and for the vendors who build these things to build systems this way. And the economic structures for us to actually upgrade all this stuff. We haven’t created those economic incentives yet.

Warzel: Isn’t the incentive structure for the power grids that you don’t want someone to find a vulnerability—you know, the 19-year-old in St. Petersburg—and take down the power grid? Like, aren’t we there? It feels to me like we’re there.

Stamos: Yeah. So, okay. I mean, I do a lot of work with some infrastructure providers. And of all the infrastructure providers, the power companies are actually the best. It’s—one, they’re quite large. Like, we’ve seen all these attacks against water utilities. And they’re, for example, in much worse shape, because they’re much smaller organizations. They’re often municipalities. A lot less time and money has been spent on water, for example, or sewer systems.

So you’re right—power is the best shape, and they do have the economic incentive. I think there’s still a bunch of coordinated action issues, in that there has not been enough work by them to put pressure on the vendor ecosystem to do all the right things. So yes; the power-grid people have done good work here. But most of the work they have done is in isolation, right?

So effectively, how they have done security is: They have basically decided that the stuff they buy is crap, and that they will make sure that attackers have no ability to reach it. That is a reasonable protective strategy for now, because that is all they can do. But in the long run, we need to rebuild these systems, and we need to make them safer. Because against the right adversaries, you can’t actually physically isolate a power grid. Because against adversaries who are advanced enough, they will physically infiltrate and send somebody up the power pole and then tap into that physically isolated network.

And I think people spend too much time on the power grid, ’cause it is the thing that worries everybody. There’s a lot of other things that, if they go down—like hospitals. Like, hospitals get slaughtered all the time by ransomware actors, right? And those things are really critical, and they’re also very hard things to disconnect, because we all have electronic medical records, right? And especially community hospitals. Things run by counties are extremely poorly resourced compared to things like the power grid, from a security perspective.

Warzel: What are some of the things that you have seen in recent months—either the disclosures or the actual attacks—that have you the most concerned right now?

Stamos: Yeah. So, I mean, I think a couple of things that were really interesting and indicative of where we’re going. First, there were these attacks against South Korean banks.

Warzel: Mhm.

Stamos: Using fully automated AI-attack systems called Artex. So they’re these AI-attack systems that use open-weight models to do all of the attacks. There’s a thing called Voyager that was released earlier this year, or late last year, that would do AI-enabled attacks, but those were mostly used for kinda low-level stuff. So if this stuff is now at the point where you can do a full attack against a bank in a technologically advanced country, then we’re really kind of there.

And so we’ve now seen a variety of attacks using Artex since then. And since this stuff is open source and the models are open weight, anybody can take these things off the shelf. So I think that was really interesting. And then the other one is the attacks against Fireworks and Railway. These look like they were pulled off by Chinese actors who are interested in the American AI ecosystem. So China has what’s called the cottage-access industry. So effectively there’s an ecosystem of private contractors who will break into Western companies on behalf of the greater Chinese economic ecosystem, with permission of the Chinese government, and somewhat de-duplicated and given cover by the Chinese government for Chinese economic purposes.

And the fact that these folks are not going after inference, they’re not going after customer data, they’re going after API keys is really interesting. Because that looks like they’re specifically going after model weights, as well as the data used to train AI.

Warzel: Break those last things down for me, just because there’s some terms I think the audience may not be totally familiar with.

Stamos: Yeah. So Fireworks is a prominent company that provides training services to companies that want to build their own AI models. And so they were attacked, and the side of Fireworks that was attacked was the side that does training. And so it looks like who attacked them was groups who were helping out Chinese companies. And they were trying to steal information that would then help Chinese AI companies improve their AI models. So there’s now an entire ecosystem of hackers who are working on behalf of Chinese AI companies trying to improve and take data that will help them accelerate their work.

Warzel: I want to talk a little bit about the pace of innovation here. Because it seems like the moment we’re heading toward is—the good guys are deploying helpful, protective AI. We’re rewriting the code using the AI to protect against the people who are, you know, using AI agents and systems to attack. How worried does that make you? Given the advancements of these agents, the fact that we’re seeing them, you know, behaving either unexpectedly or communicating in these advanced ways?

Again, it feels like this is all moving really fast, from the innovation standpoint, of understanding what these things can do. How much control, you know, humans have in these systems. And yet they’re also, you know, going to be deployed in this fight on both sides. Like, how much are you worried about the speed with which this is all moving?

Stamos: This is the crazy challenge, because we want to have really capable models to help us do cyber defense. In creating these highly capable models, we wanna do so in a way that doesn’t cause damage. You know, OpenAI released, I think, a very good blog post in the work they’re doing to create secure evaluation frameworks. So that’s where I think you’re referring to. They found ways to sneak information between their different isolation mechanisms. They did timing attacks, they’re called. And stuff like that.

At Cognition, we do model training, so it was very helpful for them to document this. And we’re working with them and Anthropic to try to align how we all do this work. I think there’s honestly been—it’s great to talk about Hugging Face and stuff. Models escaping from frontier labs is not the long-term risk, to be frank. Warzel: Sure. It’s a human problem, yeah.

Stamos: Yeah; I think there are reasons. You know, I think the labs are coordinating on like reasonable limits of what we do in training. Of making sure that these things don’t encrypt their chain of thought; making sure that there are reasonable limits put on them. I am much more worried about the capabilities that are released.

And then, to that extent, I think what we need to really align on is how—and I think this is actually quite difficult. We have to provide cyber capabilities for defense. How do you do that without allowing those cyber capabilities to be used for offense in a negative way? And that’s spectacularly hard. And the other problem here is that governments, especially the United States government, have not defined what kind of liability companies have when they do this. Or the things that, you know—the speed with which this is all happening is very concerning. It makes the challenge harder and harder.

Stamos: Look, I work on cyber defense. And one, these bugs already exist; these vulnerabilities exist. And so I don’t think that there is a good way to just kind of pause the world at this point, because the capabilities that exist at this point are enough to find a huge number of flaws. So a tiny portion of the code that’s ever been written has now been scanned by the high-end cyber models, and bugs found and/or exploited. A tiny portion, right? Part of the problem here is the coordination problem. If I could pause everybody, to try to figure out a safety regime here, I would. But I don’t have that power. And so personally, what I would like to do is, I would like to focus on finding these bugs and fixing them. I would like to focus on building better defensive capabilities. And then especially on the cost issue, because the cost of the high-end defensive capabilities is way too high. You can’t afford to run Mythos against all of this code and fix it.

So we have to focus on lowering the cost and fix—of both finding the bugs and especially fixing them. And so that’s something we’re really focused on at Cognition—is like the Pareto frontier between cyber capability and cost. So you’re right that is the issue. I agree. I don’t have power—sorry, Charlie—to fix that.

And from my perspective, what I’m personally working on is just trying to then deal with the reality of—the frontier is moving incredibly quickly. And how do we deal with that fact? And what we do is, we try to fix those things as fast as possible and then build the defensive models as quickly as possible.

Warzel: Are we maybe also seeing some resiliency of the systems? Stamos: We got very lucky. The PHP bug turns out to be quite hard to exploit. Stamos: Yeah. So honestly, it’s the same stuff you should have been doing five years ago. But now it’s just more important, right?

So the first thing is, the No. 1 way any normal person gets hacked is by reusing the same password everywhere. I know you would never do this, Charlie, or anybody who’s watching this podcast, but a lot of people use the same password in lots of places. You don’t do that, because what happens is some bad website gets hacked, and then the password gets stolen, and then that happens to be password you use for your Gmail or your bank. And especially, if your email gets taken over, your life’s over, because all of your passwords get taken. So use a password manager, something like 1Password, and go reset all of your passwords. A password on a Post-it note in your wallet or your purse can’t be stolen from thousands of miles away. So change all your passwords; have them all be different. Use passkeys if possible, right? So having passkeys in the password manager, or on your phone, is really key.

The second is just—people are just gonna have to be more paranoid. Both because of all the theft of personal information that’s gonna happen, as well as the rise of AI voice, AI, the creation of video. We’re gonna see a real rise in scams that are very realistic. So people are getting phone calls that sound like their grandchild has been kidnapped. People are getting more spam text messages. If it seems too good to be true, if it seems like something is fake, it probably is. And so people just need to be more paranoid. Go help your parents and your grandparents be more paranoid.

If you’re just in Chrome all day, or Safari, then you can get a Chromebook, or you can get an iPad, and you can live in that. You can have the surferboy1985@gmail, and use that for all the things—for, you know, your betting site and the other things that you don’t care about. It’s not a bad idea to have kind of a secondary identity, so that if it’s compromised, it doesn’t lead back to your bank. It doesn’t lead back to the things you care about.

Weak scholarship was once easy to spot at a glance: The signs included bad writing, a lack of relevant citations, and little engagement with previous scholarship, such as a paper that promised a “novel” conceptual framework without acknowledging the many other papers making similar claims. As an associate editor of a bioethics journal, I’d make a quick “desk reject” decision and not send these articles for peer review, which effectively killed any hope of publication. Now I can say: AI is affecting every part of academic publishing. Readers have started to notice that AI-assisted articles are slipping into journals. But AI is creating problems behind the scenes as well.

Weak articles now arrive on my desk with fluent prose, plausible-sounding arguments, neatly organized sections, and citations that pass initial AI checks. Only with a deeper look does the AI use become apparent. There are sentences that have all of the trappings of real arguments, but that rarely make sense—like, say, “Algorithmic triage functions less as a clinical tool than as an infrastructure of deferred accountability.” There are citations that refer only to open-access or highly cited articles, because the most popular LLMs can’t readily access paywalled journal articles, so they rely on what’s either free or described at length elsewhere.

Unfortunately, the scholars submitting papers aren’t the only ones using AI. So are many of their peer reviewers. One recent peer review that my journal received—from a senior scholar—read like an unedited cut-and-paste job from an LLM. The giveaway was that it criticized the authors for failing to cite a particular text—in this case, the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders—when the paper did indeed cite it.

[Read: The rise of parasite authors]

Some of my own papers have received similarly suspicious peer reviews. A recent one of a paper that I had co-authored came in the telltale header-bullet style of ChatGPT, requesting 22 major and 14 minor edits on matters spanning ethics, statistics, experimental design, and machine learning. A review by a human typically comes from somewhere; I can usually tell, without knowing the name, whether a reviewer is a philosopher or a social scientist. But this review came from both everywhere and nowhere. The prose sparkled (human reviewers don’t take the time to polish text that no one but the authors and editors will see); the review quibbled with an inessential word and offered three synonyms to use instead (reviewers will object to a word or phrase, but they don’t usually supply a thesaurus-like menu of alternatives); and it questioned the year a reference was published (reviewers regularly check sources, but not the accuracy of small bibliographic details).

But as every academic knows, peer review has always been a highly imperfect process. One might imagine experts with deep knowledge of a paper’s topic or methods carefully reviewing each manuscript prior to publication. The reality is that those with relevant expertise are overwhelmed with review requests and almost never have the time to write one, particularly if the request comes from a lesser-known journal. (I learned this in my first years as a journal editor, when my review requests to top scholars were mostly ignored or declined.)

Although many reviews are thoughtful, others come from people with tangential expertise. (Entire corners of the internet are dedicated to memes from “Reviewer 2,” a caricature of a pedantic scholar who has clearly misunderstood the paper.) When retractions or fabrications make the news, such as the 2024 Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology article that included an AI-generated image of a rat with oversize genitals, the public often reacts with outrage at the failure of the peer-review process. But as a journal editor, I’m surprised it doesn’t happen more often.

AI has pushed this already flawed system of peer review to its breaking point. Journals are being inundated with manuscript submissions. Some journals have reported their submissions doubling or even tripling since ChatGPT was released, a few years ago. Across the Elsevier portfolio of journals, submissions were about twice as high in 2025 than in 2024. One journal reported having to double its editors—all unpaid volunteers—to handle the boom in submissions.

Reviewing a growing number of submissions with a critical eye now takes time that minimally compensated or unpaid journal editors, including me, do not have. As a result, even as journal editors desk-reject more manuscripts, they are also sending more and more AI-assisted papers out for review, which in turn off-loads the work of picking apart AI-assisted articles to two or three other overworked scholars. These scholars are, in turn, receiving more and more review requests. In some cases, the cycle has become absurd: AI-assisted papers land with overworked editors, who send them to rushed reviewers, who in some cases use AI to write the review, which the authors revise and resubmit using AI.

A recent study of 7.3 million journal articles published from 2020 to 2025 found that by 2025, more than half showed evidence of LLM assistance, up from 12 percent in 2023. These problems are most likely to afflict the humanities and social sciences, because conceptual argumentation is easier to mimic than experimental results. But AI can generate much of an article even in the hard sciences.

[Steven Strogatz: A mathematician reads the AI future]

The journal article has long been the currency of academic social capital. Most early-career academics can tell you offhand how many they have published, like a salesperson rattling off their most recent quarter’s numbers. This currency has remained valuable because, until recently, a good article was difficult to produce. Such scholarship required time, sometimes funding, and the expertise to design a study or work through an argument. Currencies collapse when people can no longer trust them. Even if a handful of top-tier journals can preserve the value of their articles, they cannot survive in an academic economy in which tens of thousands of other outlets cannot distinguish serious scholarship from work that just looks like it.

Each week, I meet with my trainees to read and critique a journal article in our field. I recently assigned an article with a promising title that had been published in an established journal weeks earlier, which had looked fine after a skimmed read. But as we dissected it, we began to see all of the hallmarks of AI: the sheen without the substance, the citations that were slightly off, the odd absence of conceptual coherence. I’d been on a conference panel with one of the authors earlier that year. Ironically, the topic was the challenges of AI in academic publishing.

I can usually find a teachable moment in any situation. But if I can no longer tell my trainees—postdocs and graduate students—that rigorous scholarship has a value that an academic journal can recognize, then I’m not sure what kind of future, exactly, I am preparing them for.
