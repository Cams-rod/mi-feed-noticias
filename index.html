Prediction Markets Are Calling It Early

This is an edition of Over/Under, a newsletter on the latest in prediction markets, gambling, and the evolution of everyday risk. Sign up here.

Democrats aren’t the only ones celebrating this week. Prediction markets—high-speed betting arenas where users can wager on the future of the republic—have been claiming a win of their own.

The platforms foresaw a Democratic sweep, and that’s what they got. “BREAKING: Polymarket was right,” one post on the company’s X account read yesterday morning. Both Polymarket and Kalshi have said that across the entire slate of elections that were called by Wednesday morning, about 98 percent of the markets’ favored candidates won. In many cases, prediction markets were able to identify the winners early and decisively. In Texas, Kalshi and Polymarket gave James Talarico about a 75 percent chance of victory in the days leading up to the election; he won. In Kansas, Adam Hamilton’s chances were hovering around 64 percent; he won too. Even before the major networks began to make their official calls Tuesday night, and before The New York Times’ needle made its biggest swings, some of the probabilities on these platforms had approached 99 percent, indicating near-complete certainty about who the winners would be.

Part of the original idea behind prediction markets was that they’d be able to draw on the wisdom of the crowd—millions and millions of individual hunches—to produce more accurate forecasts than any single source. The question after Tuesday’s elections is whether they can consistently align on outcomes that the polls and forecasts don’t see coming. Are prediction markets oracles or aggregators? So far, the evidence can’t justify the kind of grand victory they’re claiming.

Not all of the markets’ favorites won their election; Democrats were often underestimated, just as they were in the polls. Many bettors failed to predict Darline Graham’s loss in South Carolina and Greg Abbott’s loss in Texas. And in some cases, the markets were jittery: Probabilities wavered up and down at the last minute in Texas, Arizona, and New York for unknown reasons.

The markets proved themselves most useful this week when they could fill an information gap. The markets can be a rough index of public sentiment on Election Night, when the exit polls are out but the race still hasn’t been called—or in the days after when votes are still being counted, as in Alaska and Mississippi. As Bloomberg’s Matt Levine put it yesterday: “It would be nice if someone else would piece everything together, weight it appropriately by how much it matters, and generate one simple answer to the question ‘who won?’” This is essentially what prediction markets have tried to do. But, Levine notes, the markets are largely piecing together information that already exists.

Bettors can theoretically get their data from anywhere; they might make wagers based on polling, late-night cable-news broadcasts, their own surveys, or other reputable forecasts by experts such as Nate Silver (a paid adviser to Polymarket) and Lakshya Jain of Split Ticket. And at least on Polymarket, a small share of traders are handling the majority of the money—1 percent of accounts control 75 percent of the total volume, per the Anti-Corruption Data Collective, a nonprofit. That’s part of why the demographic makeup amd political biases of the people actually using these websites doesn’t skew the probabilities all that much. The especially active traders tend to bet according to who they think will win, rather than who they personally want to win.

In many cases, the markets favored eventual winners about as much as the forecasting models did. Take New Hampshire’s Senate race: The day before the elections, Nate Silver’s FLIPR Deluxe and Split Ticket both gave the Democratic candidate, who eventually won, a 95 percent chance. At the same time, Kalshi and Polymarket had him at 94 and 95 percent, respectively. Were markets just putting their trust in Split Ticket and Silver? It’s possible, but the markets and the models are not always aligned. In the Nevada governor’s race, where the Democrat also beat his Republican opponent, VoteHub, another model, gave the Democrat a 58 percent chance of winning, and FLIPR Deluxe gave him a 53 percent chance. Kalshi and Polymarket had him at about 75 percent. This time, maybe the markets were more attuned to early voting, as Silver himself suggested—or maybe they just got lucky. (In many cases, overindexing on early voting is ill-advised.)

The prediction-market industry had a lot to lose in these midterms—not only because lawmakers of both parties are preparing for a regulatory crackdown, but also because Tuesday night was a test of what these platforms can actually do. Kalshi and Polymarket have been trying to convince regulators and the American public that their markets have real uses for society beyond speculation. The industry’s position is that its platforms are public tools that can benefit observers just as much as the hedge funds and market makers who trade on them. But whether the markets can prove their worth as forecasters will depend on whether they can consistently produce the kind of discrepancy we saw in Nevada.

For many commentators, this Tuesday was a testament to the power of these markets to forecast elections. That itself is a boon for the companies. In declaring victory, though, Kalshi and Polymarket are exaggerating the extent of their vindication. Despite the outsize media attention and PR, election markets represent a tiny fraction of today’s betting industry. Bloomberg reports that “last month, politics accounted for just 1% of trading volume across Kalshi and Polymarket, down from two-thirds during the 2024 presidential election.” Sports, parlays, and crypto trades make up the majority of that volume—and the majority of the revenue. It might be more accurate to say that these firms are sports-betting platforms with a politics function tacked on. Outside of elections, the social benefits of these markets are harder to see.

Outgoing Speaker of the House Mike Johnson made a statement earlier this year that captured his approach to the job: “I have no intention of getting in the way of President Trump and his administration.” He was answering a question about levying tariffs, a power the Constitution gives to Congress. Trump grabbed it. Johnson let him.

That’s been the pattern for most of Johnson’s tenure. When the interests of the White House and the House diverged, Johnson proved loyal to Trump and a traitor to the institution he was supposed to lead. As his run ends, I wonder: Has he been the worst speaker of all time?

His partisan affiliations and views are not the deciding factors in this evaluation. If a speaker is to be judged by the legislation he helped to pass or the worst parts of his ideology or conduct, then each one who entrenched slavery would clearly rank lower. But if a speaker is to be judged on how he performs the core job of protecting the House’s independence and authority over its enumerated powers, then no speaker has done more than Johnson to subvert the constitutional and civic purpose of that vital institution.

In 2016, Johnson, who practiced constitutional law, was elected to the House of Representatives after one term in the Louisiana state legislature. When Trump lost the 2020 election, Johnson helped organize and signed an amicus brief in a lawsuit that sought to overturn Joe Biden’s victories in Georgia, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. After the storming of the U.S. Capitol, Johnson joined Republicans who wanted to overturn Biden’s wins in Arizona and Pennsylvania. That readiness to subordinate the will of American voters to Trump’s ambitions may have helped Johnson become speaker in 2023, and it foreshadowed his sycophancy after Trump’s return to office.

The power of the purse is a key function of the House, yet Johnson sided with Trump, and against horrified fiscal conservatives, to pass a spending bill that violated his own standard of how to wield that power responsibly. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the most significant legislation that he engineered into law, is expected to increase deficits by trillions of dollars—despite Johnson’s own promise to resolve what he rightly describes as “unprecedented debt and spending” that jeopardize the country’s stability and security.

Power over tariffs, declarations of war, and oversight of the executive branch are other core responsibilities of Congress. On tariffs, Johnson did not merely acquiesce as Trump usurped an Article I power to impose duties on foreign nations (a congressional authority that Johnson had once sought to protect before he became speaker)—he took steps to stymie the ability of fellow House members to cast votes against the president’s tariffs.

This speaker has repeatedly acted to stop the House from speaking. When Trump launched a war against Iran, without congressional approval or public support, Johnson tried to shut down floor action to prevent a war-powers vote that would rebuke him. When House members attempted to force the release of files associated with the Jeffrey Epstein investigation in 2025, Johnson adjourned the session early, sending lawmakers home to delay a vote. After the Democrat Adelita Grijalva won a House seat in an Arizona special election that September, Johnson delayed swearing her in for 50 days, a dereliction of his duty to ensure that his fellow Americans were represented. Democrats accused him of stalling because her signature on the Epstein petition would have forced a vote. (Johnson denied that the Epstein matter influenced his decision, and later voted to disclose the files, after Trump changed his position on the release.)

All the while, amid allegations of presidential corruption related to foreign gifts and investment, pardons and clemency, stock profiteering and suspicious no-bid federal contracts, Johnson’s House has all but abdicated its oversight role. For almost two years, Trump has been mostly unchecked. Only future oversight can clarify the full costs of that inattention.

Admittedly, there is no definite way to identify the worst speaker in history. Since the first Congress, 56 individuals have presided over the House. Americans constantly rank past presidents against one another. Vanishingly few could name 10 speakers, let alone compare them. Even experts had seemingly never bothered to rank House speakers until 1998, when an Iowa State University master’s student named Dennis Roy Still sent a survey to 60 congressional scholars. Although he soon realized that many of them were “unfamiliar with the speakership prior to the Civil War,” he found that the worst-ranked speaker was William Pennington, an inexperienced and ineffectual leader who was elected in 1859, shortly before the Civil War.

Among modern speakers, Jim Wright is remembered for resigning amid a slew of ethics-violations allegations, and highly partisan speakers such as Newt Gingrich and Nancy Pelosi have been regarded as especially effective or especially divisive—depending on whom you ask. Then there’s Denny Hastert, the speaker from 1999 to 2007, who is a clear villain: Long after his tenure as speaker, he admitted to sexually abusing children. But even judging him on his official acts alone, the Nation writer John Nichols declared in 2007 that Hastert was “the most lamentable” speaker in history. Hastert allowed George W. Bush, a fellow Republican, to invade Iraq and Afghanistan without declaring war and “rubber-stamped the administration’s anti-Constitutional assaults on civil liberties,” Nichols argued, rendering “an essential legislative chamber as nothing more than an extension of the executive branch.”

Although I believe that Bush-era Congresses often voted unwisely, at least they did vote on matters including the Authorization for Use of Military Force in Afghanistan, the Patriot Act, and the Iraq War (Hastert helped introduce the 2002 authorization for force in Iraq). Compare this with Johnson’s House, which never authorized the use of military force in Trump’s Venezuela war or his Iran war. Although a war-powers vote on Iran did eventually happen, it took place in spite of Johnson. And this undeclared war persists without House-imposed consequences for the president. Add in tariffs and oversight, and all told, the current House has abdicated its constitutional role far more than Hastert’s did.

On Tuesday, voters gave their verdict on the House that Johnson led. In a historic rebuke, they ousted Republicans from power. Democrats, who currently hold four fewer House seats than Republicans, are projected to have at least a 50-seat majority in 2027.

Beyond that decisive signal of disapproval from voters, Johnson’s legacy will be undermining the independence of the legislative branch. Tapped to lead the House, he repeatedly betrayed it, ceding its enumerated powers and impeding colleagues who tried to check the president. For the love of the House, good riddance to speaker Johnson.

Democrats Have No Coherent Plan for AI

Among the many gifts that Donald Trump bequeathed to Democrats ahead of the midterms was allowing them the exclusive political use of the AI backlash. Now that Democrats have decisively won control of Congress, voters will expect them to follow through on their rhetoric. The problem is that Democrats don’t actually have a plan to regulate AI. In fact, they don’t even agree on what problems need to be solved in the first place.

Until recently, federal AI legislation seemed impossible until 2029 at the earliest. Trump has consistently opposed any form of AI regulation, going so far as to pressure Congress to preempt state laws. Although some Republican lawmakers have seemed more sympathetic to AI regulation, almost none would dare defy their party’s leader. Of the roughly 150 AI-focused bills that have been proposed during Trump’s second term, only one—a bill that criminalized the nonconsensual posting of sexual images, including AI-generated ones—ultimately passed.

Tuesday night’s astonishing results have made AI regulation a much more realistic possibility. Democrats could easily pass an AI bill through the House of Representatives, where they will have a huge majority. And with their surprisingly large Senate majority, they would need only a handful of Republicans to get to a filibuster-proof 60 votes. Republicans could be far more likely to vote with Democrats on AI following an electoral shellacking in which voters made abundantly clear that Trump was on the wrong side of the issue. The threat of a veto remains, but as a lame duck, the president might not care as much as he used to about the risk of slowing down the AI-driven stock-market boom.

So Democrats might be able to get something done about AI. They just don’t seem to know what that something should be. This is true even when it comes to the one AI-related issue that, politically speaking, most obviously needs to be addressed: data centers. Voters are overwhelmingly opposed to the construction of data centers in their communities. Several candidates, including James Talarico in Texas, Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, Sherrod Brown in Ohio, and Annie Andrews in South Carolina, placed their opposition to data centers at the core of their campaign. But opinions differ widely about what the precise problem with data centers is and what ought to be done about them. Some politicians focused on rising power bills and proposed making data centers pay for their own electricity generation and grid upgrades. Some seized on the fact that many data-center deals are done in secret, and argued that they should be subject to local approval. And others proposed getting rid of data-center tax breaks. A few candidates, such as Gina Hinojosa, now Texas’s governor-elect, and Andrews, campaigned on a full data-center moratoriums. These proposals varied widely in how long they would last and under what conditions they would be lifted.

[Matteo Wong: Silicon Valley’s very bad night]

Even if Democrats did agree on what to do about data centers, federal legislation wouldn’t necessarily be the right way to achieve their goals. Most Democrats have cast the issue as a state and local one. This might explain why Senator Bernie Sanders’s proposal for a federal data-center ban attracted zero co-sponsors in the Senate.

Beyond data centers, the questions get harder still. For starters, the party doesn’t have one shared view of what aspects of AI need to be regulated. Some are worried about the possibility of mass job losses caused by automation, others about AI deepfakes or children becoming overly attached to chatbot companions. Considerable disagreement exists over how to address each of these concerns. Take “AI safety”—the set of fears that ranges from rogue AI agents carrying out cyberattacks to the possibility of superintelligent systems wiping out all of humanity. Many Democrats believe that something needs to be done to prevent AI from causing problems in the real world. But the ideas for how to do this are all over the place, and each has its own drawbacks.

One school of thought focuses on making sure that AI companies can be held legally responsible if and when their models cause harm. But liability is a fix that occurs only after the damage has already been done. As Charlie Bullock, the senior policy director for the Institute for Law and AI, put it to me, “No one can sue if we’re all dead.”

Another idea is to require companies to build a “kill switch” that would shut their models down at the federal government’s command. But knowing whether a model is acting in dangerous ways before the damage occurs is difficult. Earlier this year, a group of rogue AI agents hacked into the company Hugging Face as well as OpenAI’s own internal servers without anyone at the company, let alone anyone in the federal government, realizing what had happened until well after the attacks had occurred. In other words, by the time someone thinks to reach for the kill switch, it may be too late.

Recently, bipartisan momentum has been building to require AI companies to submit to safety audits, managed by either a new federal agency or approved third-party organizations. This would give the government earlier and deeper insight into the safety of AI models before they’re deployed. But even then, knowing exactly how a model is going to behave in the real world is impossible. As AI models have gotten more and more advanced, they have begun to demonstrate awareness of when they are and aren’t being tested.

This leads to yet another idea: Slow it all down. If AI models are getting too smart to control, the thinking goes, then technology companies should pause their development at least until they know how to properly manage them. This is probably the best way to protect against the possibility of a superintelligent rogue AI. But it raises a set of new, difficult trade-offs. The first is geopolitical: Just because the United States decides to throttle its own industry doesn’t mean other countries, particularly China, will do the same. In such a situation, the dangers of unchecked AI progress would still exist, and America’s main geopolitical rival would control the world’s most powerful new technology. The second is more straightforwardly political: A forced slowdown could cause a stock-market correction, angering voters. Indeed, the S&P 500 fell 4.3 percent today, in part over fears that Democrats will follow through on promises to slow the data-center build-out.

Going from campaign slogans to sensible legislation will require dealing with the hard trade-offs of governing a new technology. Railing against Big Tech and data centers is easy. Figuring out what to do about them is much harder. But one clear lesson from the midterms is that voters have little patience for politicians who don’t even try to deliver on their core promises. Democrats would be wise not to make the same mistake that just helped get them elected.

The Ballad of Bob Dylan and the Pope

At first, the image appears to be some kind of intergalactic photo op, something out of Star Wars. A figure hooded in black, his craggy face partly in shadow, greets a serene-looking man in a white robe. Think Emperor Palpatine shaking hands with Obi-Wan Kenobi, perhaps brokering a cease-fire in the Clone Wars. Closer inspection yields a picture that is no less weird: Bob Dylan is hanging with the pope.

What they actually spoke about during their meeting yesterday at the Vatican’s Apostolic Palace, we may never know. But the questions only begin there! Why is Dylan’s head covered by a hood? Why, in some photos, does he have gloves and an umbrella? Why does he seem to have abandoned tying his polka-dotted tie mid-flip? And: Is the pope a fan? (Actually, that last one might have an answer—in a recent book about disarmament, the pope quoted Dylan’s 1963 deep cut “Go Away You Bomb”; “I want that Bomb—I want it settin in my mouth like a cigar,” goes one line. This is unfiltered Dylan that only a true fan could love.)

The most famous American musician of the past half century visits the leader of the Catholic Church. The strangeness, if not the Sith hood, makes you laugh. But these two make some sense together, if not as obvious friends then at least, each in their own way, as spiritually ambitious people. Also, they are both midwestern Bobs. Robert Zimmerman, born in Duluth, Minnesota, in 1941, meet Robert Francis Prevost, born in Chicago in 1955. They share the common matter-of-factness of people who grew up on the flat plains, who were born in the middle. There is no frenetic city energy to them. They even have similarly enigmatic smiles—lips tight in a straight line, not giving away too much. Their gifts to each other were pretty unfussy, too. Dylan handed the pope a copy of his memoir, Chronicles: Volume One, and the pope gave Dylan—of course—a rosary.

[Read: The pope doubles down on the beautiful struggle]

From their middle-class, conventional Middle American upbringings, they both embarked on wild transformations. Dylan used traditional American music to mold himself into a troubadour from nowhere. He abandoned his given name, invented a mythology around his origins, and still resists definition. Prevost had a different kind of reinvention. He joined an ancient religious order, submitted himself to its disciplines, and ultimately assumed an identity set by its history, losing his name to become the pope.

Two midwestern-American boys, avatars of self-transformation, now aged but still happily wearing costumes. But there’s more: In a moment when so many people are worried about how artificial intelligence is flattening human expression, could there be anything more affirming of human uniqueness than the life and work of Dylan and the pope?

Even Dylan’s idiosyncratic outfit was the product of ingenuity that a computer couldn’t dream of matching, if computers could dream. The workwear designer Gauthier Borsarello instantly spotted that the jacket was his own creation. “The good thing about designing everything (even the puller of your zipper) from scratch is recognizing your own design without a doubt,” Borsarello wrote on Instagram.

The pope, meanwhile, has become one of the most eloquent defenders of human creativity in the face of technological advance. His encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas, published this year, articulated beautifully why human limitations and our efforts to overcome them make us special and give our lives meaning. “Authentic culture and art” preserve a “spark,” he wrote; they allow people to take in the experiences of others, all the vulnerability and pain, and reflect them back in ways that elevate, resulting in Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony and Picasso’s Guernica. (And why not “Subterranean Homesick Blues”?—though the pope sadly didn’t put this on his list of faves.)

And then there’s Dylan. Perhaps no other artist has reveled for so long in mixing and matching as much as he does, ingesting enormous quantities of cultural material, breaking them into pieces, and recombining them into something new. That’s been his whole schtick, from the beginning. In a way it anticipates what AI has supercharged, but he does it as only a human can, informed by a lifetime of listening, reading, performing, and inhabiting the American cultural imagination.

[Read: Rock and roll faces the inevitable]

Take “A Hard Rain’s a-Gonna Fall,” from 1962. In it, he riffs on the question-and-answer structure from a traditional English-Scottish border ballad called “Lord Randall” (“Oh where ha’e ye been, Lord Randall, my son? O where ha’e ye been, my handsome young man?”). But Dylan infuses the form with surreal, apocalyptic imagery related to the Cold War and the anxiety of annihilation: “And what did you hear, my blue-eyed son? And what did you hear, my darling young one? I heard the sound of a thunder, it roared out a warnin’. Heard the roar of a wave that could drown the whole world.” Only the synapses of his weird brain could produce this. In his 2001 album, Love and Theft (an aptly titled middle finger to anyone who has a problem with this method), he lifts whole sentences from Confessions of a Yakuza, the memoir of a Japanese gangster.

I’d like to think this is the question Dylan and the pope, sitting next to bookcases full of Catholic exegesis, discussed: What makes humans worth preserving? The photo of them is indelible because it’s sort of the answer. They’re dressed in black and in white, the whole spectrum of good to evil. One’s an artist and the other’s a priest, each aspiring to prophecy. They’re both accessing something larger than themselves, even though when you look at them, they’re small, old men, nearer to the end of their existences than to the beginning—the pope battling cancer, and Dylan in his mid-80s. The spark, however, persists.

James Talarico talked a lot during his campaign about the so-called wilderness—as in, the electoral wasteland and morale morass that Democrats had been languishing in since President Trump’s reelection, in 2024. In Texas, this felt like something of a permanent condition. Talarico preferred a more sanguine interpretation of wilderness, something I’ve thought about after he became the first Texas Democrat to win a Senate race since 1988. The wilderness, he said from early on in his campaign, could in fact prove a fertile ground for reinvention.

“There is an opportunity to redefine the Democratic Party,” Talarico told me. “And you can’t do that if the brand is super set, or if there’s a strong leader at the top of the party.” This was a little over a year ago, and we were sitting in the lobby bar of an Arlington, Texas, hotel. “The wilderness,” Talarico continued, “is where new ideas and new leaders and new movements come forth.” At the time, I was inclined to dismiss this as a rationalization from another one of those fool’s-gold Democratic hopefuls in Texas. In retrospect, maybe Talarico was onto something.

If nothing else, the Democrats’ midterm rout of Trump and his supine coalition of Republicans would seem to put to rest, at least for now, the long-running discussion of Democrats as a party out of touch, ill-defined, lacking in purpose, and perpetually ambling in this “wilderness.” I admit that I participated in a fair amount in this Democratic-doldrums discourse myself, including in an opus about the party adrift that ran in February, which now feels like a decade ago. I stand by that story. It was true then, and had been for nearly four years, if you start the clock with the 2022 midterms and the disastrous denouement of the Biden years.

The sheer magnitude of Tuesday’s drubbing of Republicans should provide an antidote to the wallowing that Democrats can be prone to. It’s impossible not to see the geographic reach of their victories, the generational ascent of so many potential stars, and the stunning margins that Democrats ran up across the country and not conclude that the party has legitimate reason to feel good about itself. This was a bona fide “thumpin,’” as another unpopular Republican president, George W. Bush, called his own midterm debacle 20 years ago.

The requisite chest-thumpin’ from Democrats commenced immediately on Tuesday night. “We have five words for the American people,” Chuck Schumer, the likely next Senate majority leader, proclaimed to reporters as the returns poured in. “Relief is on the way.”

Here are five more words for Democrats to keep in mind: Enjoy this while it lasts.

The “what next” of this discussion is now at hand. And it’s pretty clear that Democrats should avoid the obvious trap of thinking that their victory means they’ve magically solved their “brand problem,” “trust problem,” or “working-class-voter problem,” among various others. The initial sense of punch-drunk euphoria will yield to questions about what the party’s sprawling new legislative majorities should focus on, how they should engage (if at all) with Trump and Republicans, what ideological framework (progressive versus moderate) they should embrace, and who should lead them going into 2028. Political victory laps can be fun but fleeting, especially for governing tribes famously given to angst and discord. Inevitably, the second-guessing and hand-wringing will return, and probably soon.

In politics, the simplest explanation for an election result is usually the smartest one (and less time-consuming than reading those endless postmortems). Tuesday’s result can be overwhelmingly ascribed to a repudiation of Trump, rather than revealing anything definitive about the diverse assortment of Democrats who prevailed. The political analyst Charlie Cook, of The Cook Political Report, gave preliminary voice to this notion when he predicted in September that this midterm would represent a “red undertow” rather than a blue wave. The results would be driven more by revulsion toward Trump and his various legislative lickspittles than by any dramatic reinvention by Democrats—or by some sudden mass affection for them by the electorate.

This was rendered vividly in an NBC News poll released just before the election, in which voters declared themselves just not that into Democrats, despite their massive advantages leading into Election Day. Only 29 percent of respondents said that they viewed the party favorably, while 38 percent viewed Republicans positively. About half of the people surveyed said that they held a negative view of both parties.

Trump will be around as the Democrats’ go-to foil for only so much longer. He has been a turnout machine for them as well as for Republicans. He has defined the GOP for the past decade, and has also defined the Democrats in opposition. It is not hard to imagine that a Republican president who did not, say, capriciously impose tariffs, start unpopular wars, or talk incessantly about building a White House ballroom would have had a considerably better midterm experience than Trump and the GOP just did. And far fewer Americans would have ever heard of Senators-Elect Talarico, Annie Andrews (South Carolina), or Adam Hamilton (Kansas).

What should also be clear to Democrats is that their time is limited. Again and again, voters have shown punishing impatience with parties that don’t deliver for them quickly, especially on such close-to-the-bone economic issues as inflation. Americans have not hesitated to swing back and forth between the two parties. When was the last election, anyway, in which voters have not been described as “angry,” “frustrated,” or “fed-up?”

In the short term, at least, Democrats can break out their Nutter Butters and enjoy the crack-up on the other side, where the center of political drama and division in America has shifted decisively since Tuesday. House Speaker Mike Johnson said that he won’t run for minority leader, setting up a potentially bitter race to succeed him; Vice President Vance blamed Tuesday’s pummeling on the Iran war (wait, whose idea was that?); and Trump chalked up the defeat to not being on the ballot himself (effectively blaming Republicans who were).

Democrats suddenly have real leverage in Washington. Trump is, to say the least, not easily humbled and not much for self-examination or course correction (to wit, he spent part of yesterday showing off his in-progress White House ballroom). He is, however, a survivor, and will take what lifelines are available. Lo and behold: “I have a lot of friends who are Democrats, believe it or not,” Trump said on Fox. “I’ve already spoken to some of them,” he added. Presumably, one of these people was Schumer, who indicated Tuesday that he would be open to working with Trump, so long as he “will actually change his ways.” It certainly beats the wilderness.

Yesterday, I reached out to Democratic National Committee chair Ken Martin, whose arrival in Washington last year was marked by infighting at the DNC, a fundraising drought, and unflattering stories about him in the press—my own among them. I’d made him the hapless head of Democratic doom in my article in February, characterizing the chairman’s demeanor as one of “resting dread face,” a description that he presumably did not appreciate. So after the election, I invited him to take an oratorical victory lap on my head. He demurred, except for this brief “update” from DNC Communications Director Rosemary Boeglin, via text: “Ken Martin has turned the frown upside down on his resting dread face.”
