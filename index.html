This is an edition of The Atlantic Daily, a newsletter that guides you through the biggest stories of the day, helps you discover new ideas, and recommends the best in culture. Sign up for it here.

On Wednesday morning, Americans awoke to the news that the Democrats had retaken both the House and the Senate. The next day, the stock market hemorrhaged $2.5 trillion.

The tech sector was hardest hit. Alphabet, Nvidia, and the major semiconductor companies shed billions. Tesla and SpaceX, whose founder, Elon Musk, had spent half a billion dollars trying to keep Democrats out of Congress, were suddenly worth approximately $420 billion less than they had been two days before.

In some ways, the election results were a referendum on Big Tech. Recent polls have revealed that most voters want the government to impose new regulations on AI, and many victorious Democrats campaigned on a promise to move away from the unchecked expansionism that has defined the industry during the Donald Trump era. Texas Governor-Elect Gina Hinojosa and Senator-Elect Annie Andrews of South Carolina have both pledged to stop the construction of new data centers, which have been the subject of voter panic across the country in recent months. (These facilities—physical manifestations of the industry’s clout—can raise local electricity costs and pollute the air.) Others vowed to stand up to imperious Silicon Valley elites, or to hold the president accountable for his corruption—much of which is linked to his family’s cryptocurrency ventures. Last week’s sell-off was a sign that the stock market is taking lawmakers at their word.

At the same time, the dip hinted at a potential challenge for Democrats. Voters made clear that they were unhappy with the cost of living, and many Democrats who ran on affordability ended up winning big. Widespread fears about what AI might do to jobs only fueled the party’s comeback. The irony is that AI companies are responsible for a sizable chunk of the United States’ GDP growth; AI products have invaded our professional and personal lives, as well as our pension funds and 401(k)s. All Americans now have a stake in the AI economy, whether they’ve opted in or not. It might even be more accurate to say that, at this point, AI is the economy.

According to a recent Bloomberg story, “nearly $32 trillion in market value had been added to the S&P 500 Index since OpenAI released ChatGPT, and more than half of that gain came from just 10 companies—all linked to artificial intelligence.” The article also noted that “almost all the growth in US business spending last year came from spending on technology,” much of which is, of course, connected to AI and the ongoing data-center build-out. The technology’s effects on the economy are complex, and not purely positive: Demand for AI is contributing to inflation and helping keep long-term-bond yields relatively high. But AI has become so entrenched that Democrats’ efforts to target the industry could have ripple effects. Their challenge will be to regulate AI in the ways they campaigned on without damaging the economy they’ve promised to protect.

It’s no accident that this technology has so thoroughly infiltrated the world of American business. Many companies have been eager to adopt it (despite the lack of proof that it makes workers more productive), and investors are hungry for a piece of what they see as humanity’s next great technical evolution. But the federal government has enabled this expansion too. Even though the industry’s leaders have warned that their technology may destroy the world, and even though AI’s ability to wreak havoc on vital infrastructure, such as banks and power grids, has become clearer and clearer, the Trump administration has refused to implement strict controls on the sector. In fact, the White House has gone out of its way to try to override state-level rule making on AI, asking Congress to adopt its pro-AI policy recommendations. And the president himself has maintained close relationships with Silicon Valley’s leaders and personally invested in AI firms. Recall that the CEOs of Meta, Apple, and Google stood behind Trump at his second inauguration, and that their companies collectively donated tens of millions of dollars to the new White House ballroom.

Meanwhile, the administration’s other policies have weakened the economy. Although the stock market has done well during Trump’s second term, the health of the American economy is measured in more than just the valuations of its biggest companies. The president’s decision to implement tariffs and to start a war in Iran have pushed up the cost of living. Energy prices have spiked, and inflation has lately outpaced wage growth, meaning that workers’ dollars aren’t going as far.

Now lawmakers have a chance to fight for the kind of changes they’ve been promising, and AI is an important part of their calculus. My colleague Matteo Wong wrote earlier this month that AI is an engine of growth, but it’s also hogging some of the attention and the investments that might have once gone to other sectors. “The industry seems to be holding not just the American economy but our entire society hostage,” he explained. “Nvidia, Google, and the like had better succeed financially, or there go your savings.” (Yesterday, Trump joined Silicon Valley leaders in defending Nvidia’s Jensen Huang after reports emerged that the CEO had cheated on his wife with an alleged Chinese spy—another acknowledgement of the country’s deep reliance on Huang and his company.)

It doesn’t help that the Democrats don’t have a coherent plan for reining in AI. As my colleague Rogé Karma has written, individual candidates have proposed some ideas, including a federally controlled kill switch that can shut models down in an emergency and an outright pause on advanced AI development. Some have made the very reasonable point that slowing the growth of AI here in the United States is a moral imperative—a way to safeguard humanity, akin to nuclear disarmament—and that it’s more important than any economic trade-offs. But voters worried about their savings may not ultimately see it that way. There are geopolitical trade-offs, too: Although legislators can choose to hamper AI’s development in the U.S., it’s unlikely that China, the country’s primary tech rival, will choose to do the same.

This week, the stock market has been well on the way to recovery. But if AI companies are weakened in the months and years to come, all Americans will eventually be affected—not just the early investors and executives stewarding this industry. Whatever congressional Democrats decide, the interconnectedness of the AI economy ensures that their moves will have consequences that extend well beyond their chosen targets.

The Lost World of Aquaterra

By Toby Lester

Imagine a planet that’s home not only to the seven continents you know but also to an immense feature you’ve never heard of, continental in scale. Extending along the world’s coastal shelves from the tropics almost to the poles, this place is roughly equivalent in size to South America, with a total area of more than 6.6 million square miles. During the last Ice Age, it consisted almost entirely of lowland ecosystems: cold grasslands and tundras in the far north, grazed by woolly mammoths and steppe bison; temperate woodlands and open country in the middle latitudes, home to aurochs, horses, and giant elk; tropical forests, grasslands, and mangrove swamps near the equator, inhabited by Stegodons, Asian hippos, and Komodo dragons; and dry scrub in the far south, roamed by marsupial lions, giant flightless birds, and lizards the size of crocodiles. Great rivers, fed by an abundance of lakes and smaller streams, cut their way through many of these lowlands, eventually opening out into marshes, lagoons, estuaries, and tidal flats as they approached the coast …

As it happens, there really is such a place. You’ve almost certainly been there yourself. It has even been given a name—and, no, it’s not Atlantis. It’s Aquaterra.

Since late last year, a small but growing chorus of cybersecurity experts has been warning that AI models were pushing the internet toward a sort of cataclysm. Hospitals, banks, utilities, basically anything and everything connected to the web could be hacked.

Over the past month or so, those scenarios have come alive in almost spectacular fashion: Last month, AI was used to find major security flaws in software used by 70 percent of websites, prompting governments to take down their websites as a precaution. A week later, attackers targeted the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange, Binance, in part by hijacking the company’s internal AI tools, and ultimately drained billions of dollars. This fall alone, AI has been used to hack hundreds of financial institutions in Latin America, not to mention data centers, banks, and government entities around the world. AI has also changed the economics of hacking, such that targeting individual people and small organizations is becoming cheap and easy enough to be worthwhile. And there’s a broader rise in cyberattacks that, though not directly tied to AI, likely leveraged the technology in some way. Last week, for instance, the FBI issued a warning about hacks targeting industrial refrigeration systems following attacks on grocery stores in France, Belgium, Ireland, Sweden, Israel, and other countries.

[Read: Perhaps connecting fridges to the internet was a bad idea]

Governments and Silicon Valley entrepreneurs, who have been tracking this most closely, are steeling themselves against what promises to be an extended period of cybersecurity vulnerability—and they advise that you do the same. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth recently said that every U.S. military base should be able to continue operating even in the case of a cyberattack disrupting the power grid. Japan has declared a state of emergency in cyberspace. South Korea’s National Intelligence Service has reportedly ordered thousands of organizations to conduct emergency cybersecurity audits. The United Kingdom is telling citizens to have several days of water and food prepared in the event of a number of potential disasters, including a cyberattack. Everybody is starting to sound, just a little bit, like a doomsday prepper, and not without reason.

So how do you keep your accounts, your family, and your finances all safe? For everyday users of the internet, there are low-lift steps you can take to avoid being a sitting duck in this new reality. The most important and easiest thing is to get your digital affairs in order. Use different passwords, ideally long and randomly generated, for all of your accounts and store them in a password manager. Enable two-factor authentication everywhere you can—that is, opt in to receiving a confirmation code on your phone or, ideally, inside a dedicated app to add a layer of security to sensitive accounts. In addition, frequently update laptops, smartphones, internet routers, and various applications. Otherwise, unpatched security flaws mean that clicking on a malicious link can allow a hacker to steal cookies and login information from your browser and bypass even two-factor authentication methods. Are these extra steps annoying? Yes. But are they more annoying than having hackers dip into your life savings and post bitcoin advertisements from your X account? No, they are not.

Additionally, be extra wary of social engineering. Phishing scams have gotten really, really good: Fake customer-service websites now regularly turn up in search results and direct you to AI voice agents, ready to steal your private information. You may receive personalized texts that look like they’re from your boss, a real-estate agent, an IRS official—you name it. The first step in the historic hack on Binance reportedly started with an employee clicking on a malicious advertisement. So, be on guard and inspect links before clicking. That can be as simple as hovering your cursor over a hyperlink in your email to view the full website address to see if anything looks suspicious (misspellings, extra words and characters). If a representative of your bank, power company, or another service provider calls you, double-check by calling them back on the official company number.

If you start taking these basic steps to prepare for AI-powered cyberattacks, you might start wondering just how far you can go. In Silicon Valley, an industry flush with money and people who tend to envision all kinds of apocalyptic scenarios, many tend to go to extremes. The influential investor Peter Thiel, who has recently given lectures about the “Antichrist,” has a sprawling property in a remote part of New Zealand on which he once planned to build a bunker-like cottage before a local council rejected the proposal. In 2016, Sam Altman told The New Yorker that he has “guns, gold, potassium iodide, antibiotics, batteries, water, gas masks from the Israeli Defense Force, and a big patch of land in Big Sur I can fly to.” These are edge cases, but they can be instructive in their own ways.

Others in Silicon Valley are undertaking less grandiose physical preparations. Jeffrey Ladish, the executive director of the AI-safety group Palisade Research, told me that he’s cautious about relying on the cloud to store his data. “It might be worth having a hard drive or some Blu-ray discs” with, say, photos and important documents. He also suggests stocking up at least a few days of food and clean water, as well as flashlights and some cash. “These could be useful whether it’s a pandemic, whether it’s a tornado, whether hacking takes down the power grid or takes down the banks,” he told me. Our conversation reminded him to check his supplies. “I should actually check on my fresh water,” he said.

Edouard Harris, the chief technology officer of the AI-safety company Gladstone AI, recommended downloading “parts of the internet that you care about the most”—for me, that might mean saving the many Atlantic articles I’ve written that only exist online. If you’re the type who partakes in cryptocurrency, that could mean moving it to a “cold crypto wallet,” or an offline hard drive. On top of that, Harris told me, “my bank password, my core email-account password, things like that are just not saved in digital format. They’re just written down on paper.” He is worried about an AI-powered hack taking down crucial infrastructure, which could, for instance, limit his ability to get power or water. That’s not a far-fetched concern: This summer, cyberattacks disrupted water utilities across the country and shut down a British power plant. Attacks on refrigeration systems could disrupt food or pharmaceutical supply chains, leaving people without groceries or essential medication.

In an extreme scenario that Harris described to me, nearly any device connected to the internet would come under attack, including your car. Harris built a website called Endgame Ready that offers a doomsday-prep kit. There are a number of similar guides circulating in the AI industry with names such as “Doomsday Pack” and a “Survival LLM” that can offer survival advice even, if you download the latter, in the absence of internet service.

Jeffrey Schlegelmilch, an associate professor and faculty director of the National Center for Disaster Preparedness at the Columbia Climate School, likened cybersecurity attacks to any other kind of disaster. “If you don’t have power, what does it matter if it was caused by an earthquake or caused by a cyberattack?” he asked me. In this view, the basic preparations suggested by Ladish and others are sensible: The typical gear recommended in the event of an earthquake or tornado—a few days’s worth of food and water, a flashlight, and a battery-powered radio—still apply. (The National Center for Disaster Preparedness has an interactive guide for making such a kit.) The most important thing, Schlegelmilch said, is to befriend—and plan alongside—your neighbors, as connectedness is “one of the strongest predictors of how communities do in the aftermath of a disaster.”

Harris has gone further still, acknowledging to me that he’s “unusual in how much effort I’ve put into this.” He has functionally disconnected his vehicle from the internet and uses 55-gallon drums to store water. For those with time and resources, he recommends storing electronics and hard drives in hardened, metal suitcases that block out radio frequencies, and buying both a wood stove and a 3-D printer to produce any parts you might need. He also recommends pooling resources with friends or family to purchase a “bugout property,” on which to take refuge, and set it up to run off-grid.

Some of this is easy to gawk at, and many of us have more pressing everyday concerns, such as the stubbornly high cost of gas and groceries and high interest rates. But it’s prudent to be careful, and governments around the world are advising just that. You don’t need to have a remote cottage, to downgrade your car, or even to save up for a solar panel to prepare. Even if cyberattacks don’t disrupt widespread infrastructure, the most practical steps are the easiest to take: Review your digital hygiene, pick up some durable pantry goods on a grocery run, have a flashlight and spare batteries in the closet. Whether you’re dealing with an AI-powered hack from Iran or just your run-of-the-mill brownout, you’ll be happy you have them.

Susie Wiles should have been untouchable. She’d been with President Trump since his first bid for the White House. She was one of the few people who’d stuck around after the January 6 attacks on the U.S. Capitol, when just about everyone else thought his political career was over. And as his chief of staff for the past two years, she was a stabilizing force in an administration that sorely needed one. But Wiles—whom Trump jettisoned last week after Republicans’ devastating midterm losses—likely had at least one thing working against her: that second X chromosome.

Since returning to the White House, Trump has refrained from the firing sprees of his first term, but he has shown a remarkable willingness to discard his female senior administration officials when they displease him. With the abrupt elevation of Stephen Miller to chief of staff last Friday, Wiles joined that most unenviable troop—The Scapegals.

Including Wiles, Trump has fired or pushed out at least half a dozen top women this term, including Attorney General Pam Bondi, Labor Secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer, Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard, Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, and White House Press Secretary Katie Zacharia. (Zacharia announced the departure of Ambassador to Greece Kimberly Guilfoyle from the White House podium—the only news she would share, it turned out—a mere three days before she, too, was summarily dismissed.) Other senior women have also left—Karoline Leavitt, who preceded Zacharia as press secretary, and Harmeet Dhillon, who ran the Civil Rights Division at the Justice Department, among others—albeit on their own terms. Most of the senior women who were forced out have since been replaced by men.

When charges of misogyny pop up against Trump, as they have with regularity in both of his terms, his defenders are quick to claim that he is an equal-opportunity offender who is just as likely to attack, say, a white male journalist as he is a female reporter of color. But this time, counterexamples are hard to find. One of the few senior men to have been jettisoned is Mike Waltz, Trump’s first national security adviser, who was binned after less than four months, following his Signalgate mishap. (He ended up with a cushy consolation prize: ambassador to the United Nations.)

A number of the discarded women had, shall we say, less than illustrious records. Zacharia—who failed the California bar yet repeatedly referred to herself as a lawyer—was fired after five days on the job following a disastrous debut at the podium. (That’s less than half of a Full Scaramucci—the nickname for the 11-day tenure of Anthony Scaramucci, a fleeting supernova of a communications director during Trump’s first term.) Noem, meanwhile, presided over the killing of two Americans by immigration-enforcement officers in Minneapolis and spent taxpayer dollars on glitzy commercials, all while allegedly deferring to her rumored boyfriend, Corey Lewandowski (both have repeatedly denied an affair), to handle much of the Department of Homeland Security’s day-to-day operations. Bondi, for her part, seemed to view the top Justice post as that of Trump’s personal fixer. (Ironically, Trump’s ultimate frustration was not that she went too far in doing his bidding, but that she didn’t go far enough.) And Chavez-DeRemer, who left earlier this year, did so amid allegations of drinking on the job and an affair with a member of her security detail, as well as accusations from at least two female staff members that her husband had sexually assaulted them. (Chavez-DeRemer’s husband has denied the accusations through an attorney, and the police investigation of the reports has since been closed.)  

Like I said: These records are hard to defend. But even at the time of the Noem-Bondi double exit—Noem left in March, Bondi in April—a Trump official told me the administration risked the appearance of firing only “attractive” women, while allowing Trump’s top men to remain.

Now, about those men. It is not particularly difficult to list various sins and missteps involving many of Trump’s top male officials that, in most other administrations, would be grounds for serious reflection, if not outright dismissal. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth—who had not managed an organization larger than about 100 before becoming the Pentagon chief—is the face and top cheerleader for the administration’s unpopular war in Iran, which helped doom Republicans’ midterm chances. FBI Director Kash Patel generated bad headlines for alleged misuse of an FBI jet, for a viral video of him chugging beers with the U.S. men’s hockey team at the Olympics in Milan, and for using FBI resources to ferry his girlfriend around the country. And few people in the administration seem to have anything good to say about Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whom they privately gripe about as Trump’s most annoying buddy and who has bungled trade negotiations and undercut the administration’s messaging on Jeffrey Epstein. Trump has stood by them all.

[Read: The king of queens]

“Donald Trump has demonstrated a much shorter fuse with women in power during the second administration,” Sarah Matthews, a deputy press secretary in Trump’s first administration, told me. “I’m not saying Kristi Noem and some of these women don’t deserve to be fired, but I think some of the men are not being held to the same standard. And it’s a pretty glaring disparity of who he’s willing to fire and who he’s not.”

Matthews, who resigned after the January 6 attacks and now is a contributor at The Bulwark, said she blames misogyny, but also thinks that Trump often identifies with men under fire. (When Maine’s original Democratic Senate candidate faced allegations of sexual assault, including rape, Matthews noted, with a touch of exaggeration, that “Trump was the only person in America defending Graham Platner” before he dropped out.) Stephanie Grisham—a former White House communications director who also served as chief of staff to Melania Trump—recounted in a 2021 Washington Post op-ed that after she told Trump that her ex-boyfriend, a fellow White House staffer, had been physically abusive, the president expressed surprise. Neither he nor the first lady, whom she said she’d also told, ever followed up. Though Grisham did not name her alleged abuser in her essay, she was referring to Representative Max Miller of Ohio, who had become the president’s deputy campaign manager and kept his role despite Grisham’s disclosure to the first couple. Miller has since been accused of domestic violence by his now-ex-wife, Emily Moreno, and he lost his reelection bid last week.

When I reached out to the White House for this story, the press office sent back a list of nine women whom Trump had elevated to senior positions, including Wiles (the first female chief of staff) and Leavitt (the youngest press secretary ever, as well as the first one to give birth while serving in the role). An official also noted that Gabbard was neither fired nor forced to resign, but left because her husband was diagnosed with a rare form of bone cancer. In a statement, the White House spokesperson Taylor Rogers argued that the administration was a meritocracy, filled by people focused on delivering for the American people. “Unlike the identity-politics-obsessed Democrats, the President judges individuals on merit and qualifications, not immutable characteristics,” Rogers wrote.

I also emailed the trio of women—Small Business Administration chief Kelly Loeffler, Education Secretary Linda McMahon, and Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins—who remain in Trump’s Cabinet, to ask what they made of the high-profile-female departures, and see if they had any insights into why they had managed to survive for so long. The press offices of all three agencies wrote back. In a statement provided on her behalf, Rollins criticized “the constant effort to turn every personnel change into a gender story,” noting that Trump “has elevated women to some of the highest levels of government.” She added that, “as a woman, there is no one I would rather fight with to put Americans first than President Trump.” Andrew Nixon, the press secretary for the SBA, similarly said that Loeffler was proud to serve as one of Trump’s top officials. “President Trump hires based on talent and merit, not on gender, race, or any other social identity,” Nixon wrote. And the Department of Education spokesperson Ellen Keast wrote, simply, “The Atlantic cannot even define what a woman is but has no issue extorting sexism for a headline.”

Trump agencies are not always particularly responsive to reporter inquiries, but on this topic, all three were unusually prompt. After all, when you are one of a trio of women remaining in Trump’s Cabinet, it makes sense to be a happy warrior, relentlessly on message.

Look, I just want to say that if Nvidia CEO and reported spy-plot target Jensen Huang did get help from AI to write an apology email to his family and company about his affair (he’s said he uses AI to help write all of his first drafts), this might be the one use of AI that makes sense to me.

People (me) are always saying about AI, “This is a solution looking for a problem!” and “Who would possibly want to delegate things like knowing information about your children or creating works of art to a third-party series of zeroes and ones?” But there are some things in life that nobody wants to do, such as get a root canal, amputate a limb, or deal with your cable provider. Or, perhaps, apologize simultaneously to your employees and your wife of 40 years for jeopardizing your business and personal relationships and perhaps also national security? (To me, though, those feel like two separate emails.)

But I do see one problem for someone attempting to delegate the writing of this email: I don’t think this specific email has ever been written before, by anyone. “Business/Personal Email Apologizing for Affair/Possible National-Security Breach” is the email equivalent of those wedding dress codes—“Beach Formal”; “Back-of-Your-Closet Country Club”; “Cocktail Business Black Tie”—that are beyond what the human brain can understand.

I’m just wondering what training data set one would even consult in this scenario. What template was AI going to work with here? James Bond scripts? (“I owe everyone here at Goldfinger Enterprises an apology. My fixation on James Bond and the time I have wasted designing a laser specifically targeted at his groin have distracted us from our mission of blowing up Fort Knox to increase the value of our gold supplies. To my many colleagues, especially Odd Job, I value our working relationship. Your dedication to throwing a sharp hat has not gone unnoticed, and you deserve better from me going forward.”) How many emails were in this data set? Were there, in fact, multiple emails from people apologizing to their families and employees simultaneously for potentially derailing the world economy over their relationship with a foreign spy that it could study? Is this a widespread problem?

I understand that Donald Trump says of Huang that “he dealt with somebody, but you know, so does everybody else,” but—I actually don’t think this situation is that common! I’ve never come across it in my life. Or if there is a spy trying to infiltrate my marriage to obtain my trade secrets (you will claw my log-in verification codes from my cold, dead hands), that spy is not very good at his or her job. But maybe I’m out of the loop! Maybe people just live like this.

Now I am worried, based on Jensen Huang’s claim that this was “completely normal and not unusual” and an Nvidia spokesperson’s insistence that his interactions with the alleged spy were “not unusual” that, actually, we’ll be seeing multiple emails like this. Finally, organic demand for AI! If so, the template will get better and better—that is, if we can still use the chips.

The representative from Kentucky’s argument is that if Republicans like him don’t hold the secretary of defense accountable now for his various abuses of power, Democrats will in January. A vote in the House could come as soon as this evening.

Massie, who was ousted by a Trump-backed primary opponent earlier this year, introduced his articles of impeachment against the defense secretary yesterday. It is Massie’s second attempt, following an unsuccessful effort in September. That time around, House Speaker Mike Johnson sent lawmakers home for recess instead of allowing a vote. Senator Ted Cruz said yesterday on his podcast that Johnson’s move was in part motivated by the fact that Republicans “didn’t have the votes to stop a Hegseth impeachment.” This time could end differently: Although most Republicans are likely to close ranks behind the GOP defense secretary in the middle of a war, only a few defections would create a majority for impeachment.

The eight articles in Massie’s impeachment resolution focus primarily on four issues: the Iran war, the Pentagon’s campaign of boat strikes, Hegseth’s attempt to punish lawmakers for warning service members not to follow illegal orders, and the military operation in January to capture the Venezuelan strongman Nicolás Maduro. In each case, Massie’s charges say that Hegseth either violated the law or engaged in disqualifying conduct while carrying out President Trump’s agenda.

Congress has only ever impeached two Cabinet secretaries: Secretary of War William W. Belknap, in 1876, and President Biden’s homeland-security secretary, Alejandro Mayorkas, in 2024. The Senate did not convict in either case; conviction requires a two-thirds majority.

Hegseth already looked likely to be the third Cabinet member ever to face the proceeding, now that a new Democratic majority will arrive in Congress in January. Hakeem Jeffries, the presumed next speaker of the House, said on the campaign trail that Democrats were ready to run the Pentagon chief “out of town.” Jeffries posted on Sunday that, when it comes to impeachment, he’s a “Hard Yes.” Other Democrats have also come out as enthusiastic backers. Pat Ryan, a former Army intelligence officer from New York, posted on social media that Hegseth “better buckle up and lawyer up.” Trump, meanwhile, has affirmed his support for Hegseth, telling reporters last week that he “has done a fantastic job.”

Hegseth responded to the filing of the impeachment resolution at a Pentagon press conference today—his first in more than 180 days—arguing that any criticism of him was actually an attack on U.S. troops. “If they want to come for the warriors, they’re going to have to come through me,” he said. “What one disgruntled and defeated congressman is attempting is not oversight, and it’s not accountability. It’s a political spectacle, a personal manifesto driven by ego and resentment.” The impeachment effort, Hegseth said, was intended to “do damage to this president, this country, and, most irresponsibly, the warriors who defend it.” Hegseth added that every military operation mentioned in the Massie resolution had been approved by Trump, while emphasizing the president’s past impeachments and subsequent reelection. (Trump was impeached twice, in 2019 and 2021, by the Democratic-controlled House; although bipartisan majorities voted to convict him in the Senate, they fell short of the necessary two-thirds.)

Trump responded to the press conference with a vote of confidence in Hegseth. “Pete Hegseth gets an ‘A’ PLUS, PLUS, PLUS for his defense against a disgruntled, angry, defeated, former Congressman, Thomas Massie, a TOTAL LOSER!,” he posted to social media minutes after the event wrapped. In a separate post last night, he described Hegseth as “perhaps the most popular and successful military official in the Country.”

Massie has used the prospect of an incoming Democratic majority to argue for a vote on his resolution now. His resolution, he said on social media, “is a much gentler version” of what may come. The House has two legislative days to act on the measure. Its options include proceeding to a floor vote by simple majority, voting to dismiss the resolution, or voting to refer it to a House committee to conduct hearings and gather additional evidence.

Here’s what is in Massie’s articles of impeachment:

Articles I, II, and III

When the United States and Israel launched attacks on Iran in late February, Trump didn’t consult Congress. Lawmakers had not authorized the strikes or approved a declaration of war. The War Powers Resolution, a law passed in 1973, allows the commander in chief to bypass congressional authority under limited circumstances largely related to self-defense. But early administration claims of an imminent threat from Iran quickly fell apart. Even in those cases where a president can act unilaterally, the president is required to terminate hostilities within 60 days or certify to Congress that conflict is unavoidable to gain an extension of up to 30 days. The administration did neither. Its decision to go to war without congressional approval forms the basis of Massie’s first three reasons Hegseth should be impeached. (Hegseth maintained today that “history will judge the Iran war quite differently than conventional wisdom right now.”)

Article IV

The fourth article is also tied to Hegseth’s conduct in the Iran war, but includes a specific accusation: that Hegseth purposefully degraded the Department of Defense’s safeguards against killing civilians. In the first hours of the war, a U.S. strike killed about 170 people at a primary school in Minab; U.S. officials have said the tragedy was caused in part by outdated intelligence.

Hegseth has dismantled offices at the Pentagon that helped the military protect civilians during combat. Staffing at the Civilian Protection Center of Excellence, a Pentagon body created by law in 2023, was reduced by about 90 percent, our colleague Missy Ryan reported. Hegseth has called rules of engagement meant to limit accidental deaths “stupid” and advocated for the military to embody “maximum lethality, not tepid legality”—two quotes that Massie cites.

Massie also suggested that the deterioration of journalistic oversight weakened the public’s understanding of harm to civilians. Hegseth imposed new, restrictive rules on the Pentagon press corps and expelled from the building journalists who wouldn’t comply. (These included staff writers at The Atlantic.) His actions eroded the Pentagon’s systems for limiting “collateral damage to civilians,” Massie wrote, and Hegseth “effected the murder of civilians and abused the trust of the men and women under his command.”

At his press conference today, Hegseth said that “the only people who target civilians are the terrorists that we hunt, and I will dignify this claim no further.” Responding to criticism about the weakening of the Pentagon’s civilian-harm-mitigation efforts, a spokesperson had previously said that “the Department remains committed to minimizing civilian harm caused by military operations and to meeting all relevant statutory requirements.”

Article V

Massie argues in this provision that Hegseth used the power of his office “in a manner that resulted in the extrajudicial killings of at least 243 persons aboard seagoing vessels alleged to be smuggling narcotics.” The military’s strikes on small boats off the coast of Latin America began in September 2025. The most recent strike, late last month, killed five people. The Trump administration has claimed that the strikes targeted boats transporting drugs for cartels. But officials have not presented evidence to substantiate that. Transporting drugs is not a capital offense under U.S. law.

Hegseth “chilled independent legal review” in the Defense Department, Massie alleges, and relied on a “purported legal basis for the use of military force.” That refers to a memo from the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel that reportedly recasts cartel associates as legitimate targets in an “armed conflict.” (The memo has not been made public.) Many legal experts, including former military lawyers, have described the boat-strike campaign as a clear violation of both U.S. and international law. Nor does it appear to have achieved its stated purpose of reducing the flow of drugs into the U.S.; some evidence suggests that the flow of cocaine has actually increased over the past year. Hegseth has defended the campaign as part of an effort to secure “our Homeland from the drugs that are killing our people.” And the White House had told us previously that the boat strikes reflect how “President Trump is using every tool at his disposal to save lives from the scourge of illicit narcotics.”

Article VI

Senator Mark Kelly of Arizona appeared in a video in November 2025 along with five other Democratic members of Congress who had served in national-security roles before taking office. They reminded members of the military and the intelligence community of their obligation to “refuse illegal orders.” Their message was posted on social media after the Trump administration had deployed troops in several U.S. cities and conducted more than 20 lethal strikes in the drug-boats campaign.

Hegseth retaliated against Kelly with a formal letter of censure and directed the secretary of the Navy to review whether Kelly had engaged in “potentially unlawful” conduct. (Kelly served as a Navy pilot for more than two decades.) He has also threatened to reduce Kelly’s military-retirement rank and pay. The matter is being litigated in court. Massie accuses Hegseth of retaliation against a member of Congress for engaging in constitutionally protected speech and for exercising his responsibilities as a lawmaker.

Massie also argues that Hegseth ignored the separation of powers by placing the Department of Defense above the legislative and judicial branches. Hegseth, in turn, has accused Kelly of making “seditious” statements and said that he and the other lawmakers were “encouraging our warriors to ignore the orders of their Commanders,” which “sows doubt and confusion” and “only puts our warriors in danger.”

Article VII

Massie alleges that Hegseth overstepped his authority by using the Department of Defense to capture Maduro, the president of Venezuela, along with his wife, Cilia Flores. In the early hours of January 3, the U.S. military launched strikes across Venezuela and captured Maduro, transporting him to New York City, where he was indicted on multiple charges, including narcoterrorism. He awaits trial in a federal jail. Members of Congress, including senior lawmakers, learned about the seizure when the public did. The article claims that Hegseth violated his “constitutional duty to take care that the laws be faithfully executed,” because the U.S. had not declared war.

The Trump administration has argued that no congressional notice was required because the strikes and the seizure of Maduro were a law-enforcement operation, not an act of war. (Trump also said that he kept members in the dark because “Congress has a tendency to leak.”) Massie argues that the real purpose of the operation was to control Venezuelan oil reserves and that it amounted to an act of war because it led to Venezuelan regime change and significant casualties. About 80 people, including civilians, were killed.

Article VIII

The final article accuses Hegseth of responsibility for civilian casualties and another unlawful war—in this case, in Yemen. By initiating and directing Operation Rough Rider—months of strikes against the Houthi rebel group in the spring of 2025—Hegseth violated the War Powers Resolution, Massie claims. The strikes led to the deaths of an estimated 224 civilians and hundreds of Houthi fighters.

Massie argues that the operation was illegal because the administration bypassed required congressional authorization. He has an unusual primary source to reference: the Signal group chat in which Hegseth and other top administration officials coordinated the strikes—while unwittingly sharing their plans with the editor in chief of this magazine, Jeffrey Goldberg. In the chat, Vice President Vance wrote that “there is a strong argument” for delaying the operation by a month, and Joe Kent, then the acting chief of staff to the director of national intelligence, said, “There is nothing time sensitive driving the time line.” Hegseth himself at one point chimed in, saying that “we can easily pause” the plans. The administration has defended that operation as furthering American “national security and foreign policy interests.” In a letter to House Speaker Johnson in late March 2025, Trump wrote that Houthi militants had “perpetrated piratical aggressions” and continued to “threaten and attack United States forces” in and around Yemen, as well as “commercial vessels in one of the most important shipping lanes in the world.”
