Donald Trump has given the federal judiciary an ultimatum: Let him put his name on the Kennedy Center, or he won’t save it.

That threat came after a federal judge, Christopher Cooper, today blocked yet another effort to add the president’s name to the building. Moments later, Trump virtually joined a Kennedy Center board meeting, at which he slammed Cooper’s decision and lashed out at Representative Joyce Beatty—the ex officio board member challenging his overhaul of the institution in court—according to a person with knowledge of the meeting, who spoke with me on the condition of anonymity to discuss internal proceedings.

Later, Trump posted on Truth Social that the center’s “Renovation and Reconstruction, which is a very large and complex job, cannot begin until such time as the D.C. Circuit rules on the Board’s approved name,” adding, “If the ruling is a negative one, which it should not be, and is not overturned by the U.S. Supreme Court, the Reconstruction and the Renovation of The Kennedy Center will not take place.”

After months defined by empty theaters and canceled shows, the Kennedy Center has become a shadow of the premier performance venue it was just over a year ago. A Washington Post report this week revealed that the center’s finances are on the brink of collapse and that it could face bankruptcy. But while the institution has diminished, its leaders—and Trump’s allies on the board—have continued to push his plans forward, repackaging their oft-repeated claim that the only way to save it from financial ruin ahead of a two-year renovation is to emblazon it with Trump’s name. The crisis at the Kennedy Center is entangled in a larger effort by Trump to control what Washington looks like—a fight that has taken on higher stakes as his administration evades federal procedures designed to protect historic places from the whims of any one president.

The immediate outcome of today’s events adds more confusion. General trustees voted in favor of closing the center, and two staffers told me that leaders subsequently announced through the building’s broadcast system that the Kennedy Center was closed to the public. It was unclear if that announcement meant that employees would also be barred from the premises, according to one of the staffers, both of whom spoke with me on the condition of anonymity for fear of reprisal. Cooper issued a preliminary injunction in May to temporarily block the center from closing; if he does not lift or amend that ruling, the announced closure will put the board in violation of the court’s orders.

[Read: Trump’s Kennedy Center shakedown]

“The Board’s decision today reflects sound judgment and a clear prioritization of safety above all else,” the center’s vice president for public relations, Roma Daravi, told me in a statement. “As the main building closes for urgent renovations—long overdue and desperately needed to protect the safety of patrons, artists and staff—the Center remains committed to pursuing a safe and efficient renovation through all legal means.” The White House referred me to Trump’s post when I asked for comment on this story.

Meanwhile, the latest evidence of trouble at the Kennedy Center is scattered on the velvet carpet in the Grand Foyer, after a portion of its ceiling collapsed during a storm on September 4. The incident, which did not result in any injuries, has become the primary example for leaders describing the building’s urgent needs—and, as Daravi put it, debate whether “decades of neglect and deferred maintenance” are to blame. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick doubled down last night, posting security footage of the moment  the chunks of ceiling suddenly fell to the floor. “If a performance had been underway, patrons could have been killed,” he wrote. The rubble and the hole in the ceiling are still on full display, while signs, ropes, and a yellow cone caution people against entering the foyer.

The fallen ceiling has become a key image of the Kennedy Center’s crisis. Trump’s allies say it’s proof that the building’s troubles predate this administration, and can be resolved only by the appropriately recognized intervention of the president-as-master-builder. But to some who oppose his overhaul, the center’s loud publicization of the problem underscores the board’s eagerness—perhaps overeagerness—to shut down the building.

In a filing this week, Beatty’s team alleged that the center was citing the ceiling damage as justification for closure, but pointed out that a similar problem in 1977 was addressed without shutting down the center. (Beatty’s lawyers told the court that further discovery could show that mismanagement under Trump led to the institution’s insolvency.)

Either way, after the board’s latest vote to close, the next step is to wait and see if Cooper will allow it this time. It’s unclear if anything he heard today will persuade him—including a lengthy assessment of the building’s conditions that a real-estate firm prepared for the board—given his previous statement that the initial closure plans were “ill-informed and seemingly preordained.” But a crumbling ceiling might just do the trick: During the hearing today, Cooper indicated that he would consider amending his preliminary injunction to authorize closure for urgent circumstances.

[Read: What I saw inside the Kennedy Center]

The other emergency—the money—is even messier. The Washington Post reported on Sunday that Kennedy Center leaders are warning trustees of financial collapse within weeks, saying that the institution could soon be unable to make payroll or cover routine bills. The alarm follows an earlier Post report on internal financial records showing that the center could face a deficit of $23 million—even amid its drastic spending cuts this year.

To keep Trump’s plans for the center alive, the board has already appealed today’s ruling against adding his name to the facade, and Beatty revealed in a court filing to Cooper that leaders had been weighing 10 different ways in which Trump’s name could be added back to the center’s edifice. “The Board seeks to provide multiple options so that President Trump can be legally and appropriately recognized and acknowledged on the facade of the Kennedy Center so that he agrees to carry the burden of managing the renovation and provides the Center with a much required financial rescue,” a resolution that the board had prepared for this week read, though it was later dropped from the board’s agenda so trustees could focus exclusively on closure.

In his ruling today, Cooper reiterated the conclusion he had already reached four months ago. “Simply put, Defendants cannot install memorials for President Trump or anyone or anything else at the Kennedy Center without Congress’s blessing,” he wrote, adding that the latest renaming plan  “bucks a federal court order and a statute Congress enacted.”

Trump’s reaction today to Cooper’s decision—both online and before the board—underscores the bargain that has defined his stewardship of the Kennedy Center. Its leaders say that it is nearing financial and structural collapse, and that Trump is uniquely capable of raising the money and overseeing the work to turn the institution around. Today’s hearing brought up new questions about those claims, including what bankruptcy would even mean for a charitable trust organization created by Congress. If its finances are truly as bad as leaders and reports say, the court might eventually need to consider placing the center under the control of an independent receiver. For now, the venue is trapped between self-inflicted crises and the conditions that have been attached to solving them. But saving the Kennedy Center may ultimately require something its leadership has gone out of its way to resist: separating the future of the institution from the wishes of the president.

The Only Thing Capable of Uniting Bernie and Bannon

If there was any question over whether the concept of “the enemy of my enemy is my friend” is still operative in Washington’s hyper-partisan political environment, the answer may be found in the budding coalition of progressives and MAGA-friendly activists who have united in recent days to speak out...

If there was any question over whether the concept of “the enemy of my enemy is my friend” is still operative in Washington’s hyper-partisan political environment, the answer may be found in the budding coalition of progressives and MAGA-friendly activists who have united in recent days to speak out against artificial intelligence. Which is how Senator Bernie Sanders and Steve Bannon found themselves speaking on the same stage today.

With warnings that the technology could cause mass economic displacement and even human extinction, political figures who once wanted nothing to do with one another are suddenly joining forces, scrambling the political landscape just weeks before the midterm elections. The odd couples, who would have been hard to fathom just weeks ago, are also reshaping the legislative agenda. Republican Senators Ted Cruz and John Thune have teamed up with Democratic Senator Amy Klobuchar on an AI-safety bill; progressive Senator Richard Blumenthal joined forces with conservative Senator Josh Hawley to push a bill requiring AI companies to obtain approval from the Department of Energy before deploying new systems.

Nearly a decade ago, Sanders urged then-President-Elect Trump to reverse his decision to hire Bannon, a pugilistic architect of the MAGA movement, and called him “a racist individual.” Last year, Bannon called Sanders a “pussy” for not going after Hillary Clinton hard enough in 2016. But the two were able to put all of that aside today to deliver the same message: If AI has the potential to wipe out humanity, Americans should have a say in how it operates.  

[Read: Trump’s see-no-evil AI policy]

“If the lives of every man, woman, and child are going to be fundamentally changed by this technology, then the people of this country must make the decisions about AI, and not just a handful of oligarchs,” Sanders said at the all-day “Pro-Human Assembly.” Bannon sounded pretty much exactly the same. “The American citizens are not going to be supplicants to the oligarchs anymore,” the MAGA strategist said in what easily could have been a continuation of the Vermont socialist’s remarks. “We can’t do it.”

Standing decidedly outside of this spirit of cross-party cooperation is Trump, who has downplayed warnings about AI’s risks and called the concerns over the rapidly advancing technology a “hoax.” Trump made a surprise appearance at an AI-friendly event yesterday by placing a phone call to Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and making clear that he does not believe the doomsday warnings that have circulated in recent days. “The robots are not going to be taking over the world,” he told Huang, who took the call while speaking at a summit in Los Angeles and put it on speakerphone for the crowd to hear. “That’s not going to happen.”

Bannon and Sanders, speaking back-to-back, essentially agreed to disagree about Trump. Sanders said that Trump’s “ignorance regarding this issue is truly embarrassing” and suggested that the president is not taking the technology’s risks seriously enough.

“Obviously, he and I have a quite different opinion of the president of the United States,” Bannon told the crowd. “But we’re trying to raise above partisanship, and we have to.” That line received applause. But overriding Trump’s opposition to AI regulation in order to make meaningful policy changes could be a heavy lift. In several social-media posts yesterday, Trump dismissed those concerned about AI’s dangers as “Conspiracy Theorists, Treasonists, Traitors, and Leakers.” He suggested that AI and the data centers that power it could represent the greatest economic opportunity in American history. “Don’t kill the Golden Goose!” he wrote. Some lawmakers and activists, including Trump supporters and detractors, are now attempting to create a formal coalition to push for stronger guardrails around AI. Sanders plans to host a closed-door, bipartisan briefing on AI tomorrow in Washington.

Today’s event in Washington was branded by organizers as an opportunity to create a “more human-centric path for AI development.” Speakers, including lawmakers, labor officials, and faith leaders, leaned heavily on the idea that the battle to slow AI’s advance should transcend traditional partisan or ideological divides.  

[Ian Bogost: The thrill of wanting AI to destroy the world]

The event was organized by the Future of Life Institute, a nonprofit that was primarily funded by a donation of more than $650 million in 2021 from Vitalik Buterin, a wealthy cryptocurrency entrepreneur. (The donation was delivered in crypto.) Politico reported in 2024 that the small organization’s hefty war chest put it in the same league as organizational behemoths such as the American Civil Liberties Union Foundation and the Brookings Institution.

Today’s event featured free merch that included Carhartt shirts, yellow hoodies, and camouflage hats featuring mottos such as Keep the future human and Pro-Human. A video booth, fully decked out with stage lighting and a single chair, offered attendees the chance to answer the prompt When did you feel most human? Not, apparently, on video: When I walked by it a few times, it was not being used.

A parody news report on nearby television screens played a staged interview with “Dan X,” who appeared to be a stand-in for an AI executive. The CEO of a fictional company called Replacement.AI was described by the faux journalist as “a walking paradox” who “loves people but isn’t crazy about humans.” Wearing a Patagonia vest and a smartwatch, the Millennial-coded CEO ended the interview by offering this advice: “Live every day as if it is your last, because one day soon, very soon, it might be.”

Stuart Russell, a computer-science professor and the president of the International Association for Safe & Ethical AI, told the crowd that his private discussions with AI-company officials included even more dire predictions about the technology’s dangers than what they have discussed publicly. But organizers seemed eager to push the event beyond dystopian warnings and move toward solutions.

“There is no better testimony to how united we are than the mind-blowing ideological diversity that you’re going to see onstage today,” Max Tegmark, a co-founder and the chair of the Future of Life Institute, said at the assembly’s opening. In addition to lawmakers, the event featured clergy who spoke about AI’s impact on faith, parents whose teenage children had committed suicide after engaging with chatbots, and economists concerned about the mass displacement of the workforce. Attendees attempting to navigate the event website had to first agree to “Standards of Conduct” requiring participants to “practice active listening” and refrain from personal attacks.

Representative Chip Roy of Texas, who is retiring from Congress, was the only elected Republican to appear onstage. He told me that organizers had encouraged him to avoid politics in his remarks but that he predicted that political differences will inevitably intrude as the debate over what to do about AI continues. “I doubt Bernie’s solutions are going to be my solution,” he said.

Still, there were signs that figures on both sides of the aisle seemed ready to at least have an honest conversation. “I don’t think you could find any two harder partisans than Bernie Sanders on the left and Steve Bannon on the right,” Bannon said during his remarks. “This is a hinge in history. We have to handle this correctly. To handle it correctly, No. 1, we can never trust what an oligarch says.”

Sanders was by then out of sight, but on that much, he surely agreed.

The Next Change Trump Has Planned for the Smithsonian

Donald Trump has not forgotten his midsummer promise to publicly shame the Smithsonian. The Trump administration is currently preparing temporary signs that will accuse the National Museum of American History of ideological bias, according to a letter I obtained sent by Interior Secretary Doug Burgum. The September 3 letter, which Burgum sent to Senator Jeff Merkley, says that the National Park Service is planning to add about a dozen signs outside the museum at an estimated cost of $84,000. Merkley, an Oregon Democrat, and Senator Lisa Murkowski, an Alaska Republican, requested information about the initiative after Trump signed an executive order in July calling for the signs to be added.

A person familiar with the plans described some of the preparations to me. Although the Park Service has an idea of the physical structure of the signs, what the signs will actually say and who is writing the text remains a mystery to people in the agency working on them, said the person, who requested anonymity because they were not authorized to speak on the record. The letter from Burgum says that the signs are not finalized, but it offers a preview of what they might look like, describing a number of things “visitors should know” before they enter the museum. Burgum, whose department includes the Park Service, extensively quotes the museum’s director, Anthea Hartig, and criticizes outgoing Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch, seeking to frame both as ideologues. The letter also calls out exhibitions and materials that have touched on groups the administration has gone after, such as undocumented immigrants and transgender Americans.

“The informational signage therefore will provide visitors with important context about the exhibits they are about to encounter and the personal viewpoints that the curators of the exhibits are seeking to advance,” the letter says. It goes on to justify the move, echoing a 162-page report on the museum released by the White House and asserting that the museum is pushing “ideologies that distract from a coherent and accurate presentation of America’s story.” Burgum adds, “This may be the first instance in which executive branch intervention of this nature has become Necessary.”

The Smithsonian, which is partially federally funded, has historically operated independently, has its own governing body, and is regarded in the wider museum world as measured in its approach. The institution has been heading off threats and criticism from the Trump administration since March 2025. Recently, the administration’s attention has turned to the American-history museum, which Trump made a number of curatorial demands of on social media this past Friday, including insisting that it remove an abstract sculpture and erect a 30-foot-tall George Washington statue in its place. Although the Trump administration has accused the Smithsonian, and specifically the American-history museum, of not being patriotic enough, the museum’s leadership has pointed to the abundance of patriotic material on display inside—368 objects and 340 labels on the founding era; 54 George Washington mentions, according to Hartig—and has maintained that telling the American story in its full complexity is a crucial part of its role.

“Our work is governed by the Smithsonian’s standards of scholarship, accuracy, independence, and nonpartisanship,” Hartig told two House committees this summer. “The museum does not take sides in America’s political debates. We preserve and document the evidence of American life in all its breadth, so that the public can encounter the past and draw their own conclusions.”

Trump’s executive order calling for the displays also ordered the Park Service to direct visitors to White House–approved resources on history and include a temporary exhibit about the signers of the Declaration of Independence. The order was unprecedented, pitting two prominent American institutions against each other.

Before this presidency, few might have imagined a showdown between park rangers and history curators on the National Mall. But the White House has had to get creative in trying to control the Smithsonian. Unlike the Park Service, the museum complex is insulated from the executive branch’s whims. The signs will attempt to put a frame around the Smithsonian’s work before visitors even reach the door.

The Burgum letter suggests that the signs will be direct in their criticism of Smithsonian leadership and materials. It points to comments Hartig has made during her tenure on topics such as the scope of U.S. history, whether the museum should have an “America First” mentality, and the acknowledgment of Native American lands—and suggests that these comments are necessary information for museum visitors. The letter says, “A commitment to public transparency means that visitors should be informed about Director Hartig’s and other Smithsonian leaders’ history of racial focus” and quotes Hartig acknowledging white privilege in a November 2020 lecture during the height of the Black Lives Matter movement. The letter further asserts that visitors should be informed about “Secretary Lonnie Bunch’s history of invidious discrimination and anti-white animus,” quoting from a 2000 essay the secretary wrote discussing racial discrimination in the museum profession.

The letter goes on to call out a handful of exhibits mentioned in Trump’s report, including three that touched on transgender themes. It also mentions resources that discuss why educators should use terms such as undocumented immigrant instead of illegal alien.

After the announcement about the signs this summer, I was curious to know who might be working on the text for the displays and contacted some of the Trump administration’s go-to history experts. PragerU and Hillsdale College both helped create the Freedom Truck mobile museums touring the country this year as part of Trump’s Freedom 250 celebrations, but a PragerU spokesperson told me that the right-leaning edutainment nonprofit is not working on the Smithsonian signs, and a Hillsdale spokesperson told me that the conservative college is not “institutionally” involved. “When the College works with the administration on educational projects (the Story of America video series, for instance), we’re glad to say so,” a spokesperson wrote to me in an email.

Mike Gonzalez, a senior fellow at the Heritage Foundation who was called by Republicans to testify about the Smithsonian at the hearings this summer, declined to comment on any involvement, writing to me that “any conversation I have with any level of government, from local dog catcher to the top of the federal level is private and confidential. Otherwise, why would they trust me?” He added, “And, no, you shouldn’t take that as a confirmation of anything.” He told me by phone that he thinks the signs are clever and that “at best, it’s a medium-term solution or a short-term solution.”

Even under pressure, the Smithsonian has continued to tell the American story. On a recent afternoon at the National Museum of American History, staff sat in a small room behind tables dotted with objects from 9/11 and spoke directly with visitors. Some museumgoers left the exhibit moved to tears, staff told me.There was no descriptive wall text, no glass, just the museum in its purest form—a conversation between historians and the public.

Gasoline prices have been up all summer, as have jet-fuel prices, as have home-heating-oil prices—but nothing has jumped quite like diesel. The average price for a gallon of diesel in the United States just hit an all-time high of $6.27, almost double what it was in late February. It’s no secret that the Iran war is largely responsible for America’s energy woes. So far, Donald Trump’s strategy has been to downplay these increases as temporary side effects (a “little glitch,” he said in March) and remind everyone that oil prices were higher under Joe Biden (which is true). Lately, though, the president has been telling us to look away from Iran and toward a different war.

Over the weekend, he called on Ukraine’s president to stop striking Russian oil refineries, which he said was “causing a shortage of diesel.” And yesterday morning, he announced a partial energy cease-fire between the two countries, claiming that the “World’s Diesel price rise is mostly caused by the Russia/Ukraine War, not Iran.” (Although both Ukraine and Russia have signaled that they’d be open to such an agreement, they continued attacking each other last night.) Focusing on Eastern Europe may be a convenient distraction tactic, but the reality is that this war is ultimately less important for U.S. diesel than the one that Trump is waging in the Middle East.

Diesel’s rally has to do with how it’s distilled. Diesel, like gasoline, primarily comes from crude oil, which has been in short supply since the Strait of Hormuz was effectively shut down in February. Middle Eastern crude, which is sludgy and sulphurous, is particularly conducive to producing diesel. Making matters worse, many local refineries have been attacked or have preemptively gone offline, leaving the region less equipped to handle the crude that remains. Yemen’s Houthis attacked a major Saudi facility last week as a prelude to a broader offensive.

The refinery problem is global. At their summer peak, the world’s facilities were producing 4.2 million fewer barrels a day than they were last year. As Trump has pointed out, Ukrainian attacks on refineries in Russia—usually the world’s second-largest diesel exporter—have only compounded the issue. With Moscow maintaining its ban on diesel exports (meant to protect domestic supply), more countries are turning to the U.S. to make up the difference. American plants are now “running all-out,” per the American Petroleum Institute, using almost all of their processing power, and the U.S. is exporting more refined-oil products on average than it was last year. U.S. refineries typically shut down for at least a couple of weeks for maintenance around this time of year, too, which could potentially squeeze supply even more.

Still, Matt Smith, the director of commodity research at the data firm Kpler, told me that Ukrainian aggression “isn’t equally to blame, let alone the leading factor,” in America’s rising diesel prices. Even if Russia and Ukraine agreed to a cease-fire today, global refining capacity wouldn’t magically return right away. As I wrote in May, damaged facilities take time to repair and come back online once they’ve been shut down. Gregory Brew, an energy historian and a senior analyst at Eurasia Group, told me that for any détente to have an effect on diesel prices, “it would likely need to last several weeks at least.” Meanwhile, the situation in the Middle East remains unstable. The Saudi Arabian government recently shut down a crucial pipeline that was damaged in a strike by Iran-aligned militias in Iraq (the pipeline had been moving 4 to 5 percent of the global oil supply—several million barrels a day). Combine these risks with elevated seasonal demand for diesel, which in the U.S. tends to peak around the harvest season, and it’s clear that the European conflict is only one force among many behind this price spike.

Americans are feeling the effects. One gas station in San Diego has been selling diesel for $9.99 a gallon, which is as high as its three-digit physical display will permit. Groceries may become more expensive because farm equipment and refrigerated 18-wheelers tend to run on diesel. Trains predominantly use diesel, too, and railroads’ fuel surcharges on grain shipments have more than doubled over the past year. Nearly 5 million homes could be paying more for heating oil (which is chemically similar to diesel and subject to some of the same economic pressures) as well.

As everyday costs continue to climb, voters are growing more and more dissatisfied with Trump’s handling of the economy. And over the past few months, the president has been looking for someone—anyone—to share the blame. A speedy Trump-negotiated end to the Ukraine war is unlikely for many reasons. But even in the fantasy world where he pulls that off, it wouldn’t save him from a diesel spike of his own creation.

When I was 13, not long after my father had committed suicide, I asked my mother, “What’s the Oedipus complex?”

Maybe. When he disappeared on December 6, 1972, leaving my mother, my sister, and me, I was troubled by a sense of profound relief. Congress Might Be About to Hand Trump Sweeping Tariff Powers

The Lindsey Graham Sanctioning Russia Act, which the House will likely vote on this week, looks like a no-brainer for supporters of Ukraine who have been calling for President Trump to get tougher on Russia.

A large majority of America’s existing sanctions on Russia, which target thousands of government officials and companies, are currently enforced by executive order, meaning that the president can remove them whenever he wants. The Graham bill would turn those sanctions into law: If Trump wanted to end them, he would need Congress’s approval. It also targets a few Russian officials and companies by name, further reducing Trump’s discretion. These provisions help explain why the late Senator Graham, one of Ukraine’s strongest defenders in Congress, originally championed the bill. President Volodymyr Zelensky himself has urged legislators to pass it, as have more than 30 national-security and foreign-policy experts who argue that it’s in Kyiv’s best interest.

But the bill is not what it seems. The legislation would impose no significant new sanctions on Russia, and it would allow Trump to waive any of its measures simply by claiming that doing so is in the national interest. In other words, a law that appears to handcuff Trump would offer him a key to free himself. What’s more, its most important provisions are about not sanctions per se, but tariffs. The bill could provide Trump with the same kind of sweeping authority to impose tariffs that the Supreme Court took away from him in February, allowing him to intensify his trade war with many of America’s allies and partners.

The legislation would give the president authority to impose levies of up to 100 percent on the five largest importers of Russian crude oil and the five largest importers of Russian natural gas, which include China, India, France, Japan, Hungary, and Slovakia. It also allows him to impose tariffs on the five countries that are deemed to be the biggest facilitators of Russia’s evasion of oil sanctions—a vague provision largely subject to presidential discretion. (There are a few carve-outs for the natural-gas category, but not for the others.)

[Read: Russia’s real strategy in Europe]

Peter Harrell, a trade lawyer who served in the Biden administration, told me that the bill “gives Trump much more flexibility on tariffs than traditional tariff laws. There are no guardrails or restrictions.” The simplest reading of the legislation suggests that Trump can impose tariffs on only the countries that appear in the various top-five lists. But Harrell noted that Trump’s second-term track record suggests that he might try to interpret the law more expansively, perhaps by treating the European Union as a single country and imposing tariffs on the entire bloc all at once. Harrell also warned that the administration might use the law as a pretext to impose tariffs on countries that have little or nothing to do with Russia evading sanctions.

It wouldn’t be the first time that Trump has employed such a pretext. Consider his 25 percent tariff on India, which the Supreme Court has since struck down. The levy was ostensibly meant to punish the country for importing oil from Russia. But reporting suggests that the decision had more to do with Trump’s annoyance that Prime Minister Narendra Modi didn’t give him credit for the cease-fire that India and Pakistan signed last year.

Going beyond the letter of the Graham bill could well result in litigation for the administration. But Trump would still have ample reason to take an aggressive approach. For one thing, the legal process takes a long time—most trade lawyers think that the February ruling came at warp speed, even though it was a year in the making. While the case was moving through the courts, they consistently allowed the Trump administration to continue collecting tariff revenue in the meantime. In all likelihood, Trump would be free to use the tariff tool expansively for most of the rest of his term.

Trump is evidently less worried about Russia evading sanctions than he is about oil prices. At the start of the Iran war, he waived some sanctions on Russian oil in an attempt to keep prices from going up. There is little reason to think that he will act more forcefully against Russian energy now, because oil prices remain high and the midterms are just a month away. Indeed, he has failed to update and enforce sanctions that are already on the books, despite bipartisan support. Moreover, Trump recently sent Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff to Moscow to dangle the prospect of sanctions relief in exchange for some modest steps toward deescalating the war.

[Read: The warrior-witches of Ukraine’s resistance]

Still, supporters of the bill believe that the benefits outweigh the risks. Eric Ciaramella, a senior fellow at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace who served on the National Security Council as an adviser on Ukraine policy in the first Trump administration, told me, “If Trump wanted to hurt Europe, he has lots of ways to do that already. This is just a marginal increase in that risk, and it adds some political pressure on him to get tougher on Russia.” Whether such pressure would get Trump to change, however, is another question.

Perhaps the strongest argument in favor of the bill is simply that the Ukrainians want it. The legislation would no doubt serve as a symbol of Congress’s support. But Ukraine needs material, not symbolic, support—particularly weapons such as air-defense interceptors and greater economic assistance to survive the winter. Trump has the power to provide both, but he has chosen not to. Turning the Graham bill into law isn’t likely to give him a sudden change of heart.

Yesterday, Democrats tried but failed to introduce amendments that would exempt the EU from being treated as a country, tighten the national-security waiver, and scrap the broad allowances for tariffs on countries other than Russia. Without those measures, the bill amounts to little more than a permission slip for Trump to restart his trade war with America’s allies and partners. That’s not a price worth paying just to give Ukraine a symbolic victory.
