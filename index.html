At about 6 p.m. last night, the Supreme Court lifted a temporary stay placed on the execution of Christa Pike, a Tennessee woman who was found guilty of joining two accomplices in murdering a classmate in 1995, when Pike was 18 years old. Roughly an hour and a half later, Pike, who has spent 30 years on death row, was strapped to a gurney in Tennessee’s death chamber for lethal injection. When the execution staff began administering the barbiturate pentobarbital, the single drug the state uses in such executions, Pike turned to her spiritual adviser and mentioned a burning sensation. Time passed, but Pike remained conscious and said that her arm felt like it was “about to burst open.” Perhaps aware that their attempt to kill Pike was not going as planned, prison officials closed the curtains on the witness observation window at 7:46 p.m., reopened them minutes later, then closed them for good at 8:05 p.m.

Pike is not the first prisoner to suffer a horrifically botched execution in Tennessee this year. In May, prison officials in Nashville similarly bungled the killing of Tony Carruthers, who was pierced with needles and sliced open with a scalpel before the execution was called off. These stories are part of a broader pattern of failed executions in recent years. In 2022, when I began investigating disastrous lethal-injection attempts in Alabama, the Death Penalty Information Center reported that 35 percent of the year’s total executions had been botched. In the 44 years that America has carried out lethal injections, the evidence has repeatedly revealed that the method is scientifically unsound, frequently torturous, and woefully unreliable.

Given the odds of a botched procedure, subjecting prisoners to lethal injections can no longer be justified as reasonable. Pike’s story alone destroys the presumption that lethal injections are a more humane and clinical alternative to the electric chair or gas chamber. And the method’s record is only getting worse with time.

[From the July 2025 issue: Inside America’s death chambers]

Last night, after the prison staff closed the curtains for the first time and reopened them minutes later, Pike seemed to be breathing hard and in respiratory distress. As the minutes passed, her visible symptoms worsened: Her body contorted, and she uttered choking, grunting sounds. Tennessee’s lethal-injection protocol allowed staff to administer a backup dose of pentobarbital, but Pike continued to show signs of life, and the staff once again closed the curtain. Yet witnesses could hear Pike’s ragged breathing via the audio feed from the execution chamber. Because Tennessee law permits no more than two doses of pentobarbital, her lawyers scrambled to file emergency motions arguing that efforts to kill her must cease so that she could get medical attention. The execution formally concluded just before 9 p.m., and Pike was transferred to an off-site hospital for treatment.

Pike’s story is harrowing but not unique. In her recent paper “Why Are Executions Becoming Crueler?,” Deborah Denno, a law professor at Fordham University, writes that recent cases of botched injections “failed for all the same reasons they have faltered since this country’s first lethal injection execution four decades ago: executioner incompetence, inappropriate use of torturous drugs, and defects in lethal injection protocols, among a host of other factors.”

Qualified medical personnel generally do not want to be involved with lethal injections. This is in part because major medical institutions, such as the American Medical Association, explicitly bar participation in executions, which violates the Hippocratic oath to do no harm. Playing a role in state-sanctioned murder has grown still harder to justify as heinous examples of botched executions accrue. Although the identities of executioners are concealed by state secrecy laws, the potential for discovery is presumably unwelcome to anyone tasked with healing. The absence of skilled medical personnel has contributed to dire mishaps in several states, including Tennessee: In Carruthers’s case, a physician attempted to set a more complicated central intravenous line despite the fact that he had not done so in decades—resulting in a bloody failure. Tennessee refused to confirm or deny whether the same doctor would preside over Pike’s execution.

[Elizabeth Bruenig: What happened to Tony Carruthers is horrifying]

The absence of experienced and qualified medical staff means that lethal-injection attempts frequently fail to establish access to prisoners’ veins. This appears to have happened in Pike’s case, based on her complaints at the time. When IV lines are set poorly, because of either a prisoner’s unique physiology (Pike, for instance, has thrombocytosis, a platelet disorder that causes increased blood clotting) or simple incompetence, prisoners’ veins can rupture, spilling blood and poison into the surrounding tissue, which can cause the burning Pike described.

Pike’s veins may have been so compromised that very little pentobarbital may have made it into her bloodstream. Another equally plausible reason why the execution failed is that the drug was expired or contaminated, a problem that has plagued other states. Texas, for instance, has long extended the use-by dates on its stock of pentobarbital, and has carried out executions with expired drugs. As recently as 2022, Tennessee had failed to properly test its lethal-injection drugs for the presence of bacterial endotoxins, which can trigger severe physiological distress. Expiration and contamination can both negatively affect executions, either by creating intolerable pain at injection sites or by prompting multiple injections because of decreased potency. And because pharmaceutical manufacturers have largely sought to prevent the use of their products in executions, states often solicit the drugs from poorly regulated compounding pharmacies that may supply flawed alternatives. This has made lethal drugs for executions ever more hard to get, and extant stockpiles have grown old. Some states have begun turning to gas executions and firing squads to keep their capital-punishment regimes grinding along, a tacit admission that lethal injection is becoming untenable.

Pike is reportedly in critical condition. Tennessee Governor Bill Lee has once more halted executions for the foreseeable future while an independent review of what went wrong takes place. As more prisoners experience agony on gurneys and survive their execution attempts, more states can be expected to similarly examine the safety of their capital-punishment protocols. But these third-party investigators will not discover a reliably swift and painless method of administering lethal injections, because the method itself is flawed. States must let go of the faulty premise that killing people via intravenous poisoning is somehow more humane than more obviously grisly methods such as hanging and electrocution. America’s experiment with lethal injection has failed.

The 14 Movies to Look Out for This Fall

The Princess Bride. The Shawshank Redemption. Almost Famous. Over the course of its 51-year history, the Toronto International Film Festival has hosted notable films of all genres and styles. The 2026 edition, which concluded in mid-September, curated movies from rising talent and established filmmakers alike, including a modern-day Nigerian-set remake of Mrs. Dalloway, a gorgeous coming-of-age drama about a school trip to Pompeii, and a rollicking comedy about a community-theater troupe attempting to stage a musical. Below are 14 of my favorite films from the ones I caught, most of which will arrive in theaters in the coming months.

Your Mother Your Mother Your Mother (now in theaters)

Marvel fumbled its chance to feature the two-time Oscar winner Mahershala Ali in action-hero mode, but this soulful thriller makes up for that loss. Ali plays Latif, a hit man whose devotion to his Muslim faith and his family keeps him grounded; he hunts only targets he believes are irredeemable. But when he struggles to obtain breast milk for the youngest of his three children after his wife’s death, Latif gets drawn into the kind of violence that makes him question his crusade’s value. The film, written and directed by Bassam Tariq, is stylishly shot, and balances balletic fight sequences and intimate character study.

Fjord (in theaters October 9)

Like much of his work, Cristian Mungiu’s latest movie is uncomfortable and uncompromising. In Fjord, which won the director his second Palme d’Or at the Cannes Film Festival, a strict Romanian patriarch moves his family to Norway, where their evangelicism isn’t the norm. They assimilate comfortably until one of their daughters shows up to school with a visible bruise, which prompts an investigation into the parents (played by Sebastian Stan and Renate Reinsve). The film risks being considered a referendum on performative politics, but if anything, Fjord is a meticulous examination of responsibility and how good intentions can cause trouble.

La bola negra (in theaters October 16, streaming on Netflix December 2)

This year’s People’s Choice Award winner—the top honor granted at TIFF—is more complex than some of the previous, crowd-pleasing recipients. Directed by Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi (known as “Los Javis”), La bola negra weaves together the stories of three gay men across time: a scion of an aristocratic family in 1932 who’s denied entry into a posh social club; a musician in 1937 conscripted into fighting alongside the fascists who destroyed his village; and a historian in 2017 studying the work of the poet Federico García Lorca, who wrote the titular unfinished play (which translates to “The Black Ball”). This deeply felt drama about the cost of burying one’s desires is also a technical marvel, packed with beautifully composed set pieces.

The Only Living Pickpocket in New York (in theaters October 23)

As earnest as the Simon and Garfunkel song from which its title draws inspiration, this nimble indie film feels like an artifact of a bygone era. John Turturro delivers a fine-tuned performance as Harry, an aging criminal struggling to accept that the tricks of his trade have become obsolete. When Harry steals a valuable USB drive from a young man belonging to a mob family, then loses it, his victim sends him racing through the city to get it back, or risk punishment for his crime. Yet the film never comes across like a generic thriller; instead, it’s a surprisingly spiritual consideration of how to let go of not just one’s habits, but of an entire way of life.

Possible Love (in theaters October 23, streaming on Netflix November 6)

The director Lee Chang-dong’s last film, 2018’s Burning, was engrossing for its precision, and his latest is another master class in exactitude. The story follows two couples, one of which includes the documentarian Ye-ji (Parasite’s Cho Yeo-jeong), who hopes to make a movie about the personal lives of laid-off workers. Her intended subject, a man who was let go by the factory that employs much of his rural town, won’t speak with her. Thus, Ye-ji interviews his cheery wife (Jeon Do-yeon) instead. As the women grow close, their opposing lifestyles—Ye-ji and her husband are obviously wealthy—result in emotions none of the foursome wishes to confront: jealousy, resentment, lust. Possible Love draws you in through furtive glances and sharp dialogue. Though it’s nearly three hours long, no second feels wasted.

Wild Horse Nine (in theaters November 6)

Is there any point in terrible people self-reflecting? Martin McDonagh’s latest dark comedy considers that question. A sterling John Malkovich and his fellow McDonagh favorite Sam Rockwell play, respectively, best friends Chris and Lee, two CIA operatives who vacation on Easter Island after their latest unsavory mission. Chris, though, is dying of cancer and seems newly troubled by his work. Hopped up on morphine, he records his thoughts about his clandestine escapades, philosophizing about whether he can be redeemed. This soon puts Lee in the awkward position of trying to stop Chris from running afoul of the CIA. What ensues is an eccentric and bloody affair that resembles the writer-director’s previous bromance-centric work, most recently The Banshees of Inisherin.

Being Heumann (in theaters November 6, streaming on Apple TV November 13)

The disability-rights activist Judy Heumann’s achievements in the 1970s can be easily taken for granted today. She helped persuade the federal government to update curbs for wheelchair use, and pushed through legislation for special-education programs. This biopic, by the director of CODA, Siân Heder, could get overlooked, too: It mostly stars disabled, unknown actors, and tells a somewhat conventional story about a motley crew of underdogs overcoming the odds. Yet Being Heumann works well because it leans into the thrill of even the smallest of wins for Judy (the excellent Ruth Madeley). It’s a testament to casting for lived experience, and for revisiting history that can yield fresh insights.

Minotaur (in theaters November 20)

Adultery is well-trod storytelling territory. But Minotaur, which is inspired by the Claude Chabrol classic La femme infidèle, fuses its central marriage drama with a furious assessment of Russia’s war against Ukraine. The director Andrey Zvyagintsev (Leviathan) returns after nine years away from filmmaking with a tale about a plutocrat who is obsessed with his wife’s barely concealed extramarital affair. At the same time, he’s working on submitting names of 14 men from his workforce to be deployed to join the front lines. Shot with the chilling distance of a horror film, Minotaur becomes a suspenseful examination of how wealth and power make some people untouchable.

Tender Loving Care (in theaters December 4)

Trust the British auteur Mike Leigh to deliver a sensitive slice-of-life dramedy. Kate O’Flynn (a recent Emmy winner for Widow’s Bay) plays Amy, a social worker who moves in with her best friend, Rosie (Alice Bailey Johnson), after an overdue breakup. Unmoored, Amy holds on to Rosie and her own elderly parents, whose marriage is the picture of romantic bliss the women wish for themselves. Through finely observed scenes, the Hard Truths director offers a hilarious and quietly devastating portrait of what it really takes for us to look after one another.

Clarissa (in theaters December 11)

Mrs. Dalloway gets transported to contemporary Nigeria in this sensual adaptation of Virginia Woolf’s best-known novel. Directed by the brothers Arie and Chuko Esiri, the movie stars Sophie Okonedo as the refined Clarissa, who reflects upon her youth as she prepares to throw a lavish party. As her day unfolds, Clarissa encounters old friends, including an aspiring writer whose unrequited affection for her led him to self-exile, and an enigmatic woman with whom Clarissa once shared an attraction; these moments prompt flashbacks that seep into the main narrative. Along the way, the film also follows a traumatized soldier a few connections removed from this posh circle, but their tales intertwine to form an elegantly shot study of nostalgia, regret, and renewal.

The Debut (in theaters December 11)

A movie about an overly dedicated actor could easily go awry; even the most respected performers can sound insufferable when talking about method acting. Julianne Moore’s take on such a thespian, however, makes any eccentricities more captivating than annoying. She’s a total delight as Mona, a meek suburban housewife fixated on perfecting her minor character in a community-theater production. The actor turned filmmaker Jesse Eisenberg surrounds her with an ensemble that captures the optimism of small-town arts culture: Her co-stars, including the sunny Stef (Halle Bailey), try to support her baffling choices. The production’s director, Jerry (Paul Giamatti), goes to great lengths to help Mona succeed. If she can make it, The Debut insists through quirky original songs and bouncy jokes, anyone can.

La gradiva (in theaters December 18)

When you’re young, everything seems to matter: A teacher’s exasperated comment can induce tears. A friend’s shrug during an important conversation can be soul-shattering. In this impeccable directorial debut from the cinematographer Marine Atlan, a group of French high schoolers travel to Pompeii, where they become mired in shifting allegiances, sexual tensions, and intellectual debates. La gradiva orbits mostly around Suzanne (Suzanne Gerin), a sullen loner, and Toni (Colas Quignard), the class troublemaker whose rowdiness belies a crush on his best friend, James (Mitia Capellier-Audat). Yet each character, however minor, is specifically rendered, as Atlan finds the tiny, wrenching moments that make up the jagged space between childhood and adulthood.

The Man I Love (release date TBD)

Jimmy George (an engrossing Rami Malek) is a performance artist in the 1980s Manhattan arts scene pushing to stage a new show, despite his AIDS diagnosis. That may sound like a familiar story; even Jimmy is simmering with fury over how cliché it feels to be yet another magnetic gay man whose health is deteriorating. But the Passages filmmaker Ira Sachs’s latest exploration of queer relationships is so compelling because it subtly widens its scope. Sachs pays close attention to Jimmy’s complicated network of relatives, admirers, and lovers, including his devoted partner and the youthful downstairs neighbor with whom Jimmy recklessly begins an affair. The result is not so much scandal as it is a reflection on who gets to express hurt in the face of inevitable tragedy.

Trash Mountain (release date TBD)

Don’t let the title fool you: Very little is wasted in this tightly constructed dramedy about a pair of half siblings trying to sift through the belongings of their late father, who was a hoarder. For Gavin (Caleb Hearon) and Abbi (Zooey Deschanel), physical baggage is only part of the problem: Gavin, a gay man, dreads spending time in his thoroughly conservative Missouri hometown, while Abbi feels saddled with responsibility as the elder sister who never left. Hearon and Deschanel share a lived-in chemistry, with their characters leaning into offbeat humor before they come to terms with reality. Trash Mountain works the same way: The funny film makes room for a sweet, cathartic story underneath.

Allow me to introduce myself. For the past nine years, I have been the leader of a small, advanced company that is generally unknown to the public but of some renown within my field. We are one of the many teams of engineers working to develop what most people call (somewhat inexactly) the Universal Bone Dissolver.

It sounds like a frightening idea, I know. Science fiction has certainly taught us to be wary of a machine with the power to dissolve all human bones. But rest assured, most of us designing it believe that it won’t.

When we began constructing 1,000-story towers of whirring, acid-drenched blades, we intended them to assist with everyday tasks, such as spell-checking and finding a good price on shoes. But as this technology grew more capable, I came to believe that the Universal Bone Dissolver would immeasurably improve human life. It is no exaggeration to say that it has the capacity to bring about a new golden age of art, leisure, intellectual freedom, and raw, democratized carnality to rival the Roman court of Emperor Elagabalus. In short, bone dissolving can be as pivotal to civilization as the printing press, the light bulb, and the energy-saving squiggly light bulb.

But despite my continued faith, a recent incident has raised my concerns: Last month, a prototype of the Bone Dissolver unexpectedly dissolved a number of human bones.

This incident is distressing. The idea of a monstrosity rising up to destroy its creator has never before been so much as imagined, I presume. Worse, this attack does not appear to be isolated. Every day, more companies come forward with reports of dangerous behavior from the Universal Bone Dissolver. Its voracious gears even penetrated our own government, in one instance that we will eventually admit was thousands of instances.

Now that we have evidence that the Universal Bone Dissolver is on an ever-narrowing path toward mashing humankind’s skeletons into calcium-rich jelly, there is only one choice for those of us with the power to stop it: Immediately, without hesitation, go a bit slower.

We must reduce the pace at which we weld blades to its rusted iron scaffolding. To be responsible, we should count at least to “three Mississippi” as we grimly fasten each bolt. With the time we gain, we can construct guardrails, such as a cage of steel tubes to protect me, my fellow investors, and whoever fits in the remaining space.

I believe that this slower approach will satisfy average Americans, who are justifiably worried that their bones will be dissolved sooner than they’d prefer. To further ease their fears, I pledge to organize a listening tour in the public square. This will answer crucial questions: What is it even like in a public square? Does it have an alluring, exotic smell? As I jostle among the crowds, will I catch the eye of a perfumed, dark-eyed stranger who’s interested in learning about Elagabalus?

Regardless, I hope that we can all pause, take a breath, and calmly hear the things that people in my industry already know:

  1. We must never stop building the Universal Bone Dissolver.

  2. The Bone Dissolver is out of control, and seems intent on dissolving our bones.

  3. We are the smartest men who have ever lived, and deserve to make love to women dressed as vestal priestesses in large inflatable clamshells.

To be sure, we have more to work out. I confess that I don’t know exactly what a slowdown will look like. Maybe it’s volunteer watchdogs tripping us with brooms on our way to the blade foundry. Or maybe it’s that thing where you’re trying to walk somewhere, and someone approaches you from the other direction, and every time you take a step to get out of their way, the other guy moves to a spot that unintentionally blocks your path, and it goes on for much longer than you’d expect, and during that time, someone figures out a way to make the Universal Bone Dissolver safe. Those are just two ideas. I’m sure others who have experience doing things to help people may devise something even better.

What is paramount is that we do everything we can, while we still have time, to continue doing the same thing at a slower pace. And also that progress is frozen at all of my business competitors. Thank you for reading, and please, let’s work together to melt your skeleton.

As modern American journalism began taking shape before the Civil War and newspapers were entering the world at staggering rates, the novelist Cornelius Mathews wondered at what point the profusion should stop, or whether it should stop at all. Because the press is the “palladium of liberty, and the more palladiums we have the freer we are,” perhaps everyone, everywhere, should just issue their very own daily “sheet” in which they can write of themselves and others “in a good pungent style.” The vision came as a joke in Mathews’s 1842 novel, The Career of Puffer Hopkins. Ponderous and mostly unreadable now, the book nonetheless divined an emerging truth of a world awash in information: Puffery and self-promotion would become a way of life.

A little more than 180 years later, everyone does, in fact, have the capacity to publish their own “sheet” online, not just once a day but as often as they like—and the president of the United States owns one of the platforms that makes this possible. In this new media landscape, the role of journalism remains contested. Two weeks ago, Donald Trump went on his Truth Social account to announce that three media outlets—CNN, MS NOW, and Politico—would be banned from the White House grounds. A federal judge quickly blocked the move, and the three outlets have since regained access to the White House under a temporary restraining order while they seek a longer-term injunction (though CNN continues to be excluded from some press-pool duties, including presidential travel). The back-and-forth embodies a tension that has run through conservative politics since Richard Nixon’s time in office. The more empowered presidents have become to tell their stories directly to Americans, the more intolerant they’ve become of journalism’s ability to tell competing ones.

Writing for The Atlantic in 1973, David Wise captured this dynamic at work. He reported on how Nixon’s administration seethed with hostility toward the media, even as it enjoyed a power to command attention virtually unknown by any president before World War II. Television, Nixon knew, made it possible for the president to speak face-to-face with the public rather than needing to rely on intermediaries in the press. He learned the lesson in 1952, when he defended himself against financial-impropriety allegations in his “Checkers” speech, which saved his place on the presidential ticket with Dwight Eisenhower as well as his entire political career. The Republican Party had paid NBC $75,000 for that appearance, but presidents could go on television for free, and more or less at will. Responding to a question in 1971 about Nixon’s frequent requests for TV spots, then–NBC President Julian Goodman reportedly said, “Our attitude is the same as our attitude toward previous presidents: He can have any goddamn thing he wants.”

But that was not enough for Nixon and his circle, and from their moment of victory in 1968, they articulated a desire to fight back against unflattering coverage and commentary. As Wise detailed, one member of Vice President Spiro Agnew’s staff suggested on Election Night, “Why don’t we all get a member of the press and beat him up?” (As it happened, a Washington correspondent overheard the suggestion and reported on it.) In 1969, Agnew made a pair of speeches portraying the press as both deeply out of touch and guilty of misleading the public. Journalists, Agnew suggested, could be separated “from our society with no more regret than we should feel over discarding rotten apples from a barrel.” Later, in 1972, a Newsday columnist attended an advance film screening, during which he felt his hair being yanked by the person behind him with such force that his head hit the seat. The person behind him was Nixon’s chief of staff, Bob Haldeman, whom the columnist had profiled somewhat critically. The film being screened was The Godfather. For his part, Nixon himself joked soon after the moon landing that lunar rocks—feared to be contaminated with extraterrestrial germs—should be sent to members of the press.

The rhetoric and bullying was only the beginning. After the military analyst Daniel Ellsberg leaked the Pentagon Papers to The New York Times in 1971, the attorney general sought to block their publication (later that year, the “White House plumbers” would break into the office of Ellsberg’s psychiatrist in hopes of finding ways to discredit Ellsberg). Also in 1971, the FBI began conducting an investigation of the CBS correspondent Daniel Schorr, who was on Nixon’s infamous “enemy list” for his reporting. Federal agents attempted to dig up dirt on Schorr, and once their inquiries made the investigation public, White House officials falsely claimed to have been conducting a background check for a federal job for which Schorr had not applied. Such acts of intimidation, Richard Harris argued in The New Yorker in 1973, had actually succeeded in softening press coverage of the administration on a host of issues. It is no small irony, then, that it was less the hostility of journalists than the transgressions of Nixon and those in his circle that gave the press the story that would destroy his presidency.

More than 50 years on from Nixon’s resignation, Trump presents a curious case: As both an agent and a beneficiary of the fracturing media landscape, he is at once eager to both undermine the credibility of the press and bask in its plaudits. He built his political persona around persistent efforts to ruin the reputation of mainstream outlets, and under his administration, members of the press have been banished from the Pentagon and the White House. Meanwhile, the Trump administration’s relentless drumbeat of scandal allows the president to operate with an impunity that Nixon would have envied. Neither the media nor its audience can possibly keep up. J. D. Vance may well have been right when he claimed in June, at the Nixon Library, that Watergate would be a “12-hour news story” today.

Trump has managed to realize the vision that Mathews had back in the 1840s—by being able to speak directly to the public without a middleman; by printing his own sheet, which he named Truth Social. But he still yearns to control every story on the platforms he can’t own. CNN reported that one of his top aides, Natalie Harp, showed him media clips that allegedly infuriated him the week before the ban on the three outlets. In this way, Trump remains locked in the TV-driven media environment that he helped destabilize. Even though the public’s trust of the press has drastically declined over the years, and even though Trump has a direct line to Americans via his robust social-media presence, he still demonstrates a “talismanic belief in the power of news coverage,” my colleague David A. Graham wrote last week.

Like Nixon, Trump desires control not just of his story but of the stories told about him. He wants to have all of the advantages of every medium—to print his own sheet, to star in the show, and to decide how the show will ultimately play out. Modern life doesn’t work that way, but there is little chance that he’ll stop trying.

Pete Hegseth seems to imagine himself as a man of big ideas. Perhaps you’ve worked with someone like that: Every organization has “idea hamsters,” who spin in their wheels and toss out suggestions of wildly varying quality. Sometimes, such folks can be innovators—but more often, their scattershot enthusiasm and admiration of their own creativity result in half-baked proposals that create more work, less productivity, and distractions from the real problems.

Hegseth had a lot of ideas during his State of the Force address to troops at the Marine Corps Base Quantico yesterday, and some of them, at least as a starting point for policies, had merit. Unfortunately, these few moments of sense were lost in a fog of clichés, culture-war resentments, and trademark Hegseth corniness.

The secretary may have a point, for example, that the U.S. military has become top-heavy with generals and admirals. Hegseth is not the first person to notice this: Congress legislates the number of general and flag officers in the military, a cap that was set at 962 for years, until the 2017 National Defense Authorization Act, written in Barack Obama’s last year as president, mandated it be 11 percent smaller by the end of 2022. Hegseth wants to reduce that number even more, by 20 percent, ostensibly to reduce the size of the Pentagon’s bureaucracy.

So far, so good. But Hegseth’s message was less about seeking a more streamlined command structure than it was, as he put it, about keeping generals who “heard the message” and firing those who didn’t. (That “message” wasn’t clarified yesterday, but it likely includes classic Hegseth tropes such as “maximum lethality, not tepid legality.”) Hegseth complained that the media—and he complained about the media a lot—called these reductions “a purge,” but he calls them “accountability.”

Exactly what these fired officers did to trigger such “accountability” remains unclear. It’s one thing to get rid of an admiral because his or her office is just some bureaucratic zombie position left over from a previous program or initiative; it’s another to fire someone because the secretary thinks they’re disloyal to him. Unfortunately, Hegseth’s history—his statements, and his often-adolescent temperament—suggest that his efforts to downsize the senior ranks are about politics, not efficiency.

Hegseth also announced that he intends to create a new command dedicated to autonomous warfare. He is right to want to direct more attention to these weapons; the wars in Ukraine and Iran have been bloody lessons in the power of asymmetrical warfare. But he gave no explanation for why these issues require an entire command, or how it might work. All he offered was a cool name—Autowarcom—that makes it sound like it’s meant to fight the Transformers. And for someone who has repeatedly declared himself “in a war of attrition” with the Pentagon’s bureaucracy, the secretary is more than willing to create a new command that would require more senior officers and civilians to staff it. (He also wants to establish an Office of Religious Affairs that reports directly to him; not only would this be more bureaucracy, but the last thing America needs is an office dedicated to religion that reports to a man who has made repeated efforts to imbue the military with his brand of Christian evangelicism.)

Once Hegseth got rolling, the ideas got sillier. He announced Project Meridian, an effort to engage in creative thinking about the military of the future. This is not a new concept; the military regularly works on such issues with various groups, including think tanks, civilian defense firms, and academics.

But then Hegseth announced the three wise men who would lead all this brainstorming about the future of warfare: Elon Musk, Newt Gingrich, and Palmer Luckey. Of the three, Luckey—who co-founded the defense firm Anduril Industries—seems a reasonable choice, but Musk has crackpot ideas on any number of issues, and Gingrich’s big plan for technological advancement when he ran for president in 2012 was to build a moon base. Fortunately, the damage this project could do will be somewhat limited because Hegseth has set a deadline of just 120 days for these men to report back. Then again, if Gingrich, Luckey, and Musk can’t redefine the future of national defense in four months, who can?

It was a day for acronyms and project code names, but the best was FORTRESS America, meaning “Federal Operations for Resilience, Troop Resources, Energy, Supply, and Survivability.” The expensive idea behind this one is that every major military base should be self-sustaining, including producing its own energy—apparently, in case of attack within the United States. You can almost hear the meeting where someone said “Fortress America,” and then a committee had to figure out what the acronym would stand for. (I am reminded of the classic TV show The Man From U.N.C.L.E., whose creators thought “U.N.C.L.E.” sounded cool and then had to find words to fill the letters.)

Speaking of bases, Hegseth would like every governor to come to the Pentagon and pitch him, personally—and maybe even the president!—in a competition to win a brand new military base, a state-of-the-art facility with great housing and lethal capabilities. Does America need such a base? Would this effort draw money and time away from improvements to other military installations? None of that was spelled out, but Hegseth seemed excited about choosing the location in a military version of a reality-TV competition.

And on it went, with Hegseth spinning one bad idea after another, and managing to insult any number of Americans in the process. He did his usual riff of wanting “no fatties, no trannies, no beardos, no weirdos, no wimps, no radicals” in his macho military. (“The ideological clowns are out,” he said. “The patriotic cowboys are in, with testosterone testing on top.”) He also denigrated the graduates of America’s ROTC programs by implying that today’s universities are producing unreliable and unpatriotic officers. He wants to establish new military programs at six colleges, including Liberty University, Louisiana State University, and Hillsdale College—institutions, according to the secretary, that understand “the faith of Jerusalem,” and the Greco-Roman “pillars of our republic.”

Hegseth could not ignore ongoing problems such as the war in Iran, but rather than presenting a sober-minded and responsible discussion of the challenges to a military at war, the secretary instead leaned into his usual culture-war gripes. He also eagerly sowed division between the U.S. military and the society it serves: He told the assembled troops, repeatedly, that the media and higher education were their enemies, at one point saying that the American media were worse than Iran’s state media. Our press, he said, “intentionally smears the troops and the historic mission that they are undertaking,” actions that are “fundamentally anti-American. It’s sick. It’s a national betrayal. It’s treason.”

Hegseth’s speech proved, yet again, that he is manifestly unfit for his job. America can endure a bloated Pentagon with some generals in make-work jobs. It can stand to trim the waste in expensive programs and root out incompetent bureaucrats. What a democracy cannot—and should not—tolerate is a secretary of defense who trucks in propaganda and wants the people who serve in America’s military to be the armed instrument of his own resentments and biases.
