President Trump has been repeatedly stymied in his efforts to bring the Iran war to a close. Resuming a limited bombing campaign didn’t get it done. Neither did making apocalyptic threats to wipe out Iran’s entire civilization. Every time Trump touts a possible breakthrough in negotiations, the hard-liners in Tehran seem to delight in changing their terms and yanking away a possible deal. It’s like Lucy holding the football for Charlie Brown, except this time, the pigskin is the Strait of Hormuz.

Nothing has worked. So Trump’s new strategy is to try doing nothing, and see whether that works. The president is de-emphasizing both diplomacy and military action, and instead placing his faith in economic pressure, in the hope that time can accomplish what force could not. This wait-and-see approach is meant to allow stepped-up financial sanctions and a U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports to inflict economic pain. The hope is that a return to this strategy—which was used, in spurts, earlier in the conflict and has been championed by some in the administration all along—will finally force leaders in Tehran to seriously engage in negotiations to open the strait and end the war.

But this new tack will require the president to show patience. Trump is, to say the least, not known for his self-discipline. Or for his ability to stick to a plan. Or for his capacity to avoid being influenced by the last segment he saw on cable TV. Advisers privately acknowledge that a single Truth Social post fired off in anger could lead to an escalation that derails the entire plan. And there are risks even if the strategy works. Iran has repeatedly surprised U.S. officials with its economic resilience; the time now needed to effectively squeeze the country all but ensures that there will be no quick resolution to the war—and no corresponding decline in energy costs—before Republicans try to defend their congressional majorities in November’s midterm elections. But Trump and his aides see few other options to end their war of choice.

Within the White House, a new set of statistics is winning the debate. In recent weeks, aides have shown Trump the significant impact that existing sanctions are having on Iran’s economy, two senior administration officials told me. The case has been laid out before the president in Oval Office meetings—and, in at least one instance, in a call from Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent while Trump was wrapping up a round of golf—that tightening the current sanctions and issuing fresh, tough ones could force leaders in Tehran to buckle, the officials said, speaking on the condition of anonymity to discuss internal deliberations. The idea is hardly new: Trump has relied on an economic “maximum-pressure” campaign since his first term to try to force Iran to abandon its nuclear program. And earlier this year, further sanctions were applied.

Aides think it may work this time. And Trump underscored this belief yesterday, telling reporters that “Iran is broke, totally broke.” But he has repeatedly misjudged the Iranians. In the conflict’s early days, Trump was convinced that the war would take a few weeks at most and that—despite warnings provided by the Pentagon—Iran would not try to close the Strait of Hormuz. Those assumptions were wrong, as was the belief held within the intelligence community that Iran would buckle quickly under the economic strain. But the regime seems willing to endure significant hardship, and its own citizens’ struggles, to maintain its grip on power.

And it has responded with its own changing list of demands that includes an insistence on collecting some sort of toll or fee for use of the narrow channel. More than once, and as recently as last week, it appeared that a deal to reopen the strait was close and that Trump was prepared to declare victory in the conflict and move on (conveniently ignoring that the waterway had been open and free for all to use prior to the war). Allies in the Persian Gulf have registered unhappiness at any increased Iranian control over the strait but were told that they would have to live with the new arrangement, at least for a while. Bessent asserted to Trump, and later in interviews, that the Strait of Hormuz would become irrelevant within two years, claiming that up to 70 percent of the energy that transits the waterway will eventually flow through underground pipelines.

But once more, the regime leaders in Tehran altered the deal, this time insisting that the strait would reopen only once the United States paid reparations for losses suffered during the five-month war. That condition was deemed a nonstarter in Washington, and an enraged Trump countered by declaring that the U.S. would need to be paid its own set of reparations for the deaths of American forces over the decades as well as for the families of Iranian protesters slain in the past 50 years, including in recent demonstrations (which won’t happen either). Despite the occasional hopeful-sounding statements from mediators in Oman and Pakistan, negotiations remain at an impasse. Trump has furiously told aides that he doesn’t feel that Iran is negotiating in good faith.

The White House stressed to me that Iran’s economy is already reeling, with soaring inflation, redacted energy revenue, and some agricultural shortfalls. One official added that “there are many levers the president can crank harder in the weeks and months ahead.”

But there is another set of numbers that also informed Trump’s decision to sit tight: the dwindling stockpiles of some U.S. munitions, especially missile defenses. Before the war, there was no monthslong buildup of forces and no large-scale deployment of troops. Although the military has since surged additional forces into the region, many assets—particularly Navy ships—were diverted from other theaters of war rather than trained for the conflict. And as my colleague Nancy A. Youssef and I reported last week, some missile stockpiles are as low as 20 percent of what the Pentagon would like to have. There may not be enough to defend American interests in Asia, and stores of some sophisticated interceptor missiles are too depleted for the U.S. to give or sell the weapons to Ukraine or to effectively defend against Iran’s launch of ballistic missiles.

[Read: Ukraine’s short-lived comeback ]

This shortage has curtailed Trump’s options. Were he to authorize another wide-scale strike against Iran, U.S. officials are not confident in their ability to protect American bases or allies in the region from retaliation. And Iran has proved effective in striking against U.S. forces. Last month, during a renewed stretch of fighting, an Iranian missile slipped through defenses at a base in Jordan and detonated on a barracks housing U.S. troops.

The danger posed by Tehran is so great that the Secret Service took extraordinary measures to safeguard Trump last month when he left a NATO summit in Turkey, which shares a border with Iran. The Qatari-made luxury jet filling in as the presidential aircraft was deemed to lack appropriate defensive measures, so the original, standard Air Force One was purportedly assigned the task of taking Trump out of Ankara. But according to The Washington Post, the threat was so credible that Trump had to secretly move to another aircraft via a small catering truck. The smaller plane carrying the president took off without notice, and the Boeing 747 normally designated Air Force One then departed, carrying some senior officials and the traveling press pool.

Of course, no one in the White House knows whether Trump will be patient enough to stick to the new plan. He has been bored of the war for a while, but perhaps the slow economic squeeze will allow the conflict to slip from the headlines and permit him to focus on other things (such as the Reflecting Pool and maybe Cuba).

But Iranian leaders have proved effective at goading Trump into action, and there are hawks in Congress and the Pentagon who have advocated for wider strikes. The president might also hear from Republicans running for reelection this fall who are saddled with the party’s poor poll numbers and the politically damaging high price of gas, who might beg him to do something—anything!—about it. Trump tends to be swayed by viral social-media posts and cable-news chyrons. His longtime adviser Steve Bannon told me years ago that Trump doesn’t try to “win a news cycle”; instead, he aims to win “a news moment, a news second,” and that trait has only intensified since. It likely wouldn’t take much to get him to shift course again.

But for now, at least, the White House thinks Trump’s best play might be to do just the opposite.

Over the past few days, the president has continued his crusade against one of the supposed vandals of the Lincoln Memorial’s Reflecting Pool. In a lengthy post on Truth Social on Sunday, he alleged that “a National Park Service career employee, a highly credible witness,” had caught the former Olympic canoeist David Hearn in the act of “ripping and tugging at the Pool’s somewhat delicate coating.”

The problem is that the Department of Justice’s case against Hearn is already effectively over. More than a week earlier, federal prosecutors dropped the charges against him, citing information from the Department of the Interior; the damage to the Reflecting Pool was “the result of a botched installation and not vandalism,” the government’s motion to dismiss reads. That evidence—and the judgment of DOJ—has done little to stop Donald Trump from pursuing this debunked theory over and over again. In recent weeks, the president has repeatedly returned to the same few vendettas and personal obsessions, trying to push them forward no matter the evidence against them. His strategy is summed up by a self-assessment that he gave CNN more than a decade ago: “I am the most fabulous whiner,” he said. “I keep whining and whining until I win.”

Today, The Wall Street Journal reported that White House officials have asked the Justice Department to consider prosecuting Hearn a second time. By doubling down on his vandalism thesis, Trump is sending a strong message to one of his most ardent supporters, U.S. Attorney for the District of Columbia Jeanine Pirro, whom he already castigated last week for dropping the charges. “I guess she choked,” he told reporters at the time.

The rulings of the Supreme Court don’t seem to sway the president either. On Thursday, he doubled down on another mission of personal importance—limiting birthright citizenship—despite the Court’s recent decision to strike down a previous executive order on the subject. Trump has now issued two new orders that are narrower in scope. One seeks to expand the category of children whom the government can deny automatic citizenship, and the other targets “birth tourism,” a phenomenon in which people travel to the United States “for the purpose of giving birth on American soil”—something that the law already prohibits. These new attempts to upend more than a century of precedent are likely to face significant legal challenges. This tactic wasn’t Trump’s only option: Drawing on Justice Brett Kavanaugh’s June opinion, the president could potentially urge Congress to amend a particular statute or enact new legislation to limit birthright citizenship (although this legislation would be unlikely to pass). Instead, he chose to use the same tool in the hope of getting a different result.

Trump is also forging ahead on his attempts to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook. Nearly a year ago, the president tried to fire her over allegations of mortgage fraud. In June, the Court ruled that she couldn’t be fired, in part because the White House hadn’t given her a chance to contest the allegations. Last week, Trump’s longtime aide Dan Scavino sent Cook a letter warning her that she may still be removed and telling her in no uncertain terms that “this is your opportunity to respond.” In this case, Trump appears to be cooperating with the Court, although it’s uncertain whether this procedure will be enough to satisfy it. The note makes clear that the president isn’t letting up in his quest to oust Cook.

Trump’s tenacity in pursuing his own agenda is nothing new. Take, for example, his eight-year attempt to brand former FBI Director James Comey as a criminal, despite a lack of evidence; when, in the fall, the first set of charges against him didn’t stick, Trump’s Justice Department simply tried again. As David A. Graham wrote in his story last week about the birthright-citizenship orders, the president’s recent actions invoke a kind of déjà vu. The commitment to doubling down has become a one-size-fits-all strategy: Trump’s desire to fight back doesn’t necessarily correlate with the odds of victory in a given situation. My colleague Tom Nichols recently noted that the president’s push to prosecute Hearn has only emphasized “just how immune to evidence and logic his beliefs have become.” Trump’s fixation on the Reflecting Pool is also directing his focus away from the issues that midterm voters care about most, such as affordability.

Meanwhile, new setbacks are arriving all the time. On Friday, after an appeals court upheld the block on Trump’s attempts to build a new ballroom for the White House, the president promised to make his case to the Supreme Court. That fight, like so many others, is not over until Trump says it is.

For several months, I had been on the hunt for new T-shirts. I vaguely had a vision of what they should look like: not so different from my current T-shirts, except in some way elevated and, more urgent, free of stains or holes. Perhaps they could come in interesting colors? Ideally, they would not be made in sweatshops. There were many, many shirts that seemed to meet these criteria—and with a few clicks, they could be delivered to my doorstep, maybe even by tomorrow. And still, I had purchased none of them.

The problem is the overabundance of options. The problem is too much information, or too little information. The problem is inflation, which has eroded any sense I had of what a T-shirt is supposed to cost. Yet none of them alone can explain why shopping is sucking up more of my time—and maybe yours too. “I don’t think people are actually enjoying the shopping process,” Jaehee Jung, a University of Delaware professor who studies the psychology of fashion, told me. “People spend more time contemplating, and the pre-decision process seems to be taking longer.”

Everywhere, someone is selling something: on Instagram and TikTok, over email and via text. At this point, the boundary between “shopping” and “not shopping” has all but collapsed. There is now a third state: “a little bit shopping.” Shopping is visiting a store or website on purpose, examining the wares, and either buying or not buying them. A little bit shopping is the constant thrum of consumer opportunities that lead mostly to more consumer opportunities. It is looking at a pair of shoes, which leads to looking at another pair of shoes, and another, and then you’re $15 short of the minimum for free shipping and is there anything else you need? It is the feeling of being haunted by the targeted ads that populate your social-media feeds. Even if you never click on them, their very presence contributes to the overwhelming sense that the retail landscape is infinite—so infinite that perfection must exist, and if you look long enough and hard enough, somewhere in the universe, it is probably on sale.

[Read: There is too much stuff]

In small doses, a little bit shopping can be fun, a private thrill. It is both a genuine attempt at making a purchase, and a hobby that is infinitely expandable to fill any length of time. Who among us has not scrolled through sweater sales while trapped on a work call? It does not require spending money, nor does it apply to urgent and essential purchases: You don’t a little bit shop for baby Tylenol. “No matter what you’re doing on the computer,” Wendy Woloson, a material historian at Rutgers University at Camden, told me, “it seems like there’s a really easy drift into the shopping mode.”

Unlike brick-and-mortar stores, the internet never closes. You can a little bit shop forever, until you are also a little bit miserable. Shopping in person, then, ought to solve the problem, but it often doesn’t. A survey of 30 retailers by the consulting firm AlixPartners found that, on average, they carried only 9 percent of their online offerings in store. The shoes are out of stock in your size, but have you checked the website? You could stand in line to buy lesser, uglier shoes and be done with it, but why? Online, beautiful shoes are infinite and, in theory, instant.

The more mindful you are about various concerns—overconsumption, worker exploitation, microplastics—the higher the bar climbs: I need not only black pants, but ethical black pants that I can wear to the beach, but also to the opera, and that will be flattering from now until my death. No such pants exist, which is why I don’t have any, but as long as you’re a little bit shopping, the possibility exists.

This state of perpetual shopping has been building for ages. People were a little bit shopping on Amazon and eBay in 2005. But the feeling has become even more acute in recent years as e-commerce has taken over and affiliate marketing has boomed. In addition to sites such as Wirecutter and The Strategist, and every media property with a gift guide, all kinds of influencers have opinions about what you should buy (and may receive a commission if you do).

[Read: What happened to Wirecutter?]

My own digital social life is a mix of people I know, people I don’t know, brands that post like people, and people launching brands. Here is my high-school lab partner’s baby. Here is a plate of pasta. Here is a picture of an antique lamp for sale from an estate-sale company in Texas. “Everyone is trying to either make money or gain recognition”—and thereby make money—“for their taste,” Isabel Slone, who writes about fashion on the Substack Freak Palace, told me. An influencer can help move $1 million worth of swimsuits in three hours. For a solitary activity, a little bit shopping is perversely social. It is pleasantly intimate, knowing how an opinionated stranger feels about their chef’s knife, or their microwave, or the life-changing sweater that got them through their divorce and is available right now if you click the link in bio. Whether it is helpful consumer education is a separate question: “I do think that the affiliate-link economy,” Slone said, “contributes to this constant feeling of not knowing exactly what is good.”

There is, of course, a way out. You could stop caring. You could embrace the good-enough and the suboptimal, understanding that a little bit shopping is a trap that keeps you shopping all the time. Eventually, I bought a T-shirt, which is in all ways absolutely adequate, and in no sense exceptional. And then, the next day, I saw a post about a T-shirt, which was better. I clicked.

In her first argument as a lawyer before the Supreme Court, Ruth Bader Ginsburg famously quoted the abolitionist Sarah Grimké, who said, “I ask no favor for my sex. All I ask of our brethren is that they take their feet off our necks.” At some point, however, Ginsburg was encouraged to use an alternative to the term sex.

“I’m typing all these briefs and articles for you and the word sex, sex, sex is on every page,” her secretary at Columbia Law School told her. “Don’t you know that those nine men”—that era’s Supreme Court justices—“they hear that word, and their first association is not the way you want them to be thinking?” The secretary suggested substituting gender to “ward off distracting associations.”

But sex and gender don’t mean the same thing. The two terms have never been stable. Still, I recall a time when, among educated people, sex was typically understood to refer to matters of biology, such as a person’s reproductive organs, and gender to refer to matters that are socially constructed, such as the norm of dressing little boys in blue pants and little girls in pink dresses. Today, there seems to be no standard usage. Parents throw gender-reveal parties to disclose the sex of their baby. President Trump said in his 2025 inaugural address, “It will henceforth be the official policy of the United States government that there are only two genders: male and female”—but “male” and “female” are sexes. A statute in California defines sex (in part) as follows: “‘Sex’ also includes, but is not limited to, a person’s gender. ‘Gender’ means sex, and includes a person’s gender identity and gender expression.”

The confusion that results from this lost distinction causes problems in academia––see the recent kerfuffle in anthropology––as well as in medicine, law, public policy, and more. People mistake meanings and talk past one another about matters that are fraught even when definitions are clear. How did we get into this semantic mess?

The concept of a “gender role” was introduced in a 1955 paper by the researcher John Money to signify “all those things that a person says or does to disclose himself or herself as having the status of boy or man, girl or woman.” He believed that people born with ambiguous or atypical genitals, whom he called “hermaphrodites or intersexes,” contributed to “this new meaning of gender.” Would such individuals operate in society as men or women? He thought that rearing would better predict a person’s “gender role” than biology.

The term gender identity, first defined as “one’s sense of being a member of a particular sex,” dates back to at least the early 1960s. The psychoanalyst Robert Stoller wrote that “gender role is the overt behavior one displays in society, the role which he plays.” By the ’70s, feminists were the most prominent group to adopt gender and related terms, using them to differentiate biological differences between men and women from differences imposed by society. In the 1972 book Sex, Gender, and Society, the feminist sociologist Ann Oakley suggested that biology may play little or no role in determining gender identity (that is, one’s sense of being a member of a particular sex). If a biological predisposition to a male or female gender identity exists at all, she wrote, it “may be decisively and ineradicably overridden by cultural learning.” In the 1990 book Gender Trouble, Judith Butler challenged the common assumption that gender mirrors sex and is binary, arguing that “a gender cannot be said to follow from a sex in any one way.” Butler also argued that “perhaps this construct called ‘sex’ is as culturally constructed as gender,” and the supposed distinction between them “turns out to be no distinction at all.”

In 1997, the sex researcher Milton Diamond and the psychiatrist Keith Sigmundson reached the conclusion that sex is a distinct and important category. The two revisited a case that Money had included in his research. “The penis of an XY individual was accidentally ablated”—that is to say, severely damaged or removed—“and he was subsequently raised as a female,” the authors recounted. “Initially this individual was described as developing into a normally functioning female.” Money had treated the case as confirmation that nurture reliably overrides nature. But the story later changed: When older, the patient rejected “this sex of rearing” and “switched at puberty to living as a male.” This mattered in part because, the authors explained, “the standard in instances of extensive penile damage to infants is to recommend rearing the male as a female.” They urged doctors to reconsider that practice.

The importance of sex as a category was further affirmed in 2001 by a group of experts convened by the Institute of Medicine (since renamed the National Academy of Medicine). Recounting several public-health guidelines that excluded women of child-bearing age from clinical trials on the erroneous assumption that they “will not differ significantly in response to treatment in most situations,” the report noted: “Sex, that is, being male or female,” is “an important basic human variable that should be considered when designing, analyzing, and reporting findings from studies.”

But the importance of sex as a category of study did not guarantee sex a stable place in scholarship, as the Harvard evolutionary biologist David Haig showed by documenting a dramatic decline in the term sex and a rise in gender in the titles of academic articles between 1945 and 2001. At first, feminist scholarship converged on socially constructed gender and biologically determined sex, Haig wrote, but “that distinction was difficult to maintain,” because “no term remained to refer to situations in which causation was unknown, disputed, or involved an interaction between biology and culture.” Soon gender spread to other disciplines, and eventually gender rather than sex began to appear even in scholarship on the physiology of animals. “Among the reasons that working scientists have given me for choosing gender rather than sex in biological contexts,” Haig wrote, “are desires to signal sympathy with feminist goals, to use a more academic term, or to avoid the connotation of copulation.”

By the 2000s, yet another obstacle to mutual understanding arose: What people meant by gender was changing, not only in academic discourse, but in popular usage. For many of the early feminists, gender was a cage built by society. Parents gave little girls but not little boys Easy-Bake Ovens, reproducing a gendered expectation that girls would grow up to do the cooking. But as the decades wore on, gender came to carry a second meaning, one very different from social norms imposed from the outside. The term now sometimes refers to an internal aspect of a person’s sense of self, or even the essence of who they truly are.

While some people kept using gender as shorthand for something from which they sought liberation––to weaken gender norms was to fight the patriarchy––others saw it as an identity, perhaps innate, that individuals discerned and expressed, and that a just society ought to affirm. In 2014, Facebook announced that users would no longer have to identify as male or female––they could choose among at least 58 “gender” options (including intersex, a term that tells us something about a person’s reproductive or sexual anatomy but not about their gender identity).

That brings us to the present era, when sex and gender loom larger than ever in political, legal, and cultural debates––but usage keeps changing faster than the public can keep up, and many semantic choices are made for reasons other than advancing shared understanding. Three examples help illustrate the dysfunction that can result:

  1. When Australia asked individuals to note their sex in its 2021 census, the form gave three options: male, female, and nonbinary, “to allow respondents to participate in the Census when the male and female sex categories did not accurately describe their sex,” the Australian Bureau of Statistics explained. Among the 25,422,788 people that the census counted, 43,220 respondents chose nonbinary.

    But this “did not yield meaningful data,” the bureau later acknowledged, because the respondents hadn’t agreed upon a definition of nonbinary. Among the small number of respondents who selected nonbinary and explained why, “a very small number” indicated that they were intersex; “a small number” noted their sexual orientation; and three-fifths listed a gender identity. The bureau found the data unusable “to estimate the prevalence of any specific group.” The 2026 census will ask about sex and gender separately.
  2. A 2024 study from JAMA Ophthalmology reviewed pivotal clinical trials in the field between 1995 and 2022. It sought to determine whether sex and gender were used correctly (relying on the National Institutes of Health definitions of sex as “a biological characteristic, reflected genetically by chromosomes and physiologically by sex hormones, internal reproductive organs, and external genitalia” and gender as “an aspect of identity that is self-determined and influenced by sociocultural and environmental factors”). The researchers found that in 85 trials, “over three-quarters applied sex and gender terminology incorrectly” and that “no trial reported how sex or gender were collected,” even though “sex and gender are gaining increasing recognition as important determinants of disease epidemiology, health outcomes, and health care disparities.” The authors concluded that the “policies of biomedical journals on sex and gender are incongruous and uncoordinated” in a way that hurts the field’s mission.
  3. In a 2023 interview with Time magazine, the soccer player Megan Rapinoe objected to barring trans women from playing on the same sports teams as biological females. “I see trans women as real women,” she declared. “What you’re saying automatically in the argument—you’re sort of telling on yourself already—is you don’t believe these people are women.” But her formulation elides the fact that women is used variably, sometimes to signify sex and sometimes to signify gender.

    Many proponents of segregating sports by sex do not reject the gender identity of trans women. For example, in the prominent case of Lia Thomas, a trans woman who, controversially, swam on the women’s team at the University of Pennsylvania, 16 teammates sent a letter to Penn that stated, “We fully support Lia Thomas in her decision to affirm her gender identity and to transition from a man to a woman. Lia has every right to live her life authentically. However, we also recognize that when it comes to sports competition, that the biology of sex is a separate issue from someone’s gender identity.” Only by distinguishing between sex and gender can we distinguish between two different viewpoints: that sports leagues should reject a trans person’s account of their own gender, or that gender is irrelevant to this debate and sex alone should govern policy around eligibility to play.

Different ideological groups will continue deploying language that best suits their ends. But we all ought to value terms that are accurate and aid mutual understanding. Clarity is best advanced by using sex to refer to biology––and, when appropriate, to specify whether the particular trait relevant to a given discussion concerns gametes, chromosomes, hormones, anatomy, or some combination. In contrast, gender should be used to refer to matters that are socially constructed––and concepts such as gender norms, gender identity, and gender expression should be used to distinguish different conceptions of gender from one another. If and when disputes arise about whether we’re referring to sex or gender or a mix, speakers should explain why they chose a given term and what precisely they mean by it.

These usage conventions can’t tell us who belongs on what sports team or what is required or forbidden under antidiscrimination law, nor can it settle any number of other debates. But they can help us all better understand and engage in them.
