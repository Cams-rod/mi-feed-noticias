Sharing the culture you love—a beloved song, that book that changed your life—can help translate your inner world to the people you love. Or it might make you look really strange. So it goes in one sketch from last night’s Saturday Night Live, in which a trio of teen girls at a sleepover (played by Sarah Sherman, Veronika Slowikowska, and Jane Wickline) take turns playing clips of their favorite get-ready-with-me videos, the popular format where content creators dress and prepare themselves for the day ahead. Two of the girls share par-for-the-course videos, before Sherman’s character introduces everyone to the influencer she loves the most: @ColoradoVicky1 (Dakota Johnson, the show’s host), a woman whose life doesn’t seem so aspirational.

As she smiles cheerfully, her eyes vacant, Vicky invites her audience to “get ready with me for court-ordered electroshock therapy.” In another clip, she styles herself in green lipstick and a Teletubby-esque antenna in preparation for getting “5150’d on the roof of a San Bernardino Whole Foods.” “She seems … unwell,” says Slowikowska’s character. But Sherman’s teen girl pays no mind to such judgments, or to her friend’s question about how she found this account, whose videos don’t even have double-digit views. “I’m legiterally addicted to her ass,” Sherman says. “Guess my algorithm is just cool-mazing?!”

These days, everyone uses a different internet. Algorithms serve up specialized content catered to each user’s unique interests, creating a warped mirror of their own reality, and last night’s sketch cleverly captured the disconnect that sometimes arises when people reveal their private online consumption to one another.

@ColoradoVicky1’s behavior only gets more bizarre the more clips the girls watch. Without blinking once, Vicky explains that her “outfit of the day” is a pair of handcuffs, which she plans to use to attach herself to the actor who plays Turtle in Entourage. She one-ups this proclamation with an even stranger how-to video, in which she asks viewers to tag along as she makes brunch and gives herself a lobotomy. (“You’re gonna want to start by finding a really good power drill,” she monotones.)

[Read: The loneliness crisis hits SNL]

Sherman’s friends seem to get progressively more put off by her media diet. It doesn’t help that Sherman’s character isn’t the only one in the house who could probably stand to log off. Every few minutes, her dad (James Austin Johnson) comes into the room with snacks and immediately reveals how depressed he is: “I’m just gonna go drink in the pool for a while, stand in the deep end,” he announces, before leaving the room. When the sleepover guests are concerned, Sherman explains that her dad is just in a funk: His boss discovered he dresses up like Sailor Moon, and he just found out that Draft Kings uses real money. His particular online milieu is bleeding into, and beginning to harm, his real-life existence.

When her friends ask if they should get help, Sherman’s character insists that they “stop giving him attention—that’s, like, exactly what he wants.” She continues to ignore his not-so-subtle cries for help, until the sketch’s ending beat, in which he randomly shows up in @ColoradoVicky1’s livestream as her new lover. Sherman seems thrilled by this development, announcing that Vicky is going to be her new mom. Finally, her father’s real-life depression is good for something.

Teen girls and their dads alike are vulnerable to having their minds and lives warped by their feeds, the sketch warns. And once brought to light, these online rabbit holes might not translate so well to real life: The sketch-comedy version of this is funny because it’s so familiar, while the fracturing of reality outside SNL is mostly just unnerving.

The wind brings the circus
to a lonesome town upstate,
and puffins to the Isle of May,
   seagirt, desolate.

The wind brings spectators
by ferry to the docks.
Side by side like gravy boats
   seals loll on the rocks.

The throng swipes their shoes
on a disinfectant mat,
running down the gangplank
   to the puffins’ habitat.

The main ring is the breeding ground
where lies their single egg
circled with a constant cry
   echoed off the crag.

They circle, circle, swoop, and dive,
coming back with silver sprats
like spiny roses in the teeth
   of undefeated acrobats.

The comic masks they wear belie
the effort it must take
to migrate all their lives in search
   of a decent windbreak.

Clown-bright lichen clothes the rocks,
sea campion froths in grass.
Here’s the roofless abbey
   on a speck in the atlas.

AI’s Real Gift to Science

Dario Amodei, the chief executive of Anthropic, has often said that AI will cure most diseases in the next 10 years. Late last month, he claimed that his company had taken a first step toward that future: Claude had, in less than 24 hours, discovered a new “enzyme system” that the company says resembles CRISPR, a gene-editing tool currently curing people of diseases. While announcing the discovery, Anthropic hinted that this was the type of finding that revolutionizes medicine and quoted a gene-editing pioneer who called it “genuinely intriguing.”

The company was open about not knowing what exactly it had spotted or whether the finding—the first released from a biology lab that the company established this spring—was really that significant. Still, many academics, along with the CEO of Eli Lilly, pointed out that the company had dressed up a very incremental finding as a major discovery. Fyodor Urnov, a scientist who coined the term genome editing and is working on a CRISPR project with Anthropic, told me that “the jury is out” on whether what Claude found is even an enzyme at all. Multiple researchers told The New York Times that, in fact, they had been aware of the supposedly new enzyme system for years, including a team at the University of Copenhagen that alleged Anthropic’s new results might have been stolen from their lab’s interactions with Claude. The company, in response, said that Claude isn’t trained using user transcripts. (Anthropic did not respond to a comment request for this article.)

In other words, Anthropic made a big deal about a finding that—although achieved in a novel, Claude-led way—may not lead to any biomedical advances, and that the company knew may not lead to any biomedical advances. Ironically, in making such grandiose statements, the company and other AI boosters obscured what Claude does appear to have accomplished: It sorted through reams of biological data that would have taken scientists much longer to analyze, if they could have at all. This capacity might not be a miracle, but for scientists facing mountains of data that have surpassed their capacity for analysis, it’s still meaningful.

Around the turn of the 21st century, researchers sequenced for the first time all 3 billion letters that make up human DNA. This effort, called the Human Genome Project, took 13 years and cost $2.7 billion. Today, sequencing a person’s full genome takes just over a day and costs as little as $200. Accordingly, the total amount of publicly available genomic data has become so large as to be unmanageable. In other fields, such as protein science, technological advances have led to a similar boom in the sheer amount of data being collected.

Trying to pull anything useful from all that data has become its own long-term project. In 2012, a committee of bioinformatics experts found that the “bottleneck in scientific productivity” had moved from data production to data analysis, but early efforts at the National Institutes of Health to deal with that bottleneck quickly sputtered out. If researchers could efficiently store, send, and examine all of the information they generated, they would likely be able to, for example, develop new drugs by identifying better genes or cellular parts to target. But it’s simply too massive for them to comprehensively attack.

The biological corpus is not, however, too large for AI agents. Bo Wang, an assistant professor at the University of Toronto and the chief AI scientist at a drug-discovery company, recently wrote that AI’s biggest impact in biology will come from its help “scaling scientific attention”: Setting agents loose on the enormous data sets can surface the outliers that warrant further human inspection. Agents can propose and test hypotheses for what might cause the unusual patterns they find, and predictive modeling can help scientists choose what experiments to carry out on their actual lab benches. Eventually, automated labs might even carry out those chosen experiments—a few already exist in the United States, and the Trump administration is spending hundreds of millions to establish more. Meanwhile, scientists remain divided over whether AI models actually steer them toward potentially paradigm-shifting experiments, or safer, less exploratory ones.

AI is already changing medicine: Models that use people’s medical history to predict their health risks, including their likelihood of developing certain kinds of cancer, are now used in most U.S. hospitals. At the same time, not every field of biology generates enough data for this approach to work. AI might be less useful in some domains that rely on less abundant microscopy data, such as the study of how cells and tissues communicate; such data tend to be harder to come by than genetic sequences, Stirling Churchman, a professor of genetics at Harvard Medical School, told me. Organelle biology and the science of cellular transport similarly require painstaking, time-consuming experiments.

Still, Churchman said, “there will be magic where there’s data.” She spent the summer at OpenAI working on a specially trained model for life-science researchers. She anticipates that by allowing scientists who can’t code to examine their data themselves, AI will enable researchers to “analyze data at least 10 times faster, freeing us to spend more time on the biological questions and experiments.”

The recent Anthropic finding is an example of how AI can identify outliers that might otherwise have gone unnoticed. Existing tools can comb through heaps of genomic data (and have already been used to identify bona fide new gene-editing systems). What AI can do is run those kinds of analyses at a much larger scale, while also evaluating intermediary results, Kendell Clement, who runs a computational-biology lab at the University of Utah, told me. Of course, that scale requires dumping in correspondingly huge resources: Anthropic directed about 950 Claude bots to simultaneously pore over a database that contained nearly 2 billion DNA entries.

AI companies have every incentive to keep evangelizing about the medical miracles they say their models will unlock, especially as backlash against them mounts. Amodei has an idea for how to quiet AI skeptics: “The thing that will work is actually curing cancer,” he recently wrote on X. That would certainly be a coup. But helping scientists work more quickly is, too, even if it doesn’t sound quite so revolutionary.

Not so long ago, heroic public statuary felt irrelevant—good for locating yourself in traffic circles but otherwise dismissed as a relic of some credulous, great-man-besotted past. The fact that places like Pyongyang and Ashgabat still went in for it just confirmed the association with autocratic backwardness and childish megalomania.

Things have changed. In Washington, D.C., millions of taxpayer dollars were spent on regilding a pair of allegorical equestrian bronzes with the cheap sheen of a dollar-store trophy, and the Trump administration has been defiantly pursuing legally contested plans for an arch of hypertrophic vulgarity. Meanwhile, in Los Angeles, an exhibition with Kara Walker’s dismembered and reconstituted statue of Stonewall Jackson and his horse became one of the most widely covered art events of the past 12 months. Figurative monuments are now being taken seriously—desired by some (well, at least one guy), vilified by many, and argued over by just about everybody. Whose history do they tell? When should they be pulled down or repurposed? How effective are they anyway?

In this environment, the advent of two big museum shows with “Pharaohs” in the title feels timely. No people on Earth have done monuments more ambitiously and lastingly than the ancient Egyptians. Staking claims to eternal fame, pharaohs stamped their names and features on objects of mind-boggling scale: the Great Sphinx of Giza, the Colossi of Memnon, the mountainous figures of Ramesses II at Abu Simbel.

Curated by the former director of the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, Tarek el-Awady, and with a catalog by the high-profile Egyptian archaeologist Zahi Hawass, “Treasures of the Pharaohs” is both a buzzy blockbuster and an assiduous cultural survey. (Now at the de Young Museum, in San Francisco, it will travel to the Kimbell Art Museum, in Fort Worth, next year.) Its 100-plus artifacts stretch from tiny gold animal amulets some 5,000 years old to a gilded incense burner about 2,700 years younger, and from a child’s clay rattle found in a workers’ settlement near Thebes to the gold funerary mask of the 21st-dynasty king Amenemope, his bland face framed by the flanged nemes headdress still de rigueur for all costume-party pharaohs.

Styles changed over that enormous time span: The French-bob wig shown on a statue of Lady Hekenu in the fifth dynasty differs from the Gidget flips worn by Ukh-Hotep’s wives in the 12th dynasty, which look nothing like the elaborate braided wigs of Senetnay and her daughter in the 18th dynasty. Wigs aside, however, the story is one of overarching continuity: a society structured around divine kingship, detailed preparations for the afterlife, and the cosmological ideal of truth, justice, and order known as ma’at, all of which found expression in a remarkably stable set of visual forms.

New pharaohs imitated the monuments of old pharaohs or had them retailored to fit themselves. The essential Egyptian pictorial convention of figures in profile with torsos twisted forward was already in place by the time of King Narmer (around 3000 B.C.E.) and was still serving Cleopatra in 30 B.C.E. (It’s worth noting that the distance between those two rulers is 1,000 years longer than that between Cleopatra and us.)

The hook is Bronze Age glamour, and exhibition publicity gives pride of place to the beautiful golden face and blue-lined eyes of Thuya, mother-in-law of the pharaoh Amenhotep III. But Hawass also wants viewers to understand Egypt as “a pioneering power that demonstrated how to build a unified state, establishing the fundamental principles of science and governance.” A model boat from the early Middle Kingdom (circa 2000 B.C.E.), fully staffed with oarsmen, a captain, and a sailor plumbing the depths, serves a discussion of Egyptian nautical sophistication. Tomb portraits of commoners such as the carpenter Inty‑Shedu back up Hawass’s claim that Egypt’s social stability rested in part on its allowance for skill-based upward mobility.

The problem for big surveys like “Treasures” is that it’s hard to keep track of so many pharaohs, dynasties, kingdoms, and gods (Hawass counts more than 1,500 deities). The larger sociological points land, and many objects dazzle, but the individual biographies tend to blur.

“Maverick Kings: Three Visionary Pharaohs of Egypt’s Golden Age,” at the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, takes a different approach. Lawrence M. Berman, the exhibit’s curator, has written two books that tease out tales of individual lives from objects in his care. This exhibition does something similar, zeroing in on Hatshepsut, Amenhotep III (him again), and his son Akhenaten. All belonged to the 18th dynasty (circa 1550–1292 B.C.E.), when Egypt was at its richest and most self-assured. All were outliers in different ways, and all were committed (it’s tempting to say addicted) to proclaiming their achievements and broadcasting their images as broadly as possible.

The daughter of one pharaoh and the half sister and wife of another (a frequent Egyptian arrangement), Hatshepsut was serving as co-regent with her young nephew/stepson when she took the title of king herself—an extraordinary, though not unprecedented, action. For more than two decades, she kept the peace, expanded trade, and transformed the temple complex of Karnak at Thebes. She aggrandized processional routes with monuments, including obelisks of solid granite that rose 100 feet and were topped with electrum (a mirror-bright natural gold-silver alloy). Built into the cliffs on the west bank of the Nile, her memorial temple rises in stepped terraces, like something Frank Lloyd Wright might have done if he’d had the power and purse of a potentate. These efforts may have been for the glory of the dynasty’s official state god, Amun‑Re, but statues of Hatshepsut punctuated the vistas like a visual drumbeat.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art
A delicately feminine statue of Hatshepsut, a rare woman who took the title of king.

In Boston, an obelisk fragment shows Hatshepsut flat-chested and broad-shouldered, wearing the kilt, headdress, and rectangular false beard of a pharaoh. But in a beguiling seated statue on loan from the Metropolitan Museum of Art, she appears distinctly feminine, delicate and beardless, though still clad in a pharaoh’s kilt and headdress. These variations tell us less about Hatshepsut’s gender proclivities than about monuments as public performance. Egyptian kingship had a recognized visual grammar, and attributes of office could operate independently of physiognomic truth. A manly statue of Hatshepsut was no different in principle from the perennially youthful Ramesses II we see depicted across his six and a half decades on the throne.

Amenhotep III was Hatshepsut’s great-great-grandnephew and perhaps “the greatest patron of the arts in Egypt’s history,” Berman writes. Having inherited a kingdom and immense wealth, he threw himself into aggrandizing old temples and constructing new ones; he built a palace complex big enough to accommodate an artificial lake and accessorized everything with statues of himself, including the 60-foot-tall Colossi of Memnon. Tempering the geometric rigor of earlier monuments with a new sensuality of form, he established an Egyptian classicism to which later generations repeatedly returned.

It’s hard, looking at the finely carved quartzite head of Amenhotep in Boston, to grasp the majesty it would once have conveyed. His skull flows into the bowling-pin-shaped crown of Upper Egypt in a way that closely resembles the Coneheads of early SNL. And whereas most pharaohs are shown with a small, serene smile, Amenhotep’s heavy-lidded eyes, elegant brows, and grinning lips convey such self-satisfied beatitude that he looks a bit stoned. Originally, however, the head was probably part of a standing statue in which the king’s living spirit was present. Instead of conveying drowsiness, those lowered eyelids would have directed his gaze down at subjects, who would have felt themselves seen by a king and a god.

Amenhotep seems also to have been a notably uxorious pharaoh. His wife Tiye is prominent on monuments and sometimes shown at the same scale as her husband, an exceptional statement of equality. When Amenhotep declared himself to be the incarnation of the sun god Re‑Horakhty, Tiye, too, was deified. Together they began to shift the focus of royal worship to the Aten, the golden disk of the sun.

Akhenaten followed in his parents’ footsteps, dedicating himself to the worship of the Aten, but then, in a profound break with tradition, he eliminated the rest of Egypt’s crowded pantheon. When he declared himself to be one with the Aten, Akhenaten fused not just with a god, but with the god. His wife Nefertiti joined with both the king and the god in a divine triad.

In Amarna, Akhenaten’s new, Aten-focused capital, the art was as peculiar as the theology, offering both the most naturalistic and otherworldly creations in the Egyptian canon. The workshop of the sculptor Thutmose (not to be confused with the pharaohs of that name) contained faces so rough and unflattering that they are thought to have been cast from life. Yet that same workshop created the famous bust of Nefertiti now in Berlin, one of the world’s most acclaimed visions of idealized beauty.

Massimo Listri / Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco
Akhenaten, Nefertiti, and two of their daughters appear as eccentrically curved figures in a scene of familial intimacy, a departure from the heroic and rigid style of royal tradition. 

In place of the heroic proportions and rigidity of royal tradition, statues of Akhenaten emphasized eccentric curves—narrow shoulders, swelling hips, carefully detailed sagging tummy flesh, and a face at once cadaverous and voluptuous. The writer Thomas Mann used the analogy of “an aristocratic young Englishman of somewhat decadent stock; spare, haughty, weary,” but the stylizations could reach buglike extremes. A stela included in “Treasures” shows Akhenaten, Nefertiti, and two of their daughters as wasp-waisted, with bulbous thighs and spindly arms. And yet the affectionate way the two little girls hold hands seems entirely natural. In the context of royal Egyptian art, such scenes of familial intimacy were a novelty, and remain touching.

All three maverick kings did everything in their substantial power to shape their image and control their legacy. It didn’t go as planned for any of them. Hatshepsut had been dead for 20 years when her co-regent and successor, Thutmose III, began erasing her from pharaonic history. The cartouches inscribed with her name were shaved down, her statues smashed and dumped in a quarry pit and in a nearby depression that excavators in the 1920s dubbed the “Hatshepsut Hole.”

Earlier Egyptologists, unable to imagine an honorable woman with power, saw this iconoclasm as a nephew’s revenge on the “vain, ambitious, and unscrupulous” aunt who’d cheated him of his birthright. In the midst of our current culture wars, we might read general misogyny into it, or hostility toward gender nonconformity. The favored explanation among contemporary Egyptologists, however, is dynastic: The aging Thutmose was aware that Hatshepsut’s side of the family had grander bloodlines than his side did, and represented a potential threat to the ascendancy of his son. Traditionally, such women were married off to the heir to gin up his claim, but Hatshepsut’s successful kingship had set a disruptive precedent. How nice, he may have thought, if people simply forgot that she had ever been pharaoh.

Earthquakes and the death of hieroglyphic literacy during the Roman empire completed the job Thutmose had begun. Hatshepsut’s name was forgotten; her memorial temple was rededicated to healing gods and later became the Coptic monastery of Saint Phoibammon, before falling into ruin. Its walls remained mute until 1829, when Jean-François Champollion, following his Rosetta Stone triumph, visited the site and puzzled over the pairing of an image of a male pharaoh with texts using feminine grammatical endings.

Egyptologists are still sorting out what happened at the end of Akhenaten’s reign. Names came and went in quick succession; Nefertiti may have claimed the throne for herself or one of her daughters. When the young Tutankhamun (thought to be Akhenaten’s son or nephew) became pharaoh, he wasted little time in restoring the old gods. Within a generation, Akhenaten’s temples had been dismantled; within two, his name no longer appeared on the official list of kings. None of his dozens (possibly hundreds) of public statues survives intact. Akhenaten’s suppression of the divine pantheon lasted perhaps 17 years, but the disappearance of Akhenaten lasted more than 3,000. Not until the late 19th century did living people again know anything about him, thanks to excavations at Amarna sparked, so the story goes, by a farmer’s chance discovery of clay tablets.

As for Amenhotep III, his legacy wasn’t attacked, just recycled, as generations of successors helped themselves to his monuments (so much more convenient than quarrying stone from the ground). The most insatiable was Ramesses II, so it is fitting that millennia later, the impending arrival in London of part of one of Ramesses’s colossi provided Percy Bysshe Shelley with what has become a byword for futile vanity:

And on the pedestal these words appear:
“My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:
Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!”
Nothing beside remains. Round the decay
Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare
The lone and level sands stretch far away.

Of all the priorities that governed ancient-Egyptian life, perhaps the hardest for us to fathom is the expenditure on the afterlife, not merely as a place of immaterial spiritual reward, but as a world of material goods. If “Treasures” glitters more than “Maverick Kings,” it’s because almost everything came out of tombs and cemeteries. Those tiny gold animal amulets were found in the necropolis at Naga ed-Deir; the gold toe coverings from the mummy of Psusennes I speak for themselves. These things endured because they were hidden away for use by their dead owners and the gods. Meanwhile, the great public efforts—the colossal statuary, the expansive propaganda programs, the image campaigns, the attempts to remake the world of living people—have been shattered, defaced, and refaced. The only unbroken funerary figure of Akhenaten to have come down to us is smaller than a Barbie doll, and isn’t gold.

Long before he became famous as the author of The Anxious Generation, Jonathan Haidt was a social psychologist whose work revolutionized the field. In this episode of The Permanent Questions, taped in front of a live audience at last month’s Atlantic Festival, David Brooks talks with Haidt about his biggest lessons from 40 years of studying human nature. David and Haidt discuss the role of the emotions in moral decision making, how to design a system that produces less loathsome elites, and why AI is “the neutron bomb for education.”

The following is a transcript of the episode:

David Brooks: Today we’re joined by Jonathan Haidt, who is a social psychologist at New York University, and these days he’s best known for The Anxious Generation, a book on what the phones are doing to our kids. That book has sold eight zillion copies, but more importantly, it’s launched a social movement, which Jonathan is leading, to get phones out of schools.

It’s been a phenomenally successful social movement. I get to visit high schools, maybe a dozen a year, and every one I go to has either banned phones from schools or is in the process of working out some arrangement to reduce the access to phones in schools. Everywhere I go, the kids love it, the teachers love it. It’s having a huge positive effect on our schools.

Jonathan and I go back a couple decades. When we first became friends, he was working as a social psychologist at the University of Virginia, and I was a baby pundit. And over the last 40 years, Jonathan has observed and also taken part in what you might call an intellectual revolution.

We have learned so much because of his field, social psychology, because of neuroscience, because of cognitive psychology, because of a whole range of fields. We have learned so much about human nature. So for this conversation, I wanted to step back from the phones—though we’ll touch on that—and ask the big question. We’ve learned a lot about human nature over the last 30 or 40 years. What’s important here? What are the key things we should understand about what human beings are and how we work?

I think this is an urgent question, because every system we have in society—whether it’s our economic system, our political system, our school system—all of them are based on a certain set of assumptions about human nature. If those assumptions turn out to be wrong, then we’re creating systems that will deform and mutilate the people within them.

One more thing you should know about this conversation. It’s a little different. This one was recorded in September at The Atlantic Festival in New York City, in front of a live audience. I was thrilled to welcome Jonathan Haidt to The Permanent Questions.

Jonathan Haidt: My oeuvre. (Laughs.)

Brooks: Or “Hoover,” as we say in New York. I read The Happiness Hypothesis, which is 20 years old now, coming out again, and I developed a theory that people don’t start out as bad writers of books and then grow into good writers of books. They start out as good writers of books and keep going. That book really is exceptional.

Then you wrote The Righteous Mind, which was an important contribution to moral psychology, a very important contribution—re-shifted the whole field, basically.
