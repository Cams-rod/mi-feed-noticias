The campaign-style ad starts with grainy video footage of President Trump, followed by a clearer look at his red-bathed profile, as his voice promises: “Together, we will defeat communism, socialism, and Marxism in America.” Then comes a strobe-style rapid display of images of the president. A pause. Then a dozen more Trumps. The pattern repeats several times. There are plugs for some of his priorities—such as cutting taxes and defending police officers—but one message stays on the screen for fully half of the 30-second commercial: AmericA will never be a communist country.

Throughout, an R&B song plays in the background, with falsetto voices singing, over and over: “Love me … Love me … Love me …” At the end comes this: “Paid for by the U.S. Government.”

Huh?

If the use of taxpayer dollars seems odd—and it should—the explanation that the White House gave me is even more bizarre. No one would speak on the record, but a White House spokesperson told me that the new ad, which began airing this week, is a public service announcement that is “educational and unapologetically patriotic.” A second White House official told me that the ad is “clearly not political,” an assertion that appears intended to provide legal cover. I also received a list of supposedly comparable PSAs from past presidents: George W. Bush spent federal dollars urging the youth of America not to do drugs. Joe Biden had a campaign to encourage the public to get their COVID vaccines. Franklin D. Roosevelt produced radio programs and posters supporting World War II. And now Trump has his own announcement of sorts: “Love me … Love me … Love me …”

“These public service announcements are about reminding Americans to love their country and understand what makes it worth defending, at home, at our borders, and abroad,” the White House spokesperson told me in a statement.

[Read: Trump plans to buy midterm ads about his own record ]

The White House would not answer my other questions, including about how much money is being spent on these “public service announcements,” which government account is being used to fund them, and who produced the spot. It is unclear how much money the government has spent on these ads and in how many places, but one advertising tracker I spoke with told me that the ad is airing in several major markets. It’s much easier to track such details for political ads, because campaigns and other groups are required to file reports with the Federal Communications Commission. The “Love Me” spot aired last night during Jesse Watters Primetime on Fox News, and it has also been seen on Newsmax.

The ad flashes text in big red letters spelling out Trump’s alleged accomplishments: Largest tax cuts in history, reigniting AmericaN manufacturing, and defend law and order and police. At the end, the voice of Dana White, the president of Ultimate Fighting Championship, comes on as images of military planes fly overhead. “He is the toughest, most resilient person that I’ve ever met, and nothing is going to stop him from fighting so hard for the country that he loves,” White says. A shorter version of the ad appeared on the White House’s official YouTube page nearly two weeks ago.

Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina, a Republican, compared the ad to something that a certain far-right former Hungarian prime minister would attempt. “It’s not like they need a GoFundMe page to have the dollars to do that sort of ad, and they could do it,” he told reporters. “But using taxpayer dollars, it feels like Viktor Orbán.” Representative Jamie Raskin of Maryland, the top Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee, said that the ad appears to violate federal law. “It is blatantly unethical under numerous federal ethics provisions proscribing the use of government resources for political campaigns,” he wrote on social media.

Senator Maggie Hassan of New Hampshire, who is poised to become the top Democrat on a key oversight committee, sent a letter today to Susie Wiles, the White House chief of staff, with detailed questions about the cost, source of funds, and legal basis for the spot. “The advertisement does not have a clear official government purpose and appears to run afoul of federal prohibitions against the use of appropriated funds,” she wrote. Top Democratic appropriators also wrote to demand that the ad be taken down immediately: “This is the sort of government propaganda one might expect in North Korea, not the United States of America.”

Donald Sherman, the president of the watchdog group Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, told me that it’s “certainly odd” and unlike anything he has seen before. “It goes out of its way, it seems, to look and feel like propaganda as opposed to something more substantive,” he said.

[Read: The watchdogs are barking themselves hoarse]

Sherman said that the ad likely does not violate the Hatch Act, which limits political activities for executive-branch employees but exempts the president. It more likely breaches federal appropriations laws, Sherman said, that restrict federal agencies from using taxpayer money for “publicity or propaganda.” Any enforcement would come from the Government Accountability Office, which operates at the request of congressional committees that are presently controlled by Republicans. A GAO spokesperson told me late this afternoon that the office had not received any requests for decisions on the matter.

“That’s the rub!” Sherman said. “This video is directed at benefiting the current majority in Congress, and the current majority in Congress has repeatedly refrained from reining in the current president’s corruption and behavior. I’m not holding my breath for them to do anything about it.”

Although I received few answers from the White House, I did receive some from JMSN, pronounced Jameson, the Albanian American artist behind the song used in the ad. He told me in an emailed statement that he had not authorized the use of his music, which has become a popular soundtrack on social media for users editing together their own photos. “I am looking for a lawyer now in order to see what our options are and how to proceed,” he said. “I have never been in this situation before, so need help in navigating something of this magnitude.”

Trump Should Stop Hosting Summits

State visits are a normal and necessary part of diplomacy. National leaders should, from time to time, sit with one another in person and have candid conversations. Summit meetings between the American president and his counterparts from Moscow and Beijing are particularly tricky because of the innately tense relationship between two adversaries. Food and hospitality are part of the festivities, but in the end, such summits provide important human contact at the highest level, and as a result, crucial business can get done that might not be possible over a telephone or in messages delivered through ambassadors.

At least, that’s how these visits are supposed to work. In the Donald Trump White House, summits are hazardous excursions during which the interests of the United States are shoved aside by a president whose cringe-inducing admiration of dictators makes these meetings into rituals of American humiliation. The country would be better off if Trump just stopped hosting or attending them.

Summits are a problem in this administration for two reasons. The most important is that Trump’s unpredictability and obvious lack of preparation make them dangerous. State visits require a lot of political groundwork, and leaders rarely go into top-level meetings without a set of issues they’ve already agreed to discuss. This agenda-setting gets done long before a president actually sits down with another head of state so that stray asides (or rising tempers) do not derail whatever business needs to be discussed.

The grim outcomes of previous summits with Trump, however, suggest that they are seat-of-the-pants affairs, with little preparation, or perhaps with preparation that was ignored by the president. Some of them, such as Trump’s previous meetings with Xi Jinping, have been brief talks alongside overseas events (such as at the G20 summits during his first term) when Trump improvised efforts to mitigate the damage from his needless trade wars. And some of them, such as his meetings with the North Korean strongman Kim Jong Un and the Russian dictator Vladimir Putin, have been fiascoes.

[Read: Xi is getting what he wants]

These summits with dictators produced outcomes that were worse than nothing, in part because Trump is so susceptible to pomp and flattery. After seizing the dubious honor of being the first American sitting president to visit North Korea, Trump returned and glowed about how he “fell in love” with the young menace running that prison state. Kim probably thought it had gone well too; he has since been able to build up his nuclear arsenal while watching Trump go to war with Iran over a nuclear program that has not yet produced a single weapon. Trump even admitted last summer that his nuclear-armed friend in Pyongyang will be “fine” as long as America has “a smart president” such as him.

No one, however, has taken Trump to the cleaners more times than Putin. Trump seems to both venerate and fear the Kremlin leader, and when they are left in a room together, things do not go well for the United States. After their 2018 meeting in Helsinki, Trump stood next to Putin and told the world that he accepted the word of a former KGB spy over reports from the U.S. intelligence community. And this was at a time when the president had a Cabinet and staff far more willing to tell him hard truths than his current circle of sycophants.

When Trump met Putin in Alaska in 2025—well, let’s stop right there for a moment. The President went to Alaska to meet a Russian leader, rather than hosting him at the White House or meeting him in a third country. This location allowed Putin to fly in and drag Trump in front of the cameras to force him to listen to Putin reiterate Russia’s brutal stance on Ukraine, and then—adding a true insult—Putin turned down the nice lunch Trump offered so that he could hop back over to Russia. Both men barely had time for a cup of coffee. What Trump said to his Russian counterpart we may never know, but this was worse than not meeting at all: Trump looked weak and hapless, while Putin looked determined and in control.

And that’s another reason to call off these summits while Trump is in office: They are humiliating in almost every respect. They make the United States look like a supplicant instead of a superpower, and they showcase the president’s unstable personality.

Like Putin before him, Xi got the red-carpet treatment on his arrival at Joint Base Andrews yesterday. This is an honor, as my colleague Jonathan Lemire noted this morning, that American presidents usually reserve only for the Pope. But Xi got an honor guard and a military flyover. Trump, who insists on saluting during the national anthem instead of placing his hand over his heart as is traditional, looked up and visibly winced at the sound of the jet overhead, while Xi, who seemed largely bored with the whole business, didn’t flinch. (Trump, for some reason, also wore a long overcoat and gloves on a seasonable day with temperatures in the mid-60s.)

The optics of meeting the leader of the world’s largest autocracy on a red carpet was bad enough, but then Trump did another thing that can be a problem at such moments: He decided to speak off the cuff. The president of the United States reportedly joked to the Chinese autocrat about how the Chinese press corps had the “friendliest” reporters—this, on a day when American reporters were fighting with Trump in court over their right to enter the White House.

Once at the White House, Trump took Xi for a quick tour. Trump had long ago replaced Joe Biden’s portrait in the hall of presidents with a picture of an autopen, a petty jab from a small and petty man, and on their walk, he made sure that Xi saw it. He even gave the Chinese leader a bit of a pat on the arm while yukking it up.

[Read: The deferential president]

This was too much even for the conservative writer Charles Cooke of National Review, who posted on X about Trump’s “grotesque behavior.” Xi, Cooke said, “is a communist dictator, and the head of a foreign state. Our internal divisions are not fair game in this context.”

But Trump does not think in these terms. His summits are a national hazard precisely because Trump apparently does not understand that he is representing the United States; instead, he seems to think he’s just having a visit with some of the guys in a very cool club of which he’d very much like to be a member. His attempts to ingratiate himself are obvious and pathetic, and they function as a giant tell to America’s opponents: This president likes you, fears you, and wants your approval.

These summits can have serious ramifications. John F. Kennedy and the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev had a brief, informal meeting in Vienna in 1961. Khrushchev looked at the 44-year-old president, judged him to be weak and inexperienced, and berated him over various issues. (“He just beat the hell out of me,” Kennedy later said.) Kennedy had mostly himself to blame; he ignored the concerns of his advisers, and he was unprepared for the older and more wily Khrushchev. They never met face-to-face again during Kennedy’s two remaining years in office.

The Vienna meeting was a disaster and may have encouraged the Soviets to take more risks, especially in Germany and Cuba. But that meeting was practically a model of presidential statesmanship compared with the incompetence and weakness Trump projects at all of these meetings with foreign dictators. The only time he seems comfortable pushing people around is when he meets with allies, including at a NATO summit in 2017, where he literally pushed the prime minister of Montenegro out of his way during a photo op.

The president has said that he wants to discuss AI with the Chinese leader, but Trump’s recent comments suggest that he has little idea what he’s talking about on that subject. Perhaps others will step in: Tonight, Trump will host a state dinner in Xi’s honor that will include guests from top U.S. technology firms, who actually know something about AI. And then, when the toasts and the meetings are over, the best we can all hope for is that Xi returns to China without incident. Then this White House can return to its normal chaos—but at least out of the sight of visiting adversaries.

With the midterm elections just 40 days away, Republicans are running hard—many of them away from President Trump.

Representative Maria Elvira Salazar, a Florida Republican whose district Trump won by 15 points in 2024, recently released an ad critiquing the administration’s immigration enforcement, saying that Hispanic voters feel “betrayed.” Byron Donalds, the GOP’s nominee for governor of Florida, scrubbed most references to Trump from his website—a notable move considering that the foundation of Donalds’s entire career has been fidelity to the Donald. The Senate contenders Mike Rogers of Michigan and Ashley Hinson of Iowa are both calling for an end to the Iran war. Tom Tiffany, the Republican candidate for governor of Wisconsin, has even suddenly changed his mind and now believes that Joe Biden won the 2020 presidential election.

The number of prominent Republicans distancing themselves from Trump has accelerated, or at least reached a critical mass, in the past 10 days. The shift is interesting—first, as an indication of what information GOP candidates are receiving privately about the president’s unpopularity and its drag on them, and second, because public critiques of Trump from Republicans have been vanishingly rare, no matter how unwise, outrageous, or unpopular his actions have been.

But the defections are perhaps better understood as a sign of normalcy, even in a deeply abnormal context. A president’s party almost always loses seats in a midterm, a result of the pendulum effect in elections. And the dynamics of this race are especially challenging for Republicans because Trump is historically unpopular, outpacing even his own poor ratings in 2018, when Democrats dominated the midterms. Trump is now presiding over persistent inflation, exacerbated by a failing war of choice in Iran. All of this predicts that GOP candidates would be wary of Trump.

News coverage about members of a president’s party trying to run away from him appears like a brood of cicadas, in this case popping up nearly every four years. You can easily find clips about Democrats trying to disassociate themselves from Bill Clinton in 1994, Republicans recoiling from George W. Bush in 2006, and Democrats avoiding Barack Obama four years after that, then again in 2014. (The 1998 and 2002 midterms were atypical because of the Clinton impeachment and a post-9/11 boost for Bush, respectively.) Though it’s mostly forgotten now, some Republicans tried to separate themselves from Trump in 2018, too, despite his effort to make the election about him. (“Pretend I’m on the ballot,” he told voters. After the voting, he blamed Republican losses on the defectors.)

Watching endangered officeholders try to run away from their party’s president every four years reminds me of a moment in Arrested Development in which a couple considers an open marriage. Lindsay asks whether it ever fixes a relationship. “No. It never does,” her husband, Tobias, replies. “These people somehow delude themselves into thinking it might, but … but it might work for us.” In a few isolated, marginal cases, a candidate successfully manages to separate themselves from the unpopular president and his party. In 2010, the Democratic Senate candidate Joe Manchin of West Virginia recorded an ad in which he fired a rifle at an Obama-backed carbon bill and won, but Manchin had years of credibility as a centrist governor to draw on.

In aggregate, however, these efforts don’t work: The usual pattern is that a president’s party loses seats. Because hard-core partisans are more likely to represent safe seats, they are also more likely to survive big midterm waves, whereas many moderates who distance themselves from the president lose because they are more likely to occupy contested seats. One reason that so few Republican officeholders have spoken out against Trump during the past eight years is that even the ones who were willing to criticize Trump lost their seats in 2018—their attempts at distancing weren’t enough to overcome the voter backlash against Trump.

Sometimes, the in-party loses seats for reasons largely beyond their control, such as big macroeconomic currents. But some of the specific cases where Republicans are breaking with Trump are on issues where the GOP as a whole is aligned with the president. If the party retains congressional control, a few scattered critics won’t be able to change the consensus. Hinson may be sincere in her desire to end the war in Iran—though her repeated votes against reining it in, including one just last week, raise doubts. Regardless, voters have no reason to believe that giving Republicans another two years of control of Congress would help end the war, given that the GOP House and Senate leaders have tried to sidestep resolutions calling for an end to the war and have opposed any actual restrictions on the White House.

Or consider Salazar’s shot across Trump’s bow on immigration. The idea that Trump is going to walk away from strict immigration enforcement, his signature issue for a decade and one dear to his base, is absurd, and House Republicans as a whole have shown no interest in restraining him. Salazar said on Face the Nation this past weekend that Trump was “very receptive” to her message, but Trump himself told reporters, “I’m very strong on the borders, and she isn’t.” The mystery is whether Salazar is gullible or just hopes that her voters are.

Many things about this moment in American history are unsettling and novel, and Trump’s threats to disrupt the elections themselves continue to cast a shadow over the midterms. But watching old patterns repeat is a reminder of how normal the dynamics of this campaign have been. Nearly every indication is that Democrats will win control of the House; the real questions are how large their victory will be and whether they can gain control of the Senate as well. In this environment, many Republicans can’t do much to save themselves. But because Trump is likely to blame losing candidates after the election anyway, rather than examine his own role, they don’t have many reasons to hold their criticism.

As far as journalism assignments go, Margaret Pratt Allen was handed a fun one: Investigate whether women can be sports fans.

“For two years the New York World gave me costly tickets to the season’s major sporting events so that I might observe and report whether or not women were there, and what seemed to be their reactions and interest,” she recounted in the May 1928 issue of The Atlantic. “I saw the Yankees and the Giants clash for the pennant, and I watched Firpo knock Dempsey into the press box and Dempsey knock Firpo into oblivion (at least I had a $27.50 ringside seat from which I was supposed to see this), not to mention other less thrilling affairs.” (The latter refers to the infamous heavyweight championship fight between Jack Dempsey and Luis Ángel Firpo in 1923—adjusted for inflation, Pratt Allen’s ticket would’ve cost more than $500 today.)

Sports fanaticism was on the rise in America in the 1920s, when superstar athletes (ever heard of Babe Ruth?) drew crowds of tens of thousands of both sexes, across a variety of sports. Pratt Allen acknowledged herself to be “a pretty good baseball fan as women go,” eager to applaud a good play and posssessing extensive knowledge of the sport rivaling that of her male companions. Nevertheless, her Atlantic story on women and athletics was constrained by the gendered assumptions of her time. Title IX was half a century away. Women had begun to compete in the modern Olympic Games only at the turn of the century, initially limited to more “refined” activities, such as tennis, croquet, and golf. And when they did show rousing enthusiasm watching games, they were criticized for their behavior.

Hoping that women’s presence could temper the boisterous behavior of male attendees, and aware that women too wanted to spectate and spend money at games, some baseball teams started regularly hosting “Ladies’ Days,” offering them free or cheap entry (though in some cases they were required to be accompanied by a man). As these promotions took off, so did complaints about the female fans: that they cheered “at the wrong times,” that their presence caused more disruption than it kept in check.

Pratt Allen disagreed, but in the form of a backhanded compliment. “The stuff that fans are made of—frenzied, hysterical, pleading, shouting, swearing fans—is not of the same piece as the pattern from which women are cut,” she wrote. At the Dempsey-Firpo boxing match, women were “conspicuous by their absence or their uncomprehending blankness in the face of the mob frenzy which gripped the male fans,” she explained. And women would never become so caught up in the game as to lob things from the stands: “Plenty of men would sacrifice brand-new headgear to the greatness of the moment, but can you imagine a ball game meaning more to a woman than a hat?” Sure, they “go because it’s the thing, these days, to be interested in all sport,” but ultimately, as the headline declared, “Women Aren’t Fans.”

Pratt Allen’s essay nods at the perennial bind women find themselves in: Damned if they cheer; damned if they don’t. Even if a female spectator “was just terribly glad St. Louis had won, her grown-up self, her real woman self” would keep her jubilance in check, Pratt Allen explained. And when women and girls have engaged in unrestrained fangirl behavior, they have been routinely mocked and denigrated for their joy. In 1964, one writer called Beatles fans “who scream themselves into hysteria” “the least fortunate of their generation.” In 2012, a critic referred to a One Direction album as “a reliable shriek inducer in girls who have not yet decided that shrieking doesn’t become them.” Modern fandom in particular has turned Pratt Allen’s assertion about “the stuff that fans are made of” on its head. The fangirl familiar to us today—shouting herself hoarse for the likes of Taylor Swift or BTS—might indeed be “frenzied, hysterical, pleading,” in Pratt Allen’s eyes. What is a girl to do?

In other words, the question is not whether women can be fans, but whether they are seen as the right kind of fan. The behavior of the female spectator ringside, courtside, in the stands, or in the gallery has long confounded those observing them. And here is where I come clean as a fangirl myself. I have screamed with abandon at concerts, but I have also held back, wondering if such enthusiasm befits a professional woman in her 30s with a mortgage and a baby. The truth is, women have long been fans. But what remains unclear is how a woman can ever be a fan in the way that she wants to be—without judgment not only from others, but also from herself.

In February 2024, according to a former staff member who told the story to The New York Times, Representative Mary Peltola, an Alaska Democrat running for the Senate, pushed an aide. As the Times put it in a report published yesterday, Peltola “shoved a male employee in the chest with one hand, causing him to stumble backward.”

You might wonder why the paper of record is reporting this detail. Did the employee then tumble off a cliff and die? The Times does not mention any bodily harm, beyond the backward stumble. The encounter is instead offered as evidence of what the Times calls “a campaign and congressional office marked by chaos.” Chaos.

The attempted exposé of Peltola is the latest installment in a familiar genre. Democratic staffers (they are almost always Democrats) complain off the record about the private behavior of their boss, who is almost always female, toward them. The behavior in question ranges from inappropriate to, more often, somewhat inappropriate to completely appropriate.

One notorious example concerns former Representative Katie Porter of California, who shouted, as she was being filmed, “Get out of my fucking shot,” at a staffer. Although nobody enjoys being shouted at, there is a certain urgency about getting the staff out of a camera shot that is supposed to focus exclusively on the principal.

[Marc Novicoff: Democrats’ great Alaskan hope]

The classic of the genre involves Senator Amy Klobuchar, who was deemed abusive by staffers in 2019, as she was mounting a presidential campaign, for episodes such as berating them for bringing her a salad on a plane without including a fork. Klobuchar was reduced to eating her salad with a comb. The story was supposed to make Klobuchar look unreasonable, if not crazy. To my mind, it reflected poorly on the staffer who did not think to procure the fork.

The Times’ Peltola exposé labels the shove as “one of the more notable incidents described by former aides.” This raises the intriguing question of what the less notable incidents entail. The story details several.

In one episode recounted by the Times, Peltola grew frustrated with a prep session for interactions with the news media. The story gravely notes that Peltola “picked up a water bottle and pretended it was a microphone, shoving it toward a staff member as she berated her.” The bottle did not strike the target, but it reportedly came within an inch of doing so, according to “text messages sent at the time.” One can only imagine the carnage Peltola narrowly averted. (Two other people present at the time described the exchange as “lighthearted.”)

Peltola’s reign of terror includes text messages requesting more campaign signs. In pursuit of this goal, which seems quite reasonable, she reportedly texted, “BIG SIGNS NEED TO BE SENT TO SOUTHEAST,” and, “OUR SIGN GAME IS FUCKED.”

Peltola also wrote disparagingly about a progressive organization. “I hope Planned Parenthood knows that if we don’t win it’s more their loss than ours. FUCK THOSE BITCHES,” she texted her staff in January 2024, according to the Times, which published an image of the text. And, apparently referring specifically to one Planned Parenthood board member, Peltola later wrote, “I hope she dies of a botched abortion.” This was an obvious, if hostile, joke. The anecdote suggests that the staffers are aggrieved by Peltola’s political leanings as well as her managerial failings.

The centerpiece of the story revolves around the care and feeding of Meatball, the office lizard. The Times presents this piece of the puzzle in the tone of Watergate-style disclosures, complete with details about sourcing: “Meatball’s preferred diet consisted of live crickets, according to three staff members familiar with the demand, including two who fed the chameleon.” You can almost imagine Ben Bradlee inspecting the copy and asking skeptically why the third source was not directly involved in the cricket-feeding. (I don’t know the answer. I was not in the parking garage where this anecdote was presumably shared.)

The Times elaborates upon the indignities this task entails: “On at least one occasion, the crickets escaped in the office and had to be recaptured, according to two former aides who witnessed it as well as a photo viewed by The Times that showed aides in the office trying to trap the crickets using manila folders and small paper cups.”

A naive reader might conclude that this episode is harmless farce, or perhaps reflects haplessness on the part of the staff. Did the crickets get loose through some elaborate Escape From Alcatraz plot, or did the staffers simply fumble the job? (One suspects the latter.)

The Times explains that, to her staffers, this task “exemplified the disregard Ms. Peltola appeared to have for her employees.” Was Peltola supposed to feed Meatball herself? This expectation would seem to make poor use of her comparative skill advantage, not to mention burden her with a frequently impossible task, given her need to travel between Washington, D.C., and Alaska.

The story does contain one bona fide example of improper behavior—an allegation that she sometimes used the slur faggot. Four former staff members report directly hearing her use the word. If true, it certainly reflects poorly on her. The Peltola campaign denied that she’d used the slur.

[Mark Leibovich: The Democrats aren’t built for this]

Yet the overall impression the story conveys is not an office beset by chaos, but one rife with staffers burdened with unrealistic expectations of workplace norms. Berating subordinates is a poor management practice. But it does not constitute chaos or the level of abuse that would justify a national news story. The fact that staffers for Peltola as well as for other Democratic politicians apparently categorize being scolded by their boss as a level of scandal requiring them to alert the media reflects a solipsistic tendency.

Staffers working for Democrats—but not for Republicans—now commonly use the national media as their human-resources department. They also use the media to publicly denounce their boss over matters of policy. Democratic staffers have sent letters demanding action on impeachment, climate change, and, on multiple occasions, the Middle East, among other issues.

Democratic staffers like these seem to believe that their role is to serve as a kind of moral check on their boss, to issue public dissents when a politician fails to uphold their preferred stances. If that is the position they imagine for themselves, then they probably take umbrage at being asked to feed her lizard. Nobody wants to be shoved by their boss. The question is whether that kind of behavior is worth going—anonymously—to The New York Times. More important, these Democrats should come to terms with the reality that sometimes, when you work for a busy, prominent person in a high-stress environment, your job really is to feed the lizard.
