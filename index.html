If at first you don’t succeed, appeal to a higher federal court. And if you even lose at the Supreme Court … just try again anyway.

This seems to be President Trump’s approach to limiting birthright citizenship. Last year, he issued an executive order that purports to end the practice of automatically granting citizenship to babies born in the United States—as has been the norm since the late 19th century. Despite Trump visiting oral arguments in an apparent attempt to intimidate the justices, the Supreme Court struck down the order this June.

Yesterday, Trump attempted to do some of the same things via new executive orders. One is intended to deny automatic citizenship to children born to people living in the United States while working for a foreign government and children of people declared to be terrorists or adversaries of the U.S. It would also deny citizenship to people born in U.S. territories if Congress passes a proposed law allowing that, creating a new group of stateless people. The second order is intended to prevent the practice of “birth tourism,” in which people visiting the United States on tourist visas give birth to babies who are then U.S. citizens.

Legal experts and advocates say the orders are unconstitutional, and they are certain to be challenged in court. Trump’s loss at the Supreme Court seems to make these orders a long shot. As Chief Justice John Roberts wrote in his opinion for the Court, “children born of parents unlawfully or temporarily present in the United States” satisfy the relevant conditions and thus “under the Constitution, they are citizens at birth.”

The language in the orders about “alien enemies” is another likely area for dispute, not because anyone is eager to welcome the children of bona fide adversaries but because this administration has made absurd claims about illegal immigration constituting an “invasion,” which makes no sense on its face and has been rejected by federal judges.

Trump’s announcement of the orders at the White House yesterday was full of fabrications, exaggerations, and lies. Trump claimed that the Fourteenth Amendment “was meant for the babies of slaves. It was—it was right after—one week or two weeks after the Civil War ended.” In fact, it passed three years later, in 1868. The presidential adviser Stephen Miller echoed the same idea, saying that the amendment “had no other meaning and purpose outside of that, and that’s been clear, obviously, since the day that it was passed.” When a politician uses the word obviously—like frankly—that’s a good sign that what follows will be neither obvious nor true. If this had been obvious since 1868, the U.S. wouldn’t have acted otherwise for more than a century, and the Supreme Court would have ruled in favor of the administration.

Trump’s claims about birth tourism were even further off base. The president isn’t making the idea up—just wildly exaggerating it. Asked by a reporter how many people are engaging in the practice, Trump said, “I can tell you right now. Hundreds of thousands. Hundreds of thousands.” This number appears to be pulled out of thin air. During Supreme Court oral arguments, Solicitor General John Sauer, arguing on behalf of Trump, was asked how commonly birth tourism occurs and replied, “No one knows for sure.” In 2020, the Center for Immigration Studies, which supports much stricter laws, estimated the number at between 20,000 and 26,000 births each year, out of roughly 3.6 million babies born in the United States.

By attempting to end or restrict birthright citizenship in order to address these issues, Trump is trying to use a bulldozer to achieve a task that calls for a small mallet. The law already prohibits fraudulent visa applications, and the Justice Department has prosecuted people for birth tourism in the past. Trying to undermine birthright citizenship risks jettisoning one of the fundamental bases of modern American society to deal with a minor challenge.

Trump could have tried another path. Justice Brett Kavanaugh offered the White House a potential lifeline in his concurrence in the birthright case, suggesting that despite the plain language of the Fourteenth Amendment, Congress could pass a law altering birthright citizenship. The likelihood of such a bill succeeding seems very low, and pushing it through would require sustained attention from Trump. Instead, he’s trying to achieve his goals through executive orders, which are both easily reversed by a future president and have already proved unsuccessful.

But the president has a demonstrated weakness for this kind of grand, overreaching presidential gesture. Last month, after a member of the U.S. men’s national soccer team was suspended for a game in the World Cup, Trump decided to use his position to exert some pressure, calling FIFA President Gianni Infantino to try to get the suspension waived. In a direct sense, it worked—FIFA allowed the player to participate—but it may have backfired. Belgium, the American opponent, was incensed, and trounced the United States 4–1. Trump ended up looking obnoxious and corrupt, but didn’t even get a victory out of it. The player in question, by the way, was Folarin Balogun—a U.S. citizen by birth because his mother, while pregnant and visiting family, got stuck in the United States.

A Chatbot-Free Childhood Will Be a Status Symbol

By Dana Suskind

The human brain has evolved to learn from specific instructors. When a child hears a gentle spoken word or a soothing lullaby from another person, when their cry is responded to, their brain lights up. Nurturing interactions, or what child-development experts call “serve and return” exchanges, help shape and strengthen the 1 million new neural connections taking place in a child’s brain every single second. Every time a child cries or serves up a bid for attention and receives a caregiver’s response, that back-and-forth helps build the circuits underlying their communication, learning, and emotional well-being. No other input, not even Johann Pachelbel’s Canon in D performed by the Berlin Philharmonic, is capable of activating the neural circuitry the way human interaction does.

“How are you on theory?” my editor asked me earlier this week. I knew what he was talking about—not my pet hypotheses about, say, the existence of cryptids, but rather a group of 20th-century French philosophers. Yet I didn’t know much else beyond that. I was familiar with Michel Foucault, partly from college coursework but largely from a popular meme that features his most famous concept, the panopticon. I know about Roland Barthes and the death of the author. I know that Derrida, Lacan, and Deleuze are names that I should know.

“Not good,” I told him.

First, here are four new stories from The Atlantic’s Books section:

  • Icarus in the faculty lounge
  • Someone is mysteriously snapping up used books around the world.
  • What if Freud was right?
  • “Out Here,” a collection of poems by the Black Seed Writers Group

I could read their works for myself, of course. I could pick up Foucault’s Discipline and Punish at any one of the college bookstores that serve four major universities in my city. But frankly—and I don’t think I’m alone in this—I’d rather not. My preference as a busy adult is to begin my approach to foundational, complicated ideas with the help of a particular kind of book: the intellectual biography.

The structuralists would hate this. Lynn Steger Strong makes exactly that point in an Atlantic essay published this week about a new group biography of these thinkers. I may be oversimplifying their ideas (as Strong writes, any “smooth summary inevitably sacrifices complexity”), but in general, they disregarded biography as a source of useful information about art; Barthes, especially, rejected literary criticism that drew on an author’s life to unlock the meaning of their work. “There was only the text, over which the writer had little control,” Strong explains. “Even the self was ultimately an unstable text.” This makes a biography of the cohort feel almost sacreligious. Emily Eakin’s new book, The Frenchmen, focuses on these men’s personal lives to illuminate their earthshaking writings. After finishing it, Strong wonders: “How could biography not be important?”

I agree. Important ideas don’t just come to Earth fully formed and then flow through their speakers; they’re influenced by the wider context in which they develop. Earlier this year, when I was curious about the life and work of Karl Marx, the first book I reached for wasn’t Capital. Instead, I read Francis Wheen’s 2000 biography of the man—yes, I learned, he did ask Frederich Engels for money immediately after Engels told him that his beloved partner, Mary, had died. And yes, he really did spend ages in the British Museum’s reading room trying to squeeze as much information as possible into Capital (which is not to say that he didn’t miss anything). The biography also reminded me that Marx did not invent socialism; he was building on an intellectual tradition that predated him by decades.

I encourage readers to try this out for themselves. Back in 2022, Talya Zax recommended nine group biographies that explain not only the lives of their subjects but also how those individuals influenced other people and movements. Personally, I’m on to a new topic: I’m about five chapters into Kai Bird and Susan Goldmark’s forthcoming biography of Roy Cohn, American Scoundrel, and the world of 20th-century New York City—an environment that incubated hundreds of famous and influential leaders, including our current president—is unfolding before me.

What to Read

The Haçienda: How Not to Run a Club, by Peter Hook

The Haçienda in Manchester was a catalyst of the U.K. acid house scene in the late ’80s, and a prophecy foretold: “The haçienda must be built,” the Situationist poet Ivan Chtcheglov wrote in 1953. Heeding these cryptic words some three decades later, the audacious (and well-read) impresario Tony Wilson opened the Haçienda together with the circle of post-punk musicians and designers involved with his label, Factory Records. Their attempt to decipher Chtcheglov’s mystical phrase lasted 15 years. Hook, the bassist for New Order, served as a kind of player-coach at the Haçienda, helping manage its madcap affairs while his band became the club’s cash cow. In this memoir of misbegotten business administration, Hook returns to the storied nights out that changed British culture even as they threatened to bankrupt him—and worse. Beset by gangs and guns, the Haçienda faltered in the ’90s despite clever-sounding schemes such as replacing the club’s security with the gangsters themselves. This is a scrapbook of utopian folly, yes, but also an insider’s look at what was, for a time, the wildest workplace on Earth.  — Andrew Holter

Do Not Paint Granite!

By Dorothy Robyn

History and aesthetics aside, Trump’s plan violates a key rule of building maintenance: Do not paint granite. Granite is a superior building facade that has been used for other distinctive structures in Washington, D.C., such as the Library of Congress and the Old Post Office. Left unpainted, granite requires little maintenance. Once granite is painted, however (and examples are rare), it requires extensive maintenance in perpetuity. In their fledgling but fascinating career thus far, the director Jane Schoenbrun has been consumed by the pop culture that consumes us. They’ve already proved to be an urgent voice on the matter: Their first feature, the low-budget We’re All Going to the World’s Fair, focused on the allure of online “creepypastas” and weird YouTube rabbit holes that despondent teenagers might go down. The second, I Saw the TV Glow, examined the psychological fallout of two isolated adolescents obsessed with a cult television series. Schoenbrun’s newest movie, Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma, is similarly interested in nostalgia: It follows Kris (played by Hannah Einbinder), a queer filmmaker tasked with rebooting a gruesome slasher franchise from yesteryear called Camp Miasma. As she does so, the past and the cynical present blend into a transfixing dilution of Schoenbrun’s interests and fears about identity and self through the prism of mass media.

Schoenbrun’s previous films were intensely melancholic affairs, and it’s immediately clear that Camp Miasma is having a bit more fun with the same themes. It jabs at Hollywood’s impulse toward rampant intellectualization—and corporatization—of the cinematic junk food of decades past. Kris, who has a minor Sundance hit under her belt, endeavors to create a “woke” and hip take on Camp Miasma without alienating its enduring fan base. A charmingly stylized early montage establishes the franchise as akin to many 1980s VHS cult classics, garishly mixing gore, nudity, and an eerie campfire vibe. The film’s many sequels yielded diminishing returns, and the series eventually stooped to wacky concepts à la Friday the 13th—the main monster, Little Death, who carries a spear and wears a mask shaped like a cubic air vent, traveled to Manhattan, then space, and so on. At her hotel, Kris watches the original film and eagerly takes notes; during a cheesy sex scene, she scribbles DESIRE in her notepad and underlines it forcefully.

Yet Kris, despite her intent to take a cerebral approach, also comes at the project with a teen sensibility. Any movie nerd might recognize her behavior: When she stops off at a gas station on a work trip, she grabs fistfuls of candy and then lazily spins through the cheapo DVD rack, excitedly calling a friend when she finds a junky Camp Miasma installment for sale. The opportunity to visit the now-abandoned sleepaway camp where the first entry was shot is both useful to her as a director and delightful to her as a fan.

[Read: Make culture weird again]

The film mostly takes place there, and Kris spends a bizarre weekend with Billy Presley (Gillian Anderson), a reclusive actor who starred in the original Camp Miasma but refused to participate in any of its follow-ups. Billy, however, now lives at the campground with the goal of giving herself what appears to be a peculiar form of exposure therapy. Anderson, herself a towering legend of genre television and cinema, is an imperiously fun and sexy figure for Einbinder’s nervous and dorky character to bounce off of. Billy seems both enticed and exhausted by Kris, who professes her struggle to bring an academic eye to the world of Camp Miasma. Billy reminds her that the franchise’s initial appeal was straightforward: The draw was “flesh and fluids.”

As Kris spends more time with Billy, Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma metamorphoses from an industry satire into a dreamlike reflection on how what we watch can shape us, no matter how lowbrow. Schoenbrun renders the desolate grounds with beautiful matte-painted backgrounds; as Kris and Billy watch the old film, the fuzzy visuals and synthy score bleed away into contemporary CGI blood and carnage. Both the throwback and present-day imagery look surreal; even though Kris can identify the obvious tropes in Camp Miasma, she can’t seem to face the power that familiar scripts hold over her. And in a fantastical twist, Little Death somehow finds its way out of the Camp Miasma movies, emerging from the on-site lake.

But this is not a tale of cinema coming to life and invading the mundane real world; it’s more about two different styles of storytelling blending into one. As past and present, “fiction” and “reality” swirl together for an inscrutable final act, Schoenbrun finds a fresh way to make an incisive observation about 21st-century life: Today’s moviegoers are immersed in their screens and hungry for pop culture, to the point that a trashy flick such as Camp Miasma can feel intrinsic to Kris’s sense of self. Someone like Kris may want to interrogate that relationship over and over in search of meaning, but perhaps it’s best not to subject what we watch to endless analysis and meta-critique. Instead, we may be better off embracing how we feel.

The Internet Is in Crisis

Almost 20 years ago, I had tickets to see Amy Winehouse perform in London, at a show that was canceled just an hour before it was scheduled to start. A friend and I went to a nearby hotel bar instead, and not much later, in walked Winehouse herself—tiny, grubby, and completely incoherent. She walked over to us and stared, her eyes both furious and vacant, until someone in her entourage came and dragged her away. I remember the person who flashed to mind in that bizarre moment: the gossip blogger Perez Hilton.

If you weren’t regularly online during the late 2000s, it’s hard to explain how dominant a presence Hilton was—how incessantly he posted about what celebrities might be doing on any given day, how shameless he was about posting upskirt photos, how judgmental his tirades about young women in crisis were. His fiendish MO was lifting pictures from paparazzi agencies and then scribbling penises or obscene epithets on them in order to claim fair use. Hilton—whose real name is Mario Lavandeira Jr.—was particularly cruel about Winehouse, who was being photographed by the celebrity press almost daily in states of disarray. He posted, by turns, about her physical appearance (“super skinny, dirty and wasted”), false rumors of her pregnancy (“We were sure that her ovaries were too gin-soaked to be of any use”), and a performance in which she wasn’t wearing underwear (“Grrrossss!!!!!!!!! Who needs to see cracked out snatch?”).

PerezHilton.com was relentless and brutal, and its readers could not get enough, especially when the blog’s coverage collided with moments of calamitous celebrity breakdown. (When the actor Lindsay Lohan was arrested in 2007, the site reportedly received 8.8 million pageviews that day.) Since then, the internet has metastasized into something officially kinder—we no longer berate famous people in crisis for sport quite as much as we used to—and quietly more appalling, where irony-poisoned young men broadcast their executions and social-media companies seem to basically shrug in response. And so, on Tuesday night, during what seemed to be a mental-health breakdown of his own, Hilton was able to stream himself live on TikTok cutting himself with a knife for about 15 minutes before his broadcast was finally interrupted.

Any number of Hilton’s followers on TikTok could have clicked on the stream thinking they were going to see a standard broadcast about Ariana Grande or Trader Joe’s ready meals (both of which Hilton had posted about on YouTube in recent weeks). Instead, they got a scene of unimaginable violence. “I am fine with horror/gore, have seen a lot real and fake, and this was so deeply disturbing I could not get the image out of my head all night,” one commenter posted on Reddit. Another added, “I watch cartel torture and beheading videos with no issues. I had to turn the perez video off after about 20 seconds, and I felt deeply disturbed for hours.”

TikTok’s failure to interrupt Hilton’s broadcast for such a long time, even as horrified viewers were reporting it, is astonishing. (A spokesperson for the site told Wired that a “moderator error” caused a delay in removal; another told Vanity Fair that Hilton was persistent in his attempts to broadcast what he was doing, using several different streams to do so.)

[Read: The unspeakable, enabled]

In the United States, Section 230 of the Communications Act shields technology and communication companies from being held legally responsible for third-party content that appears on their platforms. And since Facebook launched livestreaming in 2015, social-media sites have struggled to establish safeguards for atrocities being broadcast in real time, to the point where the phenomenon of people livestreaming their own attempts to die has become “an emerging public health crisis,” in the words of psychology researchers. After a man livestreamed himself killing 51 people at two mosques in New Zealand in 2019 on Facebook Live, Facebook said it was investing in “technical innovation” to prevent people from abusing its platform. Nevertheless, footage of mass shootings and other atrocities proliferates globally before befuddled tech companies can tie their shoes.

The tone of the contemporary internet is also darker and more nihilist than it has ever been. “Social-media platforms—and especially X—have loosened their grip on moderation at the same time that AI tools have allowed for the easy proliferation of slop,” my colleague Charlie Warzel wrote earlier this year. “Never before has there been so much cynical, cruel content and trolling.”

But the idea that Hilton might end up turning self-harm into content feels particularly fraught. A statement from his family posted online said that the influencer had been “experiencing a severe mental-health crisis,” and that his three children, his niece, and his sister were at home with Hilton when he began hurting himself, although they left immediately “to protect the children from witnessing any further trauma.” Over the past decade and a half, Hilton has tried to pivot away from the bilious celebrity coverage that made him so successful: In a 2010 video, he announced that he would no longer be cruel. But his popularity never quite recovered, and he argued in his 2020 memoir, TMI, that the toxicity of his site was what had appealed to his readers, even if they didn’t want to admit it to themselves: “The more snarky names I gave the celebrities, the more penises or coke or boogers I drew on pictures of them, the more people visited my page.”

It’s also true that PerezHilton.com peaked in its cultural relevance around 2007, the year that celebrity after celebrity seemed to be breaking down in public while pursued by paparazzi on mopeds. That year, Britney Spears shaved her head (when the actor Heath Ledger died in 2008, Hilton sold T-shirts emblazoned with the words Why Couldn’t It Be Britney?), Anna-Nicole Smith overdosed on prescription medication, Lohan was arrested for cocaine possession, Paris Hilton went to jail, and Mischa Barton was arrested for driving under the influence of alcohol. Hilton documented each of these moments in vivid and elaborate detail—my browser finally quit when I got to page 79 of his archives related to Amy Winehouse—because he understood what people wanted from him and his work. “When Britney Spears was at her wildest, people were glued to my website,” he wrote in TMI.

It’s impossible to know whether this awareness informed what he did to himself this week, and how he did it. Hilton’s breakdown is desperately sad; I’m haunted by the idea of what his children might have seen happen in their home, but also by the idea of what all children might see, at any time, in this hopelessly corrupted and seemingly unsalvageable internet.

Conversation has become optimized—but not always for the better. We send a quick text instead of making a call, react with an emoji instead of a laugh, and turn catching up into a series of efficient updates. Although staying in touch with people is easier than ever, we spend surprisingly little time talking to one another.

“No one picks up the phone anymore,” Alexis C. Madrigal wrote in 2018. That’s in part because too many faceless telemarketers have our number, and in part because more people have come to treat phone calls as something to reserve for emergencies or bad news or major life events. However, in many cases they’re the easiest way to have conversations that texting can’t quite convey—ones that meander, surprise you, and leave you feeling a little more connected than before.

Texting has made communication faster and more efficient, but not necessarily more conversational. Today’s newsletter is a reminder that you don’t need a reason or an agenda to call someone you’ve been thinking about.
