​​​​​​​This story was updated at 4:35 p.m. on November 14, 2026.

Jensen Huang is the kind of target that spies dream about. Intelligence agencies work for years to develop a source who can get so close to such a powerful man. That the former Nvidia CEO is also friends with Donald Trump makes him even more valuable. Viewed one way, the Huang operation is painfully simple: It demonstrated the power of human-intelligence gathering and the weakness of the male ego, one Western official told me, somewhat admiringly.

Much remains unknown about how China’s Ministry of State Security might have enlisted Alice Huang (no relation), who was having an extramarital affair with the tech titan, or what information she might have provided. Chinese officials have dismissed the U.S. allegations that she is an MSS asset as baseless smears, and U.S. officials have not presented the evidence they believe shows her affiliation. But experts on China’s intelligence tactics told me that she was probably not a trained operative who set out to entrap the Nvidia boss. More likely, they said, the MSS learned she was having an affair with Huang and then recruited her. Under China’s National Intelligence Law, citizens are required to assist the MSS when asked. And the agency doesn’t always ask nicely. MSS officers are known to coerce potential assets by threatening to seize their property or harassing their family members, former officials told me.

For more than four decades, China has relentlessly stolen U.S. secrets—in cyberspace, outer space, and in the flesh. China’s hackers tend to get the most attention because they have penetrated countless computer networks, vacuuming up commercial and official secrets and laying the groundwork to potentially cripple communications systems in the event of a war with the United States. But China’s spymasters have also created an extensive human network, recruiting business executives, university professors, students, and even an educator in California who later became mayor. “China is going after technology and innovation as part of a national strategy to develop their economy and strengthen their national security,” David Cohen, the former deputy director of the CIA, told me.

Beijing is acutely focused on AI as it navigates its rivalry with the U.S., one that has led American military planners to warn about the outbreak of a potential war. China’s president, Xi Jinping, has called for “an explosion of original and disruptive innovations” in AI and other fields, including quantum computing and biotech.

If a case officer were to make a list of who has the most influence over the development of AI and the rules governing it, Huang would have been at the top—at least until this week. “He’s not a surprising target,” Connor Forman, a former China analyst at the CIA, told me. “He has insight into the company and U.S. policy aims, needs, and goals. He’s of tremendous value to a nation-state, beyond chips.” The MSS would presumably have been looking for any avenue it could find into Huang’s world. Alice Huang “may have just been the right person in the right place at the right time” to provide the access that the MSS wanted, Forman said.

“I’m not at all surprised by it. This is what China does,” Alan Kohler, the former head of counterintelligence for the FBI, told me. “Someone hit a home run here, because the target was Jensen Huang. But China is also likely targeting dozens of low-level engineers that no one has ever heard of at Nvidia and other tech companies.”

Alice Huang was reportedly running a company called Megaspeed that rented computer processing power to companies in China. Chinese authorities could conceivably have threatened her business or, conversely, have offered to help her if she assisted them by spying on Jensen Huang and people in his orbit. U.S. and Chinese intelligence agencies wield very different tools. CIA officers don’t threaten Americans into cooperating or use honey traps—despite what paperback spy thrillers and Hollywood blockbusters might portray.

Chinese espionage in the United States is pervasive, current and former officials told me. But ex-CIA officers also described their tradecraft as “horrible” and “clumsy,” particularly in cyber operations.

After Xi became president in 2013, he reoriented Chinese intelligence to serve the country’s long-term strategic goals and put a stop to espionage by members of the military for their personal gain, according to a report by the Center for Strategic and International Studies. The think tank conducted a survey of publicly reported spying incidents from 2000 to 2023 and found that intelligence activity has increased significantly in the years after Xi came to power. Computer hacking was “China’s preferred mode” of clandestinely gathering information.

[Read: Will Taiwan just give up?]

In 2015, the scale of China’s cyberspying on U.S. companies became so intolerable that Barack Obama and Xi agreed that their countries would refrain from spying on each other’s companies for commercial advantage. It was a one-sided deal, because the United States already had an official prohibition against economic espionage—stealing foreign corporate secrets for the benefit of American companies. Only a year later, though, China was back to its rampant computer hacking, current and former officials have told me.

But China hasn’t limited itself to hacking; it also uses traditional methods such as recruiting agents. The country’s intelligence apparatus often blurs the line between civilian and military worlds. Nearly half of all incidents that CSIS examined involved Chinese military or government employees. But 41 percent involved private citizens.

“China uses what they call ‘nontraditional collectors,’” such as students and businesspeople, Anna Puglisi, a former U.S. national counterintelligence officer for East Asia, told me. “They view their diaspora worldwide as potential resources.” Only about 10 percent of the operations tracked by CSIS involved non-Chinese actors, usually U.S. persons recruited by Chinese officials. By contrast, the CIA works overseas to recruit foreigners to spy on its behalf.

China and the United States are in an all-out AI race, and the Huang affair may be an example of the no-holds-barred approach the country takes to accessing AI chips and technology from the U.S. But winning doesn’t necessarily mean that China will be the first nation to develop artificial superintelligence or the most sophisticated models. “It means that China incorporates this technology better and more effectively than the U.S. can,” Forman, the former CIA analyst, told me. For example, AI could help China’s military make decisions faster on the battlefield, which has historically been a weak spot compared with the United States, Forman said.

[Read: NATO’s nightmare]

To build better AI systems, China needs the best chips. “Chinese AI chip capability still lags behind the United States,” Harry Krejsa, the director of studies at the Carnegie Mellon Institute for Strategy and Technology, told me. Eventually, China would like to produce its own cutting-edge chips, but in the meantime, he said, it has to obtain that technology through other means, including espionage or smuggling.

Krejsa, who worked on technology and security issues at the White House and in the Pentagon, said he views the Jensen Huang incident as further evidence of how hard China is working to gain advantage. “I caution against seeing this as some game of 3-D chess, as opposed to a world-class intelligence service being opportunistic any chance it gets,” he said. “Any intelligence organization worth its salt is trying to get close to people” like Huang.

Alice Huang may have helped China in other ways. At the same time U.S. officials suspect she was working with the MSS, Megaspeed, the Singapore-based company where Huang was an executive, had been under investigation by the Commerce Department for suspicion of shipping Nvidia chips to China in violation of U.S. export rules, former officials told me. Officials who run those kinds of investigations have regular access to relevant reporting from the intelligence community, but it’s not clear whether they knew about Huang’s suspected MSS affiliation.

[Read: Would Claude refuse an illegal military order?]

Trump reportedly was warned twice—the first time in 2025—that the U.S. believed Jensen Huang was having an affair with a suspected MSS asset, but he gave no instructions to notify the Nvidia chief and took no obvious actions to disrupt China’s spying. The New York Times reported last month that Huang was the suspected target of China’s spies. “If the U.S. government knew about this relationship for over a year, that sounds very long to let this play out without an intervention of some sort,” said Kohler, the former FBI counterintelligence chief, who now runs Pamir Consulting, which advises U.S. companies on Chinese influence. Former officials told me it was possible that the U.S. intelligence community wanted to continue monitoring Alice Huang, her company, and her contacts in the MSS. But that could have put Jensen Huang, Nvidia, and valuable U.S. technology at risk.

That wasn’t the only oddity in the administration’s approach to Nvidia. Not long after Trump was first informed about the affair, the U.S. government agreed to allow the sale of some Nvidia chips to China, in exchange for a 25 percent tariff on the transaction. That arrangement, which had no precedent, reversed the administration’s existing policy and ran counter to a long-standing strategy of keeping advanced AI chips out of China. Critics said the administration was helping China get ahead in the AI race, the opposite of what it purported to want. The White House countered that making China more dependent on American chips was smart policy and would keep the U.S. in the lead. But if the president knew that Nvidia might have had a spy in its midst, why was he helping the company do more business in China? The new Democratic majority in Congress will certainly want answers to that question and the many others that remain in the Huang affair.

Measles is undeniably back. The disease, which was declared eliminated in the United States in 2000, is once again endemic, the Pan American Health Organization reportedly decided at a meeting this week.

PAHO made that determination based on a set of technical standards, and its official declaration is expected early next week. Those criteria say that measles is endemic when the same strain of the virus causes continuous infections for a year straight; even before this week’s meeting, publicly available data indicated that this was likely the case. But really, the evidence has been clear for some time now: The U.S. is in the midst of a measles crisis unlike any it’s faced in decades. More than 5,000 cases in 45 states have been confirmed this year. Pennsylvania started reporting measles deaths this August; its count is now up to seven. New York has had one death. Wisconsin reported its second yesterday.

This is already America’s worst year for measles since 1991, which marked the end of a three-year epidemic in which 123 people died. That surge helped spur a national vaccination push. This time, the outbreak has unfolded on the watch of a health secretary who has declined to accept responsibility for its ongoing spread.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has dismissed Democrats’ attempts to blame him for measles’ return. In April, he told a Senate committee that “the measles epidemic began before I came into office.” That’s true: Measles had already begun to spread in West Texas by January 2025, before Kennedy became health secretary. And routine childhood-vaccination rates, including the measles immunization, have declined since the coronavirus pandemic, which disrupted doctor appointments around the country. The U.S. isn’t alone in seeing cases rise. In January, six other countries—including Austria, Spain, and the United Kingdom—lost their elimination status. Measles was declared endemic in Canada again last year.

But this is Kennedy’s crisis now. He’s been the nation’s top public-health official for nearly two years, and the country’s predicament has only deepened under his leadership. The number of measles cases in the U.S. in the first 10 months of the year is more than double the total for 2025. Meanwhile, Kennedy and the Department of Health and Human Services have failed to send a clear message to Americans about the importance of curbing the virus’s spread. As one CDC official, whose name I’m withholding to protect them from professional repercussions, recently told me, “The response has lacked a clear strategy and support from the top.”

Kennedy’s CDC has offered help to states dealing with outbreaks. Recently, it’s sent staff to Pennsylvania, New York, and Wisconsin to assist with tasks such as contact tracing and surveillance. (In an emailed statement earlier this week, an HHS spokesperson cited that assistance and $100 million the CDC recently sent to states as evidence of the department’s “aggressive” response to measles.) But under Kennedy, the CDC’s response has also at times appeared constrained. The Washington Post reported that an alert that CDC scientists wanted to send regarding the risks of measles during pregnancy was not approved for unclear reasons. Meanwhile, at least some of the assistance the CDC has offered hasn’t been welcome. As Nicholas Florko reported in The Atlantic last year, Kennedy planned to send vitamin A to Texas, but health officials in the state had never requested any. (Vitamin A can help treat measles in children who have a severe deficiency but has not been shown to be effective for most Americans.)

Perhaps most dramatically, Kennedy and his HHS have fought with states over the precise toll that measles has taken. In August, after two infants died in Pennsylvania, Kennedy and Governor Josh Shapiro got into a public dispute over whether measles contributed to those children’s deaths. As the number of deaths reported by states has continued to rise, the CDC has so far declined to attribute some of them to measles; its dashboard currently lists only six fatalities.

Kennedy doesn’t talk about measles often. When he does, he tends to offer a tepid endorsement of the vaccine or downplay the seriousness of the disease. In 2024, during his failed presidential run, Kennedy—a longtime anti-vaccine activist—told an interviewer that he wouldn’t give his children the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine if they were born today. (Last year, he said he would “probably” do so.) He also claimed at the time that “kids who get the measles as a child are much healthier when they grow up,” which is not true. Last year, during an outbreak in Texas in which two young girls died, he offered only half-hearted support for vaccination while pushing vitamin A. As I reported at the time, Kennedy privately told the father of one young girl who had died of measles, “You don’t know what’s in the vaccine anymore.”

This August, in the midst of a rapidly growing outbreak in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, Kennedy said on CNN that “parents should get their children vaccinated for measles”—but only after the host, Dana Bash, asked him to encourage parents to vaccinate their children. (The HHS spokesperson wrote that Kennedy and CDC Director Erica Schwartz “continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles.”) Such a statement would be entirely unremarkable for any previous health secretary, but it felt notable coming from Kennedy. Since that CNN interview, Kennedy has devoted few words to measles and far more to mocking Shapiro and promoting other projects, such as his cooking show. One recent episode explored how to make a chickpea salad.

Kennedy has also kept mum on the many false statements about measles and vaccination from his allies and supporters. The anti-vaccine nonprofit Kennedy founded, Children’s Health Defense, published an article in September saying that “parents don’t need to be afraid of the measles” and asserting incorrectly that the virus can make children stronger. A week later, after four people had died in Pennsylvania, Kennedy told attendees at a Children’s Health Defense conference, “You have a strong and steadfast friend at the White House.”

None of this reflects well on the health secretary, but he has shown no sign that he’ll change course. (HHS did not immediately respond this morning to a request for comment on PAHO’s reported decision.) President Trump has been supportive of Kennedy’s vaccine agenda, and combatting measles was never a focus of Kennedy’s Make America Healthy Again movement.

In recent months, that movement has seemed to fray: One of the health secretary’s closest allies, Calley Means, quit his White House post in late October and then went on Joe Rogan’s podcast to complain about the influence of industry lobbyists in the federal government. The much-ballyhooed boost that MAHA would supposedly give Republicans in the midterms never materialized, either. Two of the movement’s most prominent candidates—Ken Paxton, the Senate nominee from Texas, and Zach Lahn, a candidate for governor in Iowa—lost their races by wide margins.

But MAHA, and Kennedy, may not be chastened. The new White House chief of staff, Stephen Miller, appears to be anti-vaccine curious. He has echoed President Trump’s call to split the MMR into separate shots; the vaccines are not currently available separately, and experts say splitting them up is unnecessary. And he’s complained, without citing any particular evidence, that pressure to “pump your kids full of shots days after birth” is a “medical travesty.” (The White House declined to comment on Miller’s vaccine beliefs.) Kennedy cheered Miller’s promotion and wrote on X that it was time to “unleash the Kraken.” What, exactly, he means by that is unclear. But a disease that’s arguably the most contagious in history has already been unleashed on the United States, and Kennedy hasn’t shown nearly enough interest in reining it in.

Scott Colom, the first Black U.S. senator ever elected by Mississippians, traces his lineage in the state back seven generations, to when the nation was forging its foundation on the backs of stolen Black people. He waged a campaign set on showing that “Mississippi matters,” which many registered more as a question than a declaration, doubting whether a Black man and a Democrat could win the seat. During the most expensive election cycle in U.S. history, his campaign coffers were empty compared with Democrats in other battleground races. Finally victorious on Veterans Day, he credited the state’s civil-rights titans and local people who work without an expectation of recognition for carrying on the “Mississippi faith tradition” of having hope, even—or perhaps especially—in dark chapters.

Many Americans might be surprised that Colom flipped what’s long been a deeply red seat. But this win is the product of decades of uphill, incremental, thankless, and overlooked labor powered by an ancestral charge and a conviction to reclaim Mississippi. My late stepfather, Obbie Riley, a five-term Democratic county supervisor in Mississippi, was among those who undertook that fight, although he didn’t live long enough to see its results. The state’s “faith tradition” that Colom evoked is as much about prayer as it is about remembering generations of bloodshed in Mississippi, where Black voting rights were redeemed by those who could only dream of what Colom has now achieved: finally turning his home state blue.   

“There were times when I was tired, and I felt like I couldn’t keep going, but I kept the faith,” Colom said during his victory speech Wednesday night. “And that is another tradition of our state. There’s so many people that had faith in Mississippi at times where it seemed impossible.”

That faith, and the hard work that must accompany it, was something Colom learned in his household, where he and his twin brother were reared by now-retired Judge Dorothy Colom, the first Black person to be elected to her region’s chancery court, and Wilbur Colom, an attorney who was once a Republican. Scott built on this pedigree, attending the University of Wisconsin to study law and returning home to the Columbus area, in the east-central pocket of the state, not far from the Alabama line. There he became the youngest and the first African American justice-court judge in Lowndes County, in 2011, and then Columbus’s first African American city prosecutor, in 2013. Later he took a bolder step, campaigning for district attorney in his early 30s.

[Adam Serwer: Even where MAGA won, MAGA lost]

If Columbus were anywhere else, it might be described as a natural incubator for a Democratic U.S. senator. It is a college town, home to the state’s women’s university; it’s also a vertex of a region known as “The Golden Triangle,” which is anchored by Mississippi State University and an Air Force base in Starkville. Colom ran for district attorney in 2015 as a reform candidate—and unseated a 27-year incumbent who’d defended debunked medical-examiner analyses that had led to multiple false convictions that were later overturned. Colom has been the region’s top cop for more than a decade; he instituted restorative justice and counseling for gun-violence survivors, among other reforms. In 2020, days after George Floyd was murdered, Mississippi’s attorney general suddenly dropped a police-shooting case that Colom had transferred to the state in 2016. Colom was outspoken about it, making parts of the case file public and expressing regret for handing the case over.

History is never fully in the rearview in Mississippi, and that was evident throughout the Colom campaign. On the day before he delivered his victory speech, at Tougaloo College, a historically Black school with a rich civil-rights history, Colom held a press conference wearing a mocha-brown hoodie that bore a quote from the civil-rights organizer Bob Moses: When you’re in Mississippi, the rest of America doesn’t seem real. And when you’re in the rest of America, Mississippi doesn’t seem real. Moses directed Freedom Summer, the 1964 effort to recruit hundreds of white college students to Mississippi to help open Freedom Schools, or voter-registration-and-education sites meant to overcome the ridicule, violence, and other tactics the state used to keep Black voters off the rolls. At the time, the state had the lowest Black voter-registration numbers, despite being the Blackest state in the U.S.

Training for the college students was under way when members of the Ku Klux Klan burned down Mt. Zion United Methodist Church in Philadelphia, Mississippi, after beating parishioners suspected of holding civil-rights meetings. Michael Schwerner and James Chaney, organizers who had done grassroots work at Mt. Zion, knew they had to leave Freedom Summer training in Ohio to check on the community. Andrew Goodman, a college student who volunteered for Freedom Summer, joined them on his first trip to Mississippi. On the afternoon of June 21, as the trio made their way through the center of Philadelphia, the deputy sheriff, a Klansman, pulled them over and arrested them. When they were released after nightfall, a group of Klansmen and law enforcement kidnapped them, killed them, and buried them in an earthen dam in Neshoba County, where an FBI search located them 44 days later.

This past summer, Colom made a campaign stop at the Neshoba County Fair, where politicians frequently stump. Ronald Reagan spoke there during his 1980 presidential campaign, emphasizing his belief in states’ rights, which many interpreted as a dog whistle, given the proximity to where the three civil-rights workers had been slain. Colom’s father worked for Reagan’s transition team. In a 1981 interview with The Daily Mississippian, the University of Mississippi’s student newspaper, Wilbur Colom criticized some of Reagan’s policies and said that the GOP of the ’50s and ’60s had been deficient in its support for civil-rights legislation, but that Black voters’ commitment to the Democratic Party shouldn’t be automatic either.

Wilbur’s son ran a campaign with a complementary refrain: Why should Mississippi automatically be considered a Republican stronghold, especially when Democrats have chipped away at GOP control in statewide elections over the past decade? When my stepfather died in September, he was the lone Black person and Democrat serving as a Neshoba County supervisor. And although that set him apart, he’s remembered for how he sifted through disagreements to move forward, even if the ideological overlap with his colleagues was the size of a grain of sand. Throughout his campaign, Colom likewise touted that he would work with anyone, Republican or otherwise; he even visited a Trump rally and spoke with people waiting in line. There’s a Mississippi brand of bipartisanship, one my stepdad practiced, that’s a mainstay especially in rural areas, where people of difference have found commonality even amid bitter political and racial tensions.

Colom’s win was propelled by grassroots organizing to register voters, get them to the polls, and, in some cases, help them return to certify or cure provisional ballots. The senator-elect outraised his opponent, the incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith, in the last quarter of the campaign (approximately $1.45 million to $864,000), but that was nothing compared with the money pouring in for other Democratic Senate candidates. Colom’s campaign had a “rich message but a poor treasury,” Representative Bennie Thompson said at the Tougaloo victory party, where he introduced the senator-elect.

With seemingly nothing to lose, Colom infused a little bit of fun into his campaign, producing a series of ads that showcased his sense of humor. One, called “Sinators,” parodied the Mississippi-based movie Sinners using Colom’s identical twin brother. In it, a Hyde-Smith look-alike asks for just one more chance to keep everything in Mississippi the way it is. Hyde-Smith, whose parents sent her to a segregation academy as a child, was caught on camera in 2018 saying of a supporter standing beside her that if he “invited me to a public hanging, I’d be on the front row”—in the state where more known lynchings occurred than in any other. (She later said that the remark was an “exaggerated expression of regard.”) She conceded the race after it was called on Wednesday, and congratulated Colom on his victory. The moment had a karmic quality: After President Biden nominated Colom to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi in 2022, Hyde-Smith single-handedly blocked him from the lifetime appointment, even though other statewide Republicans supported him for the role.

For a time, it looked as if the election results might face legal challenges. During the week-long vote counting, Hyde-Smith was asked about the possibility of fraud in the election and said, “There’s always irregularities.” In the summer of 2025, Mississippi shifted tens of thousands of voters to “inactive” status based on data from the credit-reporting agency Experian. DeSoto County, home to the white-flight suburbs of Memphis, inactivated more than 15,000 voters, more than anywhere else in the state. On Election Day, lines there lagged, and polling stations ran low on affidavit ballots. Colom won the county with about 53 percent of the vote; Hyde-Smith had won the county in her previous two elections.

The first time the Department of Justice used the now-gutted 1965 Voting Rights Act to protect white voters happened in Colom’s district, in Noxubee County, in 2005; Wilbur Colom represented the defendant in the case. A decade later, around the time that Scott Colom was running for district attorney, a DOJ lawyer from the 2005 case filed a lawsuit alleging issues with the Noxubee voter rolls. Instead of undertaking a costly fight, the county entered a consent decree that required a period of scrutinized voter-roll management. Noxubee and Lowndes, another county in Colom’s district, also saw voter inactivations surge in the months before the 2026 midterms. This living history is another reason many doubted whether Colom, or any Black senatorial candidate, could make it to Washington.

Political pundits have already compared this moment to the Barack Obama elections, which saw high Black voter turnout in Mississippi and elsewhere. Nadia Colom—the wife of the senator-elect, a real-estate agent, and former executive director of the Golden Triangle Boys and Girls Club—said in her speech on Wednesday that people have been calling her “Mississippi’s Michelle,” referring to the former first lady. “While it’s an honor, really and truly, to be seen in that light, what really means the most to me is that I get to be called Mississippi’s,” she said.

[Read: Why Black voters abandoned Trump]

Colom’s campaign rightly touts him as the first Black U.S. senator to be elected by Mississippians. Mississippi sent two Black senators—Hiram Revels, the nation’s first Black senator, and later Blanche Bruce—to Washington at a time when they were selected by state legislators. Black men also served as secretary of state in Mississippi for nearly a decade after the Civil War. In 1870, 14 percent of the state Senate and 47 percent of the state House of Representatives were made up of Black men. By 1874, nearly 80 percent of the state House was Black. Because Reconstruction showed such promise in Mississippi, the backlash that followed was orchestrated, acute, and violent.

A defining feature of Mississippi history is that white violence directly triggered an exodus. One historian estimates that from 1914 to 1920, during the Great Migration, one in five of the Black migrants leaving the South for economic opportunity, safety, dignity, and more came from Mississippi. White Mississippians waged yet more violence against Black folks who stayed, assassinating Medgar Evers, sickening and fatiguing Fannie Lou Hamer, and brutalizing everyday citizens into strange fruit. The remnants of that targeted violence are still visible today in the state’s stark inequality and lack of opportunity for Black Mississippians.

But Colom’s campaign challenges the narrative that Mississippi should be defined through the lens of white harm, and that those with aspirations are better off leaving. On Wednesday night, Nadia Colom said to her and Scott’s twin girls, eighth-generation Mississippians, that she hoped the race reinforced the family motto to never quit, a value instilled in their father, who passed it to them. Persistence is a precious inheritance in a state that often ranks dead-last in indicators of prosperity, livelihood, health, well-being, and education. Scott Colom didn’t quit when donors wouldn’t answer the phone, when fewer than 10 people showed up at his first town hall, when it took eight days to rightfully claim his victory. For 200 years and counting, his ancestors didn’t quit on Mississippi, toiling and resisting on the lands that he traversed during his campaign. Now he is reaping the reward of remaining.
