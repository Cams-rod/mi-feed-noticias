Okay, Sure, a Folding iPhone

Nothing quickens the pulse of an Apple fan more than these three words: one more thing.  Steve Jobs used the catchphrase to introduce the original iPhone in 2007. In his 15 years running the company, Tim Cook deployed it only a handful of times, including before his 2014 announcement of the Apple Watch. Today, nine days into his tenure as Apple’s CEO, John Ternus said the magic words. Apple’s next big invention, the company revealed at a launch event in Cupertino, is a $2,000 iPhone that sports—get ready—a “remarkable hinge.”

This is the iPhone Duo, a foldable gadget that opens like a book to reveal a capacious 7.6-inch screen. Billed as the most versatile iPhone, it can be used in folded or unfolded form, and in split-screen or full-screen modes. The screen appears seamless when open, which is a clever engineering feat. Yet an iPhone that blossoms into an iPad Mini seems unlikely to change many lives—especially at the price of a MacBook Pro. And foldable smartphones have been on the market since Samsung popularized the category, in 2019. In the pantheon of “one more things,” the Duo feels like a dud.

Apple also announced several other devices today, all pitched in superlative terms by executives to obligatory applause. The most interesting might be new versions of the Apple Watch that will listen for sounds such as sirens and doorbells, take notes on your conversations, or play back the past 15 seconds of what someone just said to you. (Exactly how all of that will work, and how well, is not yet clear.) There are also new AirPods with improved noise cancellation. The new iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone 18 Pro Max—the ones that don’t fold—offer “the longest battery life in iPhone history and the most advanced pro-camera system ever,” the company said. They also come in “a sophisticated and unique new color,” which is burgundy.

Apple may have once captured people’s imagination. Now it also captures a lot more of their money. On top of its hardware sales, the tech giant is raking in subscription fees to Apple Music, Apple News, Apple TV, iCloud, and more. Earlier this summer, the company announced price hikes on many of its products and a leasing program in partnership with Klarna for anyone who blinks at the sticker prices. One thing it didn’t announce today was the base iPhone 18, or any of its other less expensive models, which now seem likely to be released after the holiday buying season. Apple has begun to feel like a late-stage tech giant, squeezing residual value from markets it has long since saturated.

Ternus, an engineer who led Apple’s hardware division, arrives with the promise of reviving the company’s penchant for creativity. In his first company-wide memo as CEO, he told employees he couldn’t wait to “build the future” with them. The companies that are actually building the future these days, for better and worse, are the ones pushing the boundaries of artificial intelligence. Apple is not among them. If there is to be a real threat to Apple’s core business, it is likely to come from AI. For now, the iPhone is the entry point to users’ digital lives: messages, emails, websites, and apps. As chatbot users deepen their relationships with ChatGPT and Claude, those personal assistants could become the primary gateway to online tasks and information. At least, that’s what many in the AI industry believe. They are racing to build AI-first devices—pendants, glasses, speakers, and so on. Jony Ive, Apple’s former chief design officer, is working with OpenAI on what is rumored to be a smart speaker the size of hockey puck. Apple was irked enough that it sued OpenAI earlier this year, alleging that it had poached employees and pilfered trade secrets; OpenAI has denied wrongdoing.

Another candidate to build the first proper AI device is, of course, Apple itself. There were signs today that Ternus grasps the assignment: He led the event with a vision of the iPhone as an “intelligent personal hub” through which Siri and Apple Intelligence become the AI-powered command centers for all of the information stored on and sensed by a customer’s Apple devices—their calendar, contacts, messages, movements, and everything their iPhone, Apple Watch, and AirPods can see and hear. Those are, he acknowledged, “the most private details of your daily life.” That could work to Apple’s advantage; the company has cultivated a reputation for safeguarding users’ privacy. One problem: Despite years of promises of coming improvements, Siri is still not very good. Earlier this year, Apple agreed to pay $250 million to some iPhone 15 and 16 customers over claims that it had falsely advertised the AI capabilities of those devices. In January, Apple was reduced to contracting with archrival Google to supply the underlying AI for a revamped version of Siri. Even so, it is reportedly having some trouble getting app developers to buy in. (An Apple spokesperson didn’t respond to a request for comment.)

Being the boring tech giant right now has some upsides. Unlike the industry’s other titans, Apple is not lighting money on fire in a frantic race to build data centers and superintelligent AI. It also appears to be the tech giant whose products are the least likely to bring about the extinction of the human race anytime soon, for whatever that’s worth. Its emphasis on user privacy, with AI tools that keep people’s data on their device instead of sending them to the cloud, remains an important differentiator for those who care about such things. Maybe Apple can go on a while longer as the unchallenged king of fancy consumer-tech hardware while everyone else fights to the death over AI software and infrastructure. Maybe rebuilding Siri around Google’s AI will buy Apple time to eventually develop its own powerful models, the way it leaned on Intel for chips for years before creating its own. But if Ternus really wants to build the future, he will eventually have to do something that once came naturally to Apple when it was an underdog: take a risk.

When Donald Trump looks at a map, he doesn’t see nations. He sees property. All of those pretty colors do not represent actual places filled with human beings who have distinct cultures, languages, and histories; the planet, to Trump, is a big board game, like Risk or Monopoly. Greenland isn’t a real location—it’s a valuable chunk of territory nestled between Boardwalk and Park Place. The Strait of Hormuz isn’t an international waterway—it’s a thing to control somewhere between Kamchatka and East Africa.

This inability to grasp the existence of independent nations drives the president to fury when countries and their governments refuse to play along with his delusions. Trump cannot understand why Iran won’t bend to his will, why NATO won’t instantly obey his orders, why South Korea and Japan won’t just write checks or provide troops on demand. The only people Trump seems to respect are the autocrats in Beijing and Moscow and Pyongyang who, like him, view the world as a brutal competition for power and territory.

Now Trump has declared economic war on Canada, a nation that has vexed him for years. Even by the standards of Trump’s many fixations, his obsession with Canada has become not only weird but truly destructive. He has opened a giant political and economic rift with one of America’s biggest trading partners, a sister nation, and an ally in the common defense of North America and Europe. Why does Trump hate Canada so much?

We can only speculate about what goes on in the president’s head, but his actions suggest a pattern. When Trump is frustrated about something he can’t control, he lashes out at friends harder than he does enemies. He took out his anger about the Epstein files on legislators from his own party, even though he had campaigned on releasing those documents. Stymied by Iran on multiple fronts, Trump decided to blame South Korea for not helping him, and curtailed military exercises with a vital U.S. ally. (This decision also had the side benefit of allowing Trump to make another embarrassing overture to the North Korean dictator Kim Jong Un, a bromantic gesture that went unreciprocated.) Trump values absolute loyalty over everything else—not that he always returns it—and he detests any sign that others, especially those who are supposed to be his friends and allies, might have views and interests of their own.

The American economy is stalling, and Trump’s poll numbers are in the basement, so the next obvious move is to pick a huge fight with a nation that is essentially family to Americans. Canada is most definitely a foreign country, which is one reason I like to visit. (Go ahead—try to find a convenience store in America that sells Aero chocolate bars or wine gums.) But to the utterly incurious Trump, it probably looks like an extension of upstate New York.

Canada and America are bound by deep cultural and historical bonds, but ironically, the superficial similarities are likely driving much of Trump’s vitriol. How dare these people—whose country is, to him, just a cheap knockoff of America—defy him! After all, most of Canada speaks English; its cities look like ours (if definitely cleaner); we share pop stars and TV shows; they even call their money “the dollar,” despite its colorful look and plasticky feel. Canada, in the president’s eyes, is less a sovereign nation than a real-estate parcel he forgot to buy because it’s in a neighborhood he never visits.

The fact that Canada is led by someone Trump accidentally helped put in office probably irks him too. Early last year, Canada’s Liberal Party had been in power for 10 years and was facing a huge defeat in what seemed to be an impending “change” election. Trump, as usual, could not control himself when it came to the internal politics of an allied nation: He imposed tariffs during the lead-up to the Canadian vote while banging on about making Canada the 51st state. The Liberal leader Mark Carney—an unflappable public figure who is completely different from Trump in both temperament and intellect—pivoted quickly and campaigned on standing up to the American president. Carney almost overnight went from a no-hoper to prime minister. And now he has shown that he is perfectly willing to retaliate against Trump’s policies rather than fold. If Trump wants tariffs, Carney will provide them—in spades.

Imagine how all of this looks to Trump, who has made clear that Canada shouldn’t even exist as an independent state. I am humiliated as an American, and as a friend of Canada, to admit this, but Trump’s attitude toward Canada is almost indistinguishable from the way Russian President Vladimir Putin sees Ukraine: not as a state but as some kind of historical anomaly that should be returned to its rightful owners. Trump might think it’s funny to post memes of Canada with an American flag superimposed on it, but those jokes are a lot less hilarious to people in places such as Ukraine, who are fighting a war against a dictator who feels exactly the same way about their country.

Canadians are probably scratching their head over Trump’s willingness to hurt his own people and especially his own party. Trump may hate Canada, but it’s hard to imagine what he thinks he’ll gain by causing economic pain to Americans along the Canadian border, especially in states such as Maine and Michigan, where Republicans are facing tight battles for congressional seats. What the Canadians may not realize is that, with this president, none of it has to make sense, and that if recent history is a guide, he will back down, perhaps after declaring victory over Ottawa. Trump will then move on, leaving ordinary Americans and their Canadian friends to deal with the damage.

The Anniversary I Wish I Didn’t Share With My Husband

By Ellen S. Bakalian

During a visit in May to the Maritime Museum in Halifax, Nova Scotia, I watched a film about a 1917 cargo-ship explosion that killed more than 1,700 townspeople and injured many more. The grainy black-and-white images of the flattened city catapulted me back to the devastation of Lower Manhattan after terrorists attacked the World Trade Center on September 11, 2001. Halifax suffered a blizzard following the cargo-ship catastrophe, and the sight of the city covered in a deep blanket of snow recalled the dust and debris from the once-glimmering towers that coated the Financial District. When I heard the recorded voices of the Haligonian survivors recounting their memories, I walked out of the small theater to cry.

Twenty-five years on from September 11, 2001, an image or memory can still evoke the very moment I learned that my 36-year-old redheaded husband, Jeffrey R. Smith, would not be coming home from work that day.

Photographs by Sasha Arutyunova

Like most New Englanders of a certain generation, my parents viewed New York City with distrust. They associated their neighbor to the south with “The Bronx Is Burning,” Bernhard Goetz, blackouts, bankruptcy, and the famous New York Daily News headline Ford to City: Drop Dead. The heart of Manhattan was a decadent playground for the wealthy, while outlying neighborhoods were plagued by desperate poverty and violence. I grew up only 200 miles away, and yet my parents never took me there as a kid.

But if New York felt menacing and foreign to them, in reality, it is the most American of cities. New York is a symbol of the nation’s money and power, its diversity and freedom. It is our capital of culture, a proudly loud and bustling melting pot. And for those who wanted to humble the world’s sole superpower, New York was the perfect place to strike 25 years ago this week. Two of its signature skyscrapers were toppled, and nearly 3,000 lives were lost.

The response underscored how much New York belongs to America. Rescue workers from red and blue states raced to help. Donations poured in from rich California cities and small Montana towns. American flags were seemingly everywhere. For the first time in decades, and less than a year after a bitterly contested presidential election, the nation felt unified.

We’ve been chasing that feeling ever since. New York City has bounced back—revitalized and thriving—with a new skyline emerging from the dust. But the nation is deeply divided, with angrier elections and escalating political violence. The pandemic could have brought the sort of unity we felt after the terror attacks, but it instead largely fostered division and rage. There is a new conflict in the Middle East, one whose dubious origins evoke the misguided war launched there after the World Trade Center fell. And now, a quarter century after the September 11 attacks, a New Yorker is president, one who was in Manhattan that crystal-clear, late-summer day. But Donald Trump won’t be there on Friday.  

My first visit to New York City included the World Trade Center. I was in high school when, in 1995, my brother and I joined a church-group trip down from Massachusetts (my parents still wanted no part of it) to Liberty and Ellis Islands. I remember the ferry ride back to Lower Manhattan at the day’s end, staring at those impossibly tall towers. I was transfixed. Later, I was lucky enough to go to college in New York, and immersed myself in its energy. The Twin Towers were not exactly beloved architecture, but they were a constant, comforting presence; when you got out of the subway downtown, below the city’s street grid, the easiest way to orient yourself was to locate the World Trade Center. Find those towers, and you could get yourself anywhere. You could get yourself home.

Sasha Arutyunova for The Atlantic

On September 11, 2001, I was a 21-year-old intern at the New York Daily News and, with no money to my name, was between apartments and sleeping on my friend’s couch. It was Primary Day, and I had the morning off ahead of covering some comptroller candidate’s Election Night party. My friend’s nanny had the television on, and I heard her shriek. Al-Qaeda got what it wanted: As the world watched the first tower burn, the second was hit. I grabbed my notebook and tried to get to work. But I was staying uptown, at West 120th Street, and the subways were already a mess and soon shut completely. I couldn’t reach the newspaper’s assignment desk; the phones were overloaded. So I started walking south, toward the World Trade Center, trying to listen to updates on my portable radio. Not long after the towers fell, I connected with an editor who diverted me to a major hospital with a trauma center, to witness the arrival of the injured.

I was instead confronted by a jarring and heartbreaking image: rows of empty hospital beds. The scores of survivors never came; there simply weren’t many. The sky was incongruously beautiful. When the wind turned, I could smell the smoke, an acrid blend of jet fuel and steel and other things too sad to consider. My next stop, after nightfall, was a bereavement center set up farther downtown. I fielded as many questions as I asked and desperate pleas from frantic people—some covered in ash—looking for information about their missing loved ones. Soon, the city would be dotted with homemade signs about those who were lost, posters hung by families clinging to hope. Many would stay up for months.

On September 12, I slipped past the security perimeter and reached Ground Zero. I was there, off and on, for days, trying to describe the indescribable to the newspaper’s readers. The rubble continued to burn. Debris and body parts were everywhere. Firefighters refused breaks, as they dug for their fallen brothers. There was a pervasive sense of fear: It seemed as if anything could happen, including more attacks.

The four hijacked airplanes had attacked symbols of the United States’ financial might (the World Trade Center), its military (the Pentagon), and its democracy (the U.S. Capitol or the White House were the likely target of a fourth plane that passengers forced to crash in the Pennsylvania countryside). The sense of loss and disruption was overwhelming. Many nights, I cried myself to sleep. It felt as if life would never return to normal; even the small routines—going to an office, riding the subway, going to a baseball game—seemed newly fragile.

In the weeks that followed, I covered dozens of funerals. Traditionally, when a New York firefighter dies, much of the department attends. But that terrible fall and winter, many of the city’s firefighters were still working long shifts digging through the pile at Ground Zero. To make sure that each service was robustly attended, hundreds—and, at times, thousands—of firefighters journeyed from departments across the country. The funerals invariably included a version of “Amazing Grace” from the FDNY’s pipes-and-drums band. To this day, I have a hard time hearing bagpipes.

Sasha Arutyunova for The Atlantic

Yet even in those first hours, there were signs of hope. The city’s mayor, Rudy Giuliani, had woken up that morning as an unpopular lame duck, a self-styled political brawler whose success fighting crime had been overshadowed by his personal foibles and executive overreach. But with President George W. Bush largely out of sight that day, Giuliani became the face of a mourning, yet determined, city. His leadership in the weeks that followed was nothing short of heroic. He was followed into office by Michael Bloomberg, the billionaire businessman who led the city’s recovery and rebuilding (and personally financed much of the National September 11 Memorial & Museum). In bipartisan fashion, Congress passed a victims-compensation fund. Our NATO allies stood by us, invoking the Article 5 mutual-defense clause for the first and, so far, only time in the alliance’s history. Bush spoke out firmly against Islamophobia. The nation even rallied around the Yankees’ run to the World Series that year. (Okay, maybe not everybody rooted for them.)

Despite early conversations about whether anyone would ever want to work or live in Lower Manhattan again, businesses came back, and, in a surprise development, more people moved downtown. As for the World Trade Center site itself, there were certainly turf wars, differing visions, false starts and delays, and probably more new office space than really was needed. But it slowly grew again, anchored by the 1,776-foot-high One World Trade Center and the 9/11 memorial and museum. The tree-lined, eight-acre site was transformed into a peaceful park with two reflecting pools where the towers once stood. Those pools are filled by waterfalls and ringed by the names of the victims. Dennis Mojica. James P. O’Brien Jr. Cindy Yan Zhu Guan.

The rest of the city came back too. In the decades that followed, apartment prices skyrocketed, and young people transformed neighborhoods deep into Brooklyn and Queens. Crime plummeted, and the NYPD developed a counterterrorism arm. More skyscrapers soared above Manhattan. Broadway boomed. Tourists arrived in record numbers (and, yes, even my parents warmed to the city). There are still challenges, affordable housing and income inequality among them. But the city today is safer and more vibrant than ever. And in a development that would have been unthinkable a quarter century ago, the year’s September 11 remembrance services will include the city’s 34-year-old democratic socialist Muslim mayor.

The city’s communal response to the terror attacks was inspirational: People checked on loved ones and strangers; they donated blood; they saluted first responders. The tragedy also provided a template of sorts for the calamities that would lie ahead. Gone were the stereotypes that New Yorkers didn’t care about their neighbors, or would take advantage of disaster to loot or riot. In their place were scenes of regular people banding together to pull through the 2003 blackout, Hurricane Sandy, and the pandemic. During those fear-filled early months of 2020, One World Trade Center’s top would at times be bathed in red, white, and blue.

Sasha Arutyunova for The Atlantic

The city today sits in a far more divided nation. That initial feeling of national unity was dealt a series of body blows, including the Iraq War, the financial panic of 2008, and the racist backlash to the election of the first Black president. The country became angrier, more polarized, more red versus blue, more us versus them.

Donald Trump seized the moment. Over the past decade, he has widened the nation’s political and cultural fissures, eroded trust of American institutions, governed like he was president only of his supporters—and moved to punish those who didn’t back him. Giuliani, once heralded as “America’s Mayor,” embraced Trump’s lies about the 2020 election, drawing an indictment, disbarments, and a loss in a $148 million defamation lawsuit over his false claims about two Georgia election workers.

[John Avlon: What happened to Rudy Giuliani?]

Trump has never seemed all that moved by his hometown’s darkest day. Mere hours after the towers fell, he spoke to a local television station, saying that a building he owned had suddenly gained in stature. That building, 40 Wall Street, “actually was the second-tallest building in Downtown Manhattan, and it was actually—before the World Trade Center—was the tallest,” he said. (The claim was inaccurate, as well as insensitive.) Trump has attended the annual memorial at Ground Zero a few times, including in 2024, but will not do so again this week. The New York Times reported that the president is instead participating in a Pentagon event because Manhattan organizers would not let him deliver a speech. (By tradition, there are no speeches delivered at Ground Zero, only a solemn reading of the victims’ names.) It would also be easier for Trump to fly out of Washington to attend a golf tournament this weekend at a course he owns in Ireland. The White House denied that Trump had asked to speak.

Trump has long been incapable of bringing people together and uninterested in doing so. He frequently indulges in anti-Muslim rhetoric (emphasizing Barack Obama’s middle name, Hussein, and the full name of Michigan’s Democratic Senate candidate, Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed) and once proposed a travel ban for anyone of that faith. His war against Iran has raised fears of another terror attack. The relationship between the president and his hometown is broken; he rarely visits his Midtown penthouse and is greeted with protests when he does. In all three of his presidential campaigns, he lost badly in New York. And though he has seemed charmed by the city’s new mayor, Zohran Mamdani, that did not stop his administration from announcing the results of a deportation surge for the city—dubbed “Operation Rotten Apple”—just days before the solemn Ground Zero commemoration.

New York City has been abandoned by its most famous son. But it will push forward, and it won’t be alone. Former presidents from both parties are slated to attend the ceremony on Friday morning. The commemoration itself is meant for the victims’ families, and officials expect a larger-than-usual turnout. But once the event is over, and the temporary ropes come down, the memorial will again open to the public. I took my sons there earlier this week. We saw people from red states and blue ones, and heard languages from all over the world. Those visitors stared at the waterfalls, let their hands run over the names of those lost, and shared quiet hugs. That will happen again this September 11. And when night falls, the Tribute in Light will switch on and beam pillars of light upward. The illuminated towers will stretch to the heavens—and they’ll guide so many of us home.

Updated at 4:30 p.m. ET on September 9, 2026

Where were you on 9/11? Every American old enough to remember the day has an answer to that question. President Trump has several.

At a ceremony honoring first responders and victims’ families yesterday, Trump offered an elaborate account of where he was in the hours and days after nearly 3,000 Americans were killed. “I was building a big building in New York, and I took the whole crew down there right after this happened, and they had no hesitation; everybody went down with me. We went down as a big group of people. Wasn’t too much we could do, I’ll be honest. It was raw—nobody had seen anything like it, ever.”

The story presents significant problems in terms of its relationship to reality. Listeners could be forgiven for interpreting Trump’s remarks as a reference to his whereabouts on the day of the attack itself. (The president clarified that timeline last night: “I was there, obviously not on the same day as the collapse, but shortly thereafter, with a large group of Construction Workers,” he wrote in a Truth Social post.) It is true that two days after the attack, on September 13, Trump was interviewed by reporters while walking in Lower Manhattan. The president has previously made variations of the same claim—that he and the employees he sent downtown helped clear rubble in the days following the attack. “Everyone who helped clear the rubble—and I was there, and I watched, and I helped a little bit” was his version of it on the 2016 campaign trail. But supporting evidence is scant, and the whole account is likely an exaggeration. Richard Alles, a retired New York City Fire Department deputy chief who had a supervisory role in the efforts, said in 2019 not only that he never witnessed Trump at the site, but that the account doesn’t add up: “He was a private citizen at the time. I don’t know what kind of role he could have possibly played.” And, Alles added, if Trump had indeed sent workers, as he has claimed, there would be a record of it: “Is there a chance he was ever down there by himself and I didn’t know it? It’s possible, but I know of no one who ever witnessed him there.”

Nevertheless, Trump pressed on, veering into territory even further detached from verifiable fact. The U.S. Steel Building—a skyscraper in Lower Manhattan known today as One Liberty Plaza—was “really creaking,” Trump said at yesterday’s ceremony. “I’ll never forget: two firemen. We thought it was coming down on top of us and two firemen—big, strong guys. And I’m not the smallest guy in the world. They grabbed me under the arms, said, ‘Gotta get outta here.’ And they literally lifted me up—this is not easy to do; I’m big. They lifted me up, and they started running with me. I said, ‘Fellas, I can run myself.’ But they were amazing, but they thought it was coming down. It didn’t come down: U.S. Steel.”

There was, in fact, public concern in the days following the attack that the building at One Liberty Plaza could collapse. But the notion that Trump was inside the building and rushed out by firefighters has not been corroborated by any evidence. The president has offered versions of the same account before, though in less colorful form. In 2016, he told a story about being evacuated by firefighters—but in that rendition, it took place “a couple of days” after the collapse, and the U.S. Steel building was “not so far from us” when the two “massive guys” grabbed him. “I never knew I could run that fast,” he said then. In 2021, Trump likewise recalled two firemen grabbing him after hearing “creaks.”

Trump’s whereabouts on September 11 are no mystery. They are fairly well documented by his own prior statements about watching the attacks unfold from his home in Trump Tower, about four miles north of the Twin Towers. For those who have reason to doubt Trump’s statements, his whereabouts are also documented on video. “I have a window that looks directly at the World Trade Center, and I saw this huge explosion,” he said in a phone interview with a New Jersey news channel on 9/11. “For years, I’ve looked right directly at the building—I’d see the Empire State Building in the foreground and the World Trade Center in the background—and now I’m looking at absolutely nothing. It’s just gone, and it’s just hard to believe.”

Trump’s view may have been clear, but his recollection of September 11 has been murky, as reporters have tracked for more than a decade.

Sometimes, in his accounts, he watched the second plane hit the World Trade Center from his apartment in Trump Tower; when he called into the live broadcast that day, he did not mention having witnessed the second jet. Sometimes, he claims to have personally seen people jumping from the Twin Towers. “I have a window in my apartment that specifically was aimed at the World Trade Center,” he said in 2015 on the campaign trail, “and I watched as people jumped.” In his TV interview the day of the attack, he didn’t see those scenes himself but talked to someone who had. “I have somebody that was down there who witnessed at least 10 people jumping out of the building from 70 and 80 stories up in the air,” Trump said.

The president has also embellished what he saw in news coverage on that day, most notoriously claiming to have seen widespread celebrations by Muslim residents in New Jersey. “I watched when the World Trade Center came tumbling down,” he said in a 2015 stump speech. “And I watched in Jersey City, New Jersey, where thousands and thousands of people were cheering as that building was coming down.” That claim has been thoroughly disproved. A professor who spent months combing through news reels and video archives from that day found one possible instance that could slightly resemble those events—a few Arab American teenagers who briefly gathered in front of a library in South Paterson in a way that could be seen as celebratory.

Even the live phone interview Trump participated in on the day of the attack contains inaccuracies. On the call, Trump said that one of his buildings was now the tallest in downtown Manhattan: “Forty Wall Street actually was the second-tallest building in downtown Manhattan,” he said when asked whether his property had been damaged. “When they built the World Trade Center, it became known as the second tallest. And now it’s the tallest.” But as The Washington Post documented, using data from the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat, neither claim was true. Forty Wall Street was the tallest building for only about two years and that was in the 1930s, before Trump was born.

At the center of yesterday’s ceremony was an important honor—one that Trump had promised to bestow months prior. Welles Crowther, who was first identified as “the man in the red bandana” in a 2002 New York Times story, rescued more than a dozen people from the South Tower before being killed when the building collapsed. His parents, who had given him a red bandana as a boy and knew that he always carried it with him, were able to recognize him by that detail and confirmed through the survivors that their son had helped them. Crowther was an equities trader and a volunteer firefighter, and he was 24 years old when he died. While awarding a posthumous Presidential Medal of Freedom to Crowther, Trump drew the focus to himself, comparing his own fame to that of the fallen hero: “The Man in the Red Bandana, he’s become more famous than me—I don’t like it. Very unhappy. But he’s become much more famous than me.”

[Read: Trump is putting his face on everything]

When the president has brought up 9/11 lately, the remark has usually been severed from the attack, its victims, or its perpetrators. This year, Trump has tended to mention the day in the context of lambasting political opponents. He’s invoked it on several recent occasions—alongside World War I, World War II, and Pearl Harbor—when arguing that the attack and all three of those historical calamities are dwarfed by the danger communism poses to America today.

Critics have noted that other presidents, both Republican and Democrat, might have faced months of blowback—if not the end of their political careers—were they caught exaggerating or outright inventing aspects of their experience on one of the most catastrophic days in American history. But Trump has rarely if ever paid a real political price for misleading statements. In a second term marked by his fixation on his legacy, Trump’s relationship with history has often involved “taking bits and pieces from a grab bag and turning it into a story that’s useful to him—either politically useful, as in the case of his fabrication about Muslims celebrating 9/11, or making himself the center of a story that obviously he was not the center of 25 years ago,” Tim Naftali, a presidential historian at Columbia’s School of International and Public Affairs, told me. The fabrications are unlikely to stop, he said, especially as the president ages. “It doesn’t happen the same to all men in their 80s, but we may see more of this: new recollections about the past that we’ve never heard before, because they never happened,” Naftali said. The White House did not address my specific questions about the president’s most recent claims, but Davis Ingle, a White House spokesperson, said in a statement that the president “was relaying his experience of a city reeling” from the attacks.

September 11 continues to be a point of tension about what the government owed, and continues to owe, those who were most affected. The mayor’s office released more than 170,000 pages of city records this week showing that city officials misled New Yorkers about air quality following the attacks. And the health toll continues to grow; the number of 9/11 survivors receiving assistance for cancer treatment roughly tripled over the past five years, according to a Washington Post analysis of reports by the World Trade Center Health Program, which funds health care for responders and survivors. Twenty-five years later, Americans everywhere will pause to remember what the nation lost that day—both those who suffered directly and those who lived through it by watching the tragedy unfold from the safety of their homes, much as Trump did.

Is Donald Trump trying to help Republicans win the midterm elections? The obvious answer to this question is yes, of course, simply because all political leaders benefit from their party winning elections. Yet several of Trump’s actions throw this normally perfunctory question into at least some doubt.

Traditionally, candidates laboring under the burden of an unpopular president seek to detach themselves from the national brand. Trump is making this impossible. He has launched a national ad campaign focused on himself, and thrown a high-profile “midterm convention” designed to further reinforce the synonymity of the party and its leader. Trump has also made several remarks that convey an emotional detachment from the election’s outcome—comments such as “I’m not affected by the election” or “I don’t care about the midterms.”

Perhaps he is merely feigning indifference. Maybe he truly does not care. More likely, Trump cares about the outcome, but not enough to permit Republicans to acknowledge his lack of popularity. His approach, at any rate, nicely illustrates the drawbacks of handing total control of a political party to an egomaniac.

Midterm elections have historically disfavored the president’s party. Candidates for office who are running in neutral or hostile territory accordingly tend to emphasize their differences with the president—think of Joe Manchin running an ad in West Virginia in 2010 showing him firing an actual bullet at Barack Obama’s cap-and-trade bill, or the Republican Will Hurd in 2018 criticizing Trump’s racism and affinity for Russia.

[Listen: Trump’s economy goes to the polls]

Trump’s public standing is unusually dire. He is currently polling far worse than either he did at this point in 2018 or Joe Biden did four years ago. In theory, this ought to give Republicans an especially strong incentive to edge away from Trump and emphasize local themes in their elections.

Trump has decided to block this escape route. He is commandeering earned media with his “Trumpapalooza” show, drawing attention to the person endangered Republicans desperately need their constituents to forget. He has hoarded the party’s ad budget, warning Republicans, “This is my money that I control,” and that he would spend it on “Republicans that I think are good,” which is to say, slavishly loyal. He has promised to barnstorm the country, telling reporters, “I’ll be making a lot of stops,” including in Alaska, where the Republican Dan Sullivan is trailing.

There is an old Simpsons episode in which the elderly oligarch Montgomery Burns informs the obsequious Smithers that, upon his own death, his loyal aide will be buried alive at his boss’s feet. Trump appears to be thinking along similar lines.

This self-defeating behavior makes a degree of sense if you accept that Trump’s interest in politics is purely egocentric. He has difficulty caring about anything that does not directly involve himself, and the prospect of Republicans holding seats by repudiating his record is likely unpalatable.

The benefits of same-party control of Congress are also smaller for Trump than other presidents, because he governs mainly through executive action. To the extent that a Democratic majority in either chamber might threaten him with oversight, he will surely disregard it, as he did during his first term. Although he finds Republican majorities preferable, the size of those majorities matters to him far less than their composition—a fully loyal minority may seem preferable to a fractious majority.

What makes less sense is the willingness of many Republicans to cooperate with a strategy that consigns many of them to defeat.

Consider, for instance, Mike Rogers, the Republican Senate candidate in Michigan. Rogers came up through politics long before Trump entered it, and is trying to win office in a state where the president’s approval rating is 25 points underwater. A recent poll of the state found Trump’s endorsement made voters less likely, by a 55 percent to 27 percent margin, to support a candidate. Yet Rogers has opened no daylight whatsoever with the unpopular president, even echoing his false claims of having the 2020 election stolen from him. When Trump launched a trade war with Canada, a move that poses an especially dire threat to Rogers’s own state, he declined to take the opportunity to display independence. Instead, he issued a statement mumbling his approval. (“President Trump is right to put America First—and tariffs are necessary, but are not a one-size-fits-all solution.”) The same dynamic is occurring in other races, where Trump is throwing himself behind incumbents who are in danger mainly because voters don’t like him.

[Read: Trump plans to buy midterm ads about his own record]

To be sure, some Republicans subscribe to the theory that the Trump era has suspended normal midterm election dynamics. According to this account, a decisive sliver of voters are loyal to Trump, and not his party. Republicans can win only by enticing them to vote, which means making the election about their hero. “We’ve seen that a lot of Trump voters—they will crawl over glass to vote for the president,” one Republican strategist explained to Politico.

Yet Trump has tried before to insert himself into non-presidential elections. And his efforts have mostly failed—not only in the 2018 midterms, when he refused to lie low, but also in multiple off-schedule elections (the most famous case being Georgia’s 2021 Senate runoff, which he made into a referendum on his effort to overturn the 2020 election). In the Trump era, his party has fared well only when it has been able to focus on the shortcomings of unpopular Democratic opponents, such as Hillary Clinton in 2016 and Biden and Kamala Harris in 2024. Making elections about Trump—which also occurred when he ran for reelection in 2020—has been a losing gambit.

The political tip sheets in recent days have been filled with the lamentations of fatalistic Republican operatives. “We have no choice. We’re stuck on the boat with him whether we like it or not,” a top Republican campaign official confessed to Axios. They cannot stop Trump from staging Trumpapalooza, so they are reduced to praying for low ratings. “My only hope is that no one watches it since it’s competing versus NFL opening games,” a person close to the White House told Politico. “Hopefully nobody notices the embarrassing show.” Republicans are cooperating in a strategy that sacrifices their best chance of political survival, because the past decade has trained them to believe that resistance to Trump is futile.

Trump did far less damage to the country during his first term, both because institutions and norm-following officials restrained him, and because his pathological instincts frequently undermined his support. The former limitation has broken down in the second term, but the latter one remains. Trump’s support has collapsed in large part because of his own poor choices.
