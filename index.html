This is an edition of The Atlantic Daily, a newsletter that guides you through the biggest stories of the day, helps you discover new ideas, and recommends the best in culture. Sign up for it here.

One of the few issues that unites voters and politicians of both parties right now is data centers. Three-quarters of Americans would oppose a data center being built near where they live, according to a new poll released last week by Heatmap Pro and Embold Research. The two leading lights of the left, Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Senator Bernie Sanders, have sponsored a bill in Congress to create a temporary moratorium on new centers. They have an unlikely ally in archconservative Governor Greg Abbott of Texas, who said yesterday on ABC’s This Week that data-center companies “basically dug their own grave” and “got the backlash they deserve.”

Standing against this consensus is Donald Trump. The president remains a big booster of data centers. “Communities that don’t take a data center—they’re making a mistake,” he said Thursday during an interview with his on-again, off-again lackey Michael Cohen. “Data centers throw off tremendous amounts of jobs and money.”

This was no one-off. Trump had made several similar comments recently. “If I were the mayor of a town or the governor of a state and I had a chance to get a big plant in, an AI plant or a data center, I would absolutely want it, because the jobs are enormous and the money paid, the taxes paid, are just enormous,” Trump said last week—although he allowed that the sector could use “a little public-relations help.”

This puts Trump, the most successful demagogue in American political history, in the unusual position of misreading, and opposing, a populist groundswell. During his 2016 presidential campaign, Trump took many stands that were unpopular among establishment politicians. Some of these positions put him at odds not only with Democrats but also with most Republican elected officials. Trump broke with Republicans in claiming that he would protect social-welfare programs such as Medicare and Social Security, and he broke a bipartisan consensus by attacking free-trade agreements and promising protectionism. On these matters (as well as on his willingness to encourage and participate in gutter racism), Trump demonstrated that a big constituency existed for views that other politicians had deemed electoral liabilities.

Data centers’ political arc has been different. Initially, many officials seemed to back the centers, following exactly Trump’s logic: They offered jobs and tax benefits. But as public opinion turned—driven by a mix of backlash to AI in general, environmental concerns, worries about energy prices, and old-fashioned NIMBYism—politicians changed their view as well.

Why has Trump missed the shift? Presumably one reason is that he is surrounded by advisers and tech executives such as David Sacks and Larry Ellison, who are major boosters of and players in the industry. Tech companies invested heavily in Trump at the start of his second administration, lavishing him with gifts, including a gold-plated bauble and donations to projects such as his inauguration and his ballroom, and Trump has reciprocated by taking policy stances that benefit them. Trump may also be personally profiting from the AI boom; his financial disclosures show he’s become more of a tech investor.

Trump has always tended to adopt the latest thing he’s heard, no matter what it is. He has been willing to alienate corporate interests in the past—when he saw some political upside. But he is not hearing from voters who might alert him to how unpopular data centers are. Trump’s rallies and speeches once created a feedback loop in which he conveyed his message to voters and—crucially—they conveyed their thoughts to him. Now, as his energy sinks, his travel schedule gets lighter, and security makes events more challenging, Trump is getting out less.

Though his stump speeches dragged on for hours, Trump was carefully attuned to audience attention and what did and didn’t work. “You know, if it gets a little boring, if I see people starting to sort of, maybe thinking about leaving, I can sort of tell the audience, I just say, ‘We will build the wall!’ and they go nuts,” he told The New York Times in 2016. I think often about a speech Trump delivered in July 2022. It was billed as the launch of a new policy agenda, but as he read it, both Trump and his audience seemed bored. Then he ad-libbed a riff about trans athletes and the crowd came alive. Trump picked up on the shift and emphasized it; trans issues would become a central talking point for his 2024 campaign.

The president has always been susceptible to fake news and bad at filtering information, but rallies provided a gut check about his base, if not about the broader national reality. Now Trump isn’t even receiving that kind of information. One of his major conduits for information is Natalie Harp, a sycophantic aide who isn’t interested in exposing Trump to criticism. He could read public-opinion data, but as his own approval sinks, he has taken to saying (and may well believe) that polls are fake.

As his world shrinks, Trump’s populist touch has faded. Despite being elected twice on an antiwar platform, he’s launched attacks on Venezuela and Iran. As the Iran war has gotten bogged down, he’s been incapable of reading the public mood. He has dismissed voter frustration over rising fuel costs, saying “I don’t even think about” gas; he previously called affordability a “hoax.” In response to reports that American sailors aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln were struggling with a long deployment, Trump said they hadn’t been deployed “nearly long enough.” And the collapse of trade talks with Canada on Friday threaten Republican prospects in border states including Michigan and Ohio.

Each of these cases represents a growing gap between Trump and his base, but none is as ironic as Trump’s stance on data centers. The AI facilities store terabytes of data, but the president can’t get access to the information about them that he needs.

The Trump Comedians Are Tired

By Will Gottsegen

In 2017, as Donald Trump was getting used to the White House furniture, Alec Baldwin was feeling exultant. His impersonation of the new president on Saturday Night Live had made him momentarily inescapable and given the comedy show its highest ratings in years. To capitalize on the momentum, Baldwin co-authored a book-length satirical memoir written from Trump’s perspective. Baldwin’s autobiography—which also came out that year and was excerpted as a cover story for Vanity Fair—noted that his take on the president “seemed to delight nearly everyone in the People’s Republic of Manhattan.” When he accepted an Emmy for his performance in the fall, he addressed Trump directly: “At long last, Mr. President, here is your Emmy.”

It was disorienting, then, to hear Baldwin declare, when I spoke with him earlier this summer, that this sort of comedy has become stale, boring, and useless. “None of it’s funny. I don’t find it funny anymore,” he told me. “There’s nothing left to say about Trump.”

One afternoon earlier this month, I pulled up to the rec center and realized that I had a problem. It was not the three boisterous tweens in the back seat who were clamoring to be set loose in the basketball gym. It was my parking app. I had somehow gotten logged out of Passport Parking, which has become the only way to pay for a spot in much of my town, and it was demanding a four-digit PIN to let me back in. My first guess drew a warning message in red letters: “Invalid PIN. PIN cannot contain 4 sequential numbers, 2 repeated digits, or the last 4 digits of your phone number.” I searched my trusty password manager, which was supposed to spare me these predicaments, but it came up empty. By that point, the kids had run ahead and piled themselves against the gym door, waiting for me to let them in.

Lately, the process of accessing apps and websites has grown dizzying. “I’m in password hell,” a colleague confided to me recently. “Every login attempt is playing the lottery.” The problem is not just the sheer number of digital accounts, all of which require their own password, ideally unique and unguessable (which usually also means un-memorizable). It’s the pileup of solutions to that problem: all of the different layers of software offering to remember your passwords, the six-digit codes sent to your phone, the authenticator apps, the passkeys.

Browsers and operating systems compete with third-party password managers to generate, store, and autofill your credentials. Most of my logins live in 1Password, but others are stored in Apple’s Passwords app (usually by accident), and still others were created via “single sign-on” features such as those offered by Google and Facebook. I vaguely recall certain apps—Facebook is one—prompting me many years ago to copy down a set of “login recovery codes” that would become extremely important should I ever get locked out of my account. Whatever device I stored them on was probably wiped and sold long ago.

Logins have always been a hassle. In the 1990s, forgetting your password sometimes meant mailing a postcard to your service provider and waiting a week or two for it to mail you a new password. In the early 2010s, hackers learned to steal massive troves of logins and then reuse them on other services to steal people’s identities. Things spiraled from there; services started requiring unique, complex passwords with arbitrary requirements. These days, the user experience of logging in is “probably worse than ever,” Troy Hunt, a cybersecurity expert who runs the website Have I Been Pwned, told me. “There’s just no consistency.”

Password managers promised a way out, but they often don’t work as seamlessly as you might hope. I click to autofill my password somewhere, and a 1Password authorization appears, only for an iCloud Keychain authorization to pop up directly over it, preventing me from clicking on the 1Password one. When I manually enter a login I thought I remembered, my browser prompts me to remember it; only after I hit “Save” (pro tip: Do not do this) does the website inform me that the password was not recognized. Even when you log in successfully, there’s the tedious dance of waiting for a six-digit authorization code to appear on your phone or in your email, which defeats the concept of a single password for everything. And if you’re somewhere without cell service or you’re sharing a login with someone who isn’t around, you might just be out of luck.

All of these steps serve a purpose. At least in theory, each added layer helps prevent hacks and breaches. Erring on the side of safety is worth an amount of inconvenience. At the same time, extra steps can introduce security problems of their own. Last year, Hunt fell for a clever phishing scheme when he clicked a link to log in to his newsletter service. His password manager failed to autofill his information, which should have alerted him that something was wrong, he said. But like the rest of us, he was so used to things not working that he went ahead and manually entered his credentials on a site that turned out to be an impostor, designed to steal and exploit them.

None of these obstacles is insurmountable on its own. My problem with Apple’s native password manager fighting with 1Password, for instance, was eventually solved with a 10-minute foray into the bowels of my device settings. But together, they amount to a near-daily irritant. For the less tech-savvy, the situation can be overwhelming. My recent attempts to help my mother fix her password-storage system—which involved loose-leaf notepad sheets full of hand-scrawled credentials scattered around her house like Easter eggs—consumed the better part of a day. By the end, she had all of her credentials updated, validated, and stored in a password manager, just as the experts have been advising for years. But even then, her preferred browser, Google Chrome, showed no interest in surfacing the credentials we had stashed in Apple’s Keychain. And some websites short-circuited her attempts to log in by prompting her to set up a “passkey,” which she interpreted as meaning that her password had failed and she needed to scrap it and set up a new one.

Cybersecurity experts will tell you that passkeys—which typically entail logging in to apps or websites with a face or fingerprint scan instead of a password—are the future. When they work properly, they’re both painless and secure. “It may get to a point where we’re all using passkeys and we don’t even know we’re using passkeys,” Lorrie Cranor, a computer-science professor at Carnegie Mellon University who researches privacy and cybersecurity, told me. “I just say ‘Log in’ and I smile at it, and it gets my passkey, and I’m logged in.”

But that future isn’t here yet, and in the present, passkeys have added a new layer of frustration. Part of the problem is that virtually no one seems to know what they are. “The fact that even I have trouble explaining them should be a clue,” Cranor said. Her past research on the rollout of two-factor authentication suggests that most people tend to resist new security features for as long as they can. Companies can nudge them, but for websites and apps trying to attract and retain users, pushing people to adopt an unfamiliar authentication technology risks scaring them away.

To further complicate matters, passkeys generally aren’t yet replacing passwords altogether, even on sites that use them. Because many passkeys live on your device, one that works on your phone might not work on your laptop, and one that works on your personal laptop might not work on your office device. So you still need a password that works across devices as a backup. It’s a lot, Dave Lewis, 1Password’s global-security adviser, acknowledged to me in an email. “The problem is cognitive overload,” he said. “All these tools are trying to help, but they don’t always work together intuitively.” Still, he disagreed that the situation has never been worse: “When the alternative was using the same password across dozens of accounts or putting it on a sticky note, the fact that people now have password managers, passkeys, biometrics, and multifactor authentication is a good thing.”

The mess might compound before it improves. Hacking attempts have been multiplying as crooks harness AI tools. Companies are adding even more security layers that make logging in harder, and they are pleading for people to make the switch to passkeys. On the bright side, maybe the seamless biometric future is actually within sight, and all of our login woes will soon be over. Then again, that’s what we were promised with password managers too.

On August 3, Education Secretary Linda McMahon sent a letter to states and higher-education leaders with instructions to, as McMahon put it, “describe to the American public your commitments to rigorous teaching, pathbreaking research, and national service—commitments that make our universities a bedrock institution of the American republic.”

A request like that would be simple enough if not for the history between the Trump administration and higher education—a relationship that can fairly be described as antagonistic. Case in point: last year’s “Compact for Academic Excellence in Higher Education,” which critics from across the political spectrum argued effectively asked colleges to compromise academic freedom in exchange for privileged, if vague, access to federal funds. There’s also the suite of federal investigations targeting diversity programs on college campuses.

Questioning colleges is normal. There are outstanding concerns about the experience, the education, and the enterprise of higher education: How much does it cost, and is that more than families can pay? Who gets in, and why? Are faculty members treated with dignity? What is college even for? So, although college leaders argue that a few of the concerns voiced in the document are legitimate, they also see within it the bane of Ilium.

Stripling: But in any case, you’ll remember it was the same story that we were hearing then, which was that a lot of the drivers of cost have to do with personnel; they have to do with structural issues within higher education; they have to do with students wanting more amenities—that was kind of an old sall. And I think you’re hearing a lot of the same things, but the fact is they can’t get away from the reality that people are losing confidence in higher education as an institution.

If you look at the latest polling on this, I think it’s, like, four in 10 Americans that say they have high confidence or a lot of confidence in higher education now. And even Democrats who have historically supported higher education more than Republicans are starting to lose their affection for the institution.

So I think there’s a recognition that it’s a real problem, and I really do think that the money is the big factor. We keep talking about the politics, but when people are saying I don’t know if college is worth it, they’re usually saying I don’t know if it’s worth the money, not I don’t know if it’s worth risking meeting a liberal person.

Harris: I do want to talk more about the purpose of higher education, but I wanted to ask first about the pressures for ideological change from the administration and state lawmakers. You’re in Florida, and that’s a good example of the changes in terms of curriculum, institutional culture. So are you noticing any effects?

Stripling: I think it kind of remains to be seen, Adam. I still—and again, this is really more gut than data—but I still do think cost is the major thing families are worried about.

I will say that, to your point, I do live in Florida and, you know, this is famously the place where woke goes to die, as Ron DeSantis said. And a lot of action has been taken on the ideological front that is also connected to this question of What is college for?

So if you think about the core curriculum, the courses that colleges are saying everybody should take, there’s usually some menu of sorts. A lot of institutions over time—there’s been a lot of bloat there. There’s been a lot of things that we might not consider essential that have come into the core curriculum. So some of these ostensibly sort of ideological attacks resonate to some degree with faculty who say, Actually we have gotten a little out of control in what’s in this core.

But what you’re pointing to, Adam, is the key distinction, which is, is higher education about vocation or does it have some sort of more aspirational mission? And this is an old problem. Are you there to learn about how to prepare for your first job, or is it about preparing you for a life of greater intellectual enrichment? And I think that that’s at the heart of a lot of the conversations we’re having, even if they are politically coded. What is college for? continues to be something that, you know, sort of divides people.

What’s interesting is that parents and even students will tell you, I really want to go to college and be able to get a good job. And I want to be able to leave here with a credential that matters and that is seen as authentic, even though I use ChatGPT for half my classes.

But students also say they want more. If you look at the studies around mental health that places like the Harvard Grad School of Education have done, they are desperate for something real. And so when they come to an institution that seems like it’s all about pleasing the next corporate overlord, they chafe. That’s why they’re booing at commencement ceremonies when people praise AI, right? So students actually do want something more than something to punch up their résumé.

Harris: So, Jack, we’re in August, whole school year ahead of us. What will you be keeping your eye on the most as this year develops?

Stripling: I go back to what I said before, which is this collective sense of Is the Trump administration done with us?

I think that that is something that I’m starting to hear people in higher education ask. Like, Was this Act 1, or is this letter in and of itself a form of surrender, a sort of concession? They’re looking for a symbolic win. Using the DOJ and these other arms of the government to try to exact concessions or revenge is very expensive. It’s very time consuming, and they’ve lost in court in some big ways. Now these cases are still unfolding, but that’s the reality.

What I will be paying attention to is this question of purpose, though. And I know this is going to sound weird, Adam, but have you read Stoner?

Harris: Yes, I have.

Stripling: Okay. So for folks who don’t know, this is a 1965 novel by John Williams. We just talked about it on my podcast, College Matters. We had sort of a reading club.

And it’s the story about this professor at Mizzou, who’s this farm boy who goes to the University of Missouri thinking that he’s going to major in some kind of agricultural sciences. He’s going to come back and work on the family farm, and life’s gonna be happy for him and his parents. What ends up happening instead is that he goes into this class and he’s exposed to his first Shakespearean sonnet, and it immediately changes his world. He can’t explain why, but the way he sees people, the way he sees worlds, the way he interacts with the universe changes in some profound way. And the frustration of the novel is that he can’t really explain it to anyone. It’s just something he feels.

And that’s the problem with this product that we call “higher education.” When it works as it should, it’s transformational. It changes you in profound ways. But it’s nearly impossible to explain how.

And that makes it really hard to charge $100,000 for. And so what higher education is going to have to do this year, like it has every other previous year I’ve been covering it, is explain that transformational experience in a way that resonates with every constituency, whether it’s the Trump administration, the student who doesn’t have enough money to go to college, the parents who are skeptical about the the job return, the people who care about AI and how that’s changing credentials. Somehow they’re going to have to make that case, and maybe reading Stoner is a good start. But that’s what I’m always thinking about.

Harris: Well, Jack, as always, it is fantastic to see you, to talk to you, and you’ve given us a lot to think about as we head into this new school year. It’s great to have you on.

Stripling: Thank you, Adam. Let’s do it again. I really appreciate it.

Harris: We’re back in the archives this week with a look at a question that has troubled some of the greatest minds throughout history. It’s a question that Booker T. Washington and W. E. B. Du Bois tried to answer in The Atlantic between 1896 and 1903: Are colleges designed only to train people to work for their own improvement, or are they designed to train people to create a better society?

Booker T. Washington was the most powerful Black man of the late 19th and early 20th century in America. He was born on a tobacco plantation in 1856, and he was emancipated from slavery at the end of the Civil War. He was 9 years old then. Education changed his life.

Writing about his upbringing in the September 1896 issue of The Atlantic, Washington said: “After the close of the war, while working in the coal-mines of West Virginia for the support of my mother, I heard in some accidental way of the Hampton Institute.”

Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute, near the Chesapeake Bay in Virginia, had opened its doors in 1868. It had a simple mission: “to train selected Negro youth who should go out and teach and lead their people first by example.” The students from Hampton were expected to get land and homes, to work for their money with knowledge of why they were doing the work—or, as the institution’s founder put it, “to replace stupid drudgery with skilled hands.”

Washington would later write in the pages of The Atlantic in 1896 the education he received at Hampton changed him, it “caused me for the first time to realize what it meant to be a man instead of a piece of property.”

He was ready to proselytize, to spread the word on this industrial, practical education that wasn’t just a mirror of what was popular. It wasn’t just teaching French for the sake of saying that it did or the piano while expecting nothing but a future of poverty in return for its graduates. It was a functional education, which would help Black people less than a generation removed from slavery learn the skills they needed to advance.

In 1881, Washington went to the Deep South, to the Black Belt in Alabama, to establish a school in Tuskegee. The school opened with one teacher, 30 students, and not a lick of property to speak of save a shanty and a church. But Washington was a savvy dealer; he coaxed donations from wealthy people in both the North and the South by preaching a philosophy of self-reliance. The students worked to build buildings on campus while they received an industrial education. And by the time he was writing in The Atlantic in 1896, that little college in Tuskegee had grown to 800 students, taught by 79 faculty members, spread across 1,400 acres of land which housed 30 buildings.

Washington’s reputation grew alongside the institution, not least because of what he was willing to set aside to achieve his goal.

His philosophy would have allowed—at least in the short term—the continuation of Black Americans’ second-class citizenship.

Black people, Washington argued in his Atlantic essay, were to find their dignity through their industrial labors and the financial gains it brought them. He wrote that “it is through the dairy farm, the truck garden, the trades, and commercial life, largely, that the negro is to find his way to the enjoyment of all his rights.”

Now, despite the critique that Washington’s school only taught Black people to do the same jobs that they did during slavery, even his critics were forced to admire his brilliance and thrift and ability to make a tangible difference in the lives of Black people in the Black Belt.

Perhaps most prominent among those critics was W. E. B. Du Bois, who argued that the training students received at Tuskegee was necessary but insufficient on its own. Sure, it might lead to the economic advancement for some of its students; it might make them self-sufficient, able to eat and clothe themselves. But Du Bois argued that there was more to life than food and clothing.

Du Bois thought a true college education—a liberal education of the kind taught at Fisk, Atlanta University, Wilberforce, Howard, and Lincoln, among others—was the foundation of advancement. In the September 1902 issue of The Atlantic, Du Bois argued that there needed to be teachers for would-be students. Those colleges like Fisk that were training Black people to go back and teach their kin were of the highest importance. Du Bois wrote: “In a single generation they put thirty thousand black teachers in the South; they wiped out the illiteracy of the majority of the black people of the land, and they made Tuskegee possible.”

The schools Du Bois praised developed not only workers but full men. They produced people capable of critical thought. The Bible says that man shall not live by bread alone; Du Bois had his own version. “We shall hardly induce black men to believe that if their bellies be full it matters little about their brains.”

The reality that both men acknowledged is that both forms of teaching—the one that begins with the theoretical and the other with the practical—are necessary to improve the situation for Black people in America, to unwind the damage done by generations of laws which banned Black education.

Washington thought enough of his critics to respond, which he did in the October 1903 issue of The Atlantic, where he wrote: “I cannot understand how any reasonable person can object to such education for a large part of a people who are in the poverty-stricken condition that is true of a large element of my race, especially when such hand training is combined, as it should be, with the best education of the head and heart.”

But the issue was that Washington’s idea of a practical education which would disregard social equality was weaponized: It was treated as the only way rather than a way.

As Du Bois noted in his 1902 essay, “I sit with Shakespeare and he winces not. Across the color line I move arm in arm with Balzac and Dumas, where smiling men and welcoming women glide in gilded halls.” Reading the classics gave him a sense of the ties that bind humanity; they welcomed him to the great brotherhood of mankind.

The question animating the debate between Washington and Du Bois is as relevant as ever: Is college meant to train people for the workforce of today or to produce graduates capable of building a better tomorrow? It’s a question that we should all be considering.

The Atlantic has hired Jessica Goldstein as senior vice president of events, who will work in partnership with managing director for events Evan Smith to lead and produce the magazine’s robust live programs nationwide. She begins with The Atlantic in early September.

Jessica has spent her career telling stories through reporting and creating live experiences that resonate far beyond the stage. She spent eight years creating and leading NPR’s live cultural programming, including creating the Tiny Desk Contest, NPR’s annual search for new musical talent, and the How I Built This Summit. She most recently led the Skoll Foundation’s global convenings strategy and directed the Skoll World Forum.

At The Atlantic, she will work with Evan to lead one of the most influential and long-running events practices in media. The Atlantic convenes dozens of annual in-person and virtual events that reach thousands of people every year, anchored by its flagship event, The Atlantic Festival, which returns to New York City September 17–19, after being held for nearly two decades in Washington, D.C. The Atlantic is also under way with a new 50-state tour, The Atlantic Across America, that has already held events in 10 states, addressing some of the most complex issues of our time.

Below is Evan Smith’s announcement to staff:

I’m excited to let you know that we’ve hired the amazing Jessica Goldstein as our new senior vice president of events. As the second-in-command on our growing team, she’ll oversee the operations, planning, and execution of our robust public programs, working closely with our sales, marketing, design, and CSG colleagues to grow The Atlantic’s audience and revenue; shine a great, big light on our brand; and—most crucially—bring our journalists and our journalism to stages from coast to coast through The Atlantic Festival, The Atlantic Across America, The Atlantic Summits, and so much more.

Jessica comes to us most recently from the Skoll Foundation, where she was managing director of convenings, leading the organization’s global events strategy and directing the Skoll World Forum, a weeklong gathering of heads of state, social entrepreneurs, and civil-society partners. But it’s her reputation at NPR that really precedes her—and makes her the ideal hire in this moment. As senior director and executive producer of Events & Experiential, she spent eight years creating and leading NPR’s live programming, including the Tiny Desk Contest and the How I Built This Summit. Previously she was a senior supervising producer for NPR Music, a senior producer on the science desk, and a producer of audio documentaries known as NPR Radio Expeditions. A native of New York, Jessica is a graduate of the University of Wisconsin at Madison.

Jessica believes in the power of storytelling, of sharing ideas and sparking inspiration, which is precisely how we think about our work in the events space. There is no loftier task before us than getting people from divergent backgrounds—great minds who don’t think alike—in a room to listen and talk and continue the conversation after we’re gone. She will bring to bear her decades of experience, her imagination, her creativity, her calm, and her charisma as we expand the ambitious work of the Live team.
