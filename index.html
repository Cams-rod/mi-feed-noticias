If for nothing else, the United Nations’ annual climate gathering can be useful for focusing world leaders’ attention on the fate of the planet for a week or two. During this year’s conference, though, Presidents Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin were instead meeting with one another in China, where they agreed to increase oil production; only one G7 leader, British Prime Minister Andy Burnham, showed up in Antalya, Turkey, where the UN conference, known as COP 31, was held.

When a world leader did appear at the conference, a media scrum would form, Marek Pruszewicz, a former BBC editor who now runs the climate publication Dialogue Earth, told me. But when the leaders left, reporters seemed to vanish just as quickly. He saw television journalists working amid empty seats and rows of vacant press tables. “It’s just not cutting through,” Pruszewicz said.

The same could be said of the year’s biggest climate fight in the United States, over a permitting-reform bill that the Senate passed on Thursday. Proponents argue that the bill is necessary for clean energy to thrive; opponents see it as a gift to gas companies. If you talk with people involved in this debate, or dip into the corners of social media where the issues are being litigated out loud, you’ll still find vehement arguments about the fate of the planet. But it’s hard to shake the feeling that the world seems to care less about solving this problem than it once did. On Sunday, the COP negotiations collapsed for only the third time in the conference’s 30-year history.

The world’s diplomats spent more than 20 years at these climate negotiations before they agreed to even mention fossil fuels, the dominant driver of global warming, as part of the problem. That happened five years ago, at the UN climate summit in Glasgow, when negotiators agreed not on a “phase out” but on just a “phase down” of the use of coal. The following year, in Sharm el-Sheikh, Egypt, dozens of countries pressed for an agreement to “phase down” all fossil fuels; only in 2023, in Dubai, did diplomats commit to the more effervescent idea of a “transitioning away.”

The negotiations over the past two weeks in Antalya made those past efforts sound ambitious, even idealistic. During the talks this week, negotiators stripped out all references to fossil fuels and to reducing the world’s dependence on them from a draft agreement. The negotiators also cut references to the historical responsibility of developed countries for causing climate change. Mohamed Adow, the director of Power Shift Africa, a climate-advocacy group, told reporters on Friday that this has been “the worst COP ever.” The negotiations ran into overtime, as negotiators tried to land on an agreement that could gain the necessary consensus to pass, but on Sunday, they admitted that they had failed.

The signature proposal, set by the host nation at the conference’s outset, was to set a goal of increasing the proportion of electrification of the world’s energy consumption from 23 percent, where it is now, to 35 percent by 2035. This shift is seen as important for eventually lowering carbon emissions, by switching out internal-combusion cars and oil heaters with electric alternatives, which can be more efficient even if their electricity comes from power plants burning fossil fuels. Reaching “35 by 35” would not be easy—it would require a more than fourfold speedup in the pace of electrification—but hitting that goal still wouldn’t keep the atmosphere from warming 2 or more degrees Celsius from the baseline before the Industrial Revolution. That amounts to “catastrophic global warming,” Johan Rockström, the director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, told me from Antalya last week. The electrification goal, he said, was, to begin with, a “minor and insufficient consolation.” Ultimately, the negotiations broke down in part over the question of whether the agreement would mention fossil fuels at all.

Some environmentalists worry that Congress, too, is too willing to ignore the problem of fossil fuels’ future. The bipartisan permitting bill, which will now head to the House, would rewrite bedrock environmental laws to speed up the build-out of transmission lines and new sources of clean energy. But these laws would also make it easier to build all sorts of infrastructure, including (opponents point out) gas pipelines, data centers, and ICE-detention centers, as well as (proponents note) ecological-restoration projects.

Like COP’s organizers, the architects of this bill see it as a practical measure that will continue advancing climate goals. One of its sponsors is Senator Sheldon Whitehouse, who has been one of the most consistent advocates for climate policy in Congress. The bill’s supporters argue that it clears the way for clean energy to continue its takeover, even if, in the short term, the bill also helps the fossil-fuel industry. (Opponents see fossil-fuel companies as better positioned to take advantage of any new build-out.) Some former Biden-administration staffers believe that the bill could fix a problem they ran into with their signature climate legislation: They made it easier to build solar and wind farms, but not easier to build the power lines to connect them to anything. No one thinks that the bill does enough, on its own, to dramatically change the trajectory of U.S. emissions; people on both sides of the argument fear a future in which the status quo sticks.

Climate scientists are taking encouragement where they can. If the biggest climate fights of 2027 will be focused on limited “practical” questions, then so will the clearest advances: The use of solar- and other renewable-energy sources is growing. So is the adoption of EVs. Both are happening far faster in many other countries than they are in the United States. Still, the reminders about the costs of continued warming were hard to miss this year: the glacier collapse in Nepal that swept away more than 1,500 people. The summer heat wave in Europe that killed some 50,000 more. The El Niño in an already overheated Pacific Ocean that’s spinning out droughts and floods around the world. Climate scientists say the goal of keeping warming below 1.5 degrees Celsius will inevitably be surpassed; the latest report by the World Meteorological Organization found that global mean temperatures from January to September were 1.47 degrees Celsius above preindustrial levels. Celeste Saulo, the WMO secretary general, told reporters last week that the past dozen years have been the warmest on record, and the past four, including 2026, were the warmest of those. “There is no end in sight,” she said.

The question has moved to how bad the “overshoot” period will be and what can be done to minimize the damage and avoid tipping points. “That includes your large ice sheets melting, glaciers melting,” Rachel Cleetus, the climate-program director at the Union of Concerned Scientists, told me. “Once that happens, you can’t bring them back.” The mood going into COP was “pretty somber,” she said, in a year when climate disasters have piled up and fossil-fuel companies reported record profits. She had left for Antalya from Boston amid fall temperatures in the 70s Fahrenheit. By the time the COP talks ended with little to show, most everyone had gone home, and the mood was grim.

Throughout history, a familiar pattern has shown itself: Political elites lose touch with the people over whom they rule, and those people rebel in one way or another. But eventually a new kind of elite asserts itself. The rise of Donald Trump as a political force more than a decade ago was part of one such global wave of anti-elite sentiment. If the midterm results signal the end of the Trump era, do they also signal the beginning of the end of our populist moment?

In this episode of The Permanent Questions, Fareed Zakaria joins David Brooks to discuss the history of liberal democracy and the inevitable reactions against it, why technology makes this reaction different from others, and the ways in which our liberal elite will have to change if they want to own the future.

The following is a transcript of the episode:

Here are some examples: The Roman empire in the fifth century, the French monarchy in the 18th century, the Russian aristocracy in the early 20th century, and the shah’s Iran in 1979. There is increasing economic inequality. There’s increasing social inequality.

The elites have grown insular and condescending toward the people. They become self-indulgent and corrupt. The elites break the social contract with the people, and the people withdraw their trust and any sense of legitimacy.

It seems to me that the United States and many other societies around the globe have been flirting with this kind of catastrophe for many decades. Millions of people have lost faith in the elites, in institutions, in the future, and in each other. These losses of faith produce populist backlashes, and that’s what we’ve been living through over the past 15 years or so.

High-tech gadgets proliferated almost as rapidly in Mark Twain’s time as they do in ours, and he prided himself on being an early adopter. So when Alexander Graham Bell’s new telephone company began to offer service in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1877, Twain signed up, boasting (incorrectly) that his was the first line “ever used in a private house in the world.” Shortly afterward, he did set a record, becoming “the first author to deploy the telephone in fiction, in a peculiar story for The Atlantic Monthly,” James Gleick writes in The Telephone: A New History.

The comedy of “The Loves of Alonzo Fitz Clarence and Rosannah Ethelton” comes mainly from its high-concept premise: The title characters fall in love, quarrel, reconcile, and get married entirely over the phone, without ever meeting in person. At the time the story appeared, in March 1878, very few Americans had actually seen a telephone, and Twain plays up the strangeness of the new technology. When we are first introduced to Alonzo, in his study in Eastport, Maine, he seems to be talking to himself, or to someone in the next room, before it’s revealed that he’s actually on the phone with his Aunt Susan in San Francisco.

But although Twain found technology fascinating, he also knew that things—even things that do amazing things—don’t make for good stories. The only subject that human beings find enduringly interesting is one another, and Twain was remarkably prescient about how the telephone would challenge traditional terms of interaction. The lovers in Twain’s story are separated by thousands of miles, but the conversation between them is not at either end, nor is it somewhere in the middle; it is a meeting of minds that have escaped the body’s requirement of place. This raises questions that we’re still asking today, in the age of dating apps and OnlyFans. Can people be real to one another without physical presence? Can there be love without touch? “There was a feeble but frantic sound for some little time, as if wool-lipped, disembodied spirits were exchanging kisses,” Twain writes about his telephone lovers. Is that funny or nightmarish?

Gleick’s great achievement as a science writer has been to show how complicated, abstract ideas end up changing the way we experience the world. In Chaos, he explored chaos theory, which used new mathematical tools to explain why weather and other complex systems are so unpredictable. In The Information, he showed how modern discoveries such as DNA and the computer involved new ways of thinking about data and communication. The Telephone, too, is the story of a scientific discovery. But it’s just as much the history of a business—the Bell System, a group of companies led by the American Telephone and Telegraph Company (AT&T) that became America’s most powerful and enduring monopoly. The book is also a wide-ranging cultural history, showing how the telephone changed people’s lives and how they tried to make sense of those changes. When Bell gave a public demonstration of his invention in Salem, Massachusetts, speaking with Thomas Watson 18 miles away, in Boston, “it seemed as if an electric thrill went through the audience,” he remembered. “They recognized for the first time—what was meant by the ‘Telephone.’ ”

But what did it mean, exactly? That is where novelists like Twain came in—along with playwrights, psychologists, social scientists, and legions of pundits in what Gleick calls the “Telephone Century.” Like artificial intelligence today, the telephone was an obviously momentous new technology that everyone had heard about and no one fully understood. If the parallel holds, we won’t know how AI is going to change the world until after it happens, because early predictions about how people would use the telephone turned out to be wildly off base.

A New York Times article imagined it as a way to listen to sermons without having to go to church, joking that soon houses for rent would offer Baptist preachers “laid on in every room,” like hot and cold water. In France, the Théâtrophone service allowed subscribers (including Marcel Proust) to listen to live opera performances over the phone. In both cases, the telephone was viewed as a one-way delivery system. But its power would lie in being a network—literally, a web of metal cables spanning continents and oceans, which allowed any two people, anywhere in the world, to have a back-and-forth exchange.

Not even Bell knew at first exactly what he was inventing. The initial goal of his research wasn’t to transmit the human voice, but to increase the carrying capacity of existing telegraph lines by sending multiple messages simultaneously at different pitches. In the course of his experiments with vibrating steel reeds, he discovered the principle that made the telephone possible: Sound and electricity speak the same language. Sound travels through the air in waves of pressure, and electromagnetic force also takes the form of a wave. The telephone was a tool for translating between the two kinds of waves. A small magnet, vibrating in response to a speaking voice, would generate an electrical current, which could be sent down a wire and reconverted into sound at the other end.

Bell wasn’t the only person to make this discovery. Philipp Reis had built a device he called das Telephon in Germany in 1861, and Elisha Gray, an American inventor, was so close on Bell’s heels that both men’s applications reached the U.S. Patent Office on the same day. Bell was awarded the patent, as well as the chance to go down in history as the inventor of the telephone, mainly because his well-connected Washington lawyer bribed the patent examiner with $100 and a gold watch.

Making the new invention useful would be a lot more expensive. As Gleick observes, other Industrial Age enterprises benefited from economies of scale. The more automobiles you make, the cheaper manufacturing each one becomes. With the telephone, the calculation was reversed: The bigger the network, the greater the cost per call, given how many lines and switchboards were required to put all the calls through. Raising capital from ultrarich investors became crucial. Originally Bell was one of four partners in the company that bore his name. Within three years, he had been reduced to one of 540 shareholders and ceased to play any real role.

At first, independent telephone companies tried to challenge the Bell System, in business and in court. Some enterprising farmers even turned their barbed-wire fences into telephone lines by running electric current through them. But Bell’s patents gave AT&T an unbeatable head start, and it ruthlessly maintained its advantage for more than a century. In the 1920s, for instance, a small company called Hush-a-Phone started to sell a cheap plastic device that attached to the mouthpiece of a telephone; talking into it would make your desk phone “private as a booth.” But AT&T prohibited customers from using any third-party equipment, and “when company repair personnel saw a Hush-a-Phone in a customer’s home, they threatened to disconnect service altogether,” Gleick writes. Not until the case reached a federal appeals court, in 1956, did the company finally win the right to sell its $10 product.

An antitrust suit led to the breakup of AT&T in 1984. By then, the telephone was so ubiquitous that its effect on modern life could hardly be grasped. Over a century, the meaning of distance had changed: The other side of the country, or the planet, didn’t seem so far away when you could communicate across it instantly, instead of waiting weeks to exchange letters. Privacy had become more accessible—teenagers could take the phone into their room, or a closet, and talk without a chaperone. But it was also more vulnerable. You couldn’t be sure that you weren’t being eavesdropped on—in the early days, by an operator or by a neighbor on a party line; later on, by someone picking up an extension or, if you were important enough, by a government wiretapper.

Operating switchboards came to be seen as women’s work—mainly because women were thought to be better than young men at sitting still and could be paid less.

The telephone helped bring women into the public sphere, too, by creating a new kind of job. “As if by heavenly decree,” Gleick writes, “a consensus formed that operating a switchboard was women’s work,” and that “only women were suited to it”—mainly because they were better than young men at sitting still and could be paid lower wages. Even so, the job offered many women a path to personal independence and professional respect. When the U.S. Army needed skilled telephone operators during World War I, it had no choice but to hire women, although by law they couldn’t enlist in the military. In 1918, the 33 women of Telephone Unit No. 1 shipped out for France, where they “ate army food and slept in army barracks,” and wore their own armbands, embroidered with “black telephone transmitters and yellow streaks of lightning.”

After that war, and even more so after World War II, women found new telephone-related jobs in offices as receptionists and secretaries. But switchboard operators were doomed by their own success. America’s telephone network was expanding so fast that there weren’t enough women to keep up. Around 1910, AT&T’s chief engineer estimated that by 1930, at least a quarter of New York City’s population would be needed just to run its switchboards. The company had to find a way to automate the process of making phone calls. The solution turned out to be the rotary dial, which allowed callers to enter the phone number they wanted to reach instead of telling it to an operator. Gleick shows that each major advance in telephone technology followed the same pattern: Science would rise to the demands of business. Technical challenges, such as signal amplification for long-distance calls, seemed insurmountable until they were solved. Then they were taken for granted.

Harold M. Lambert / Getty
How to dial a rotary phone.

This dynamic is familiar in our own time too. Yesterday’s miracle becomes today’s necessity and tomorrow’s antique. The rotary dial was so cutting-edge in the 1920s that AT&T worried that customers wouldn’t be able to use it. New phones came printed with instructions: “Remove receiver. Put finger in hole over first figure wanted. Turn to stop and let go.” Today, you’d need to give the same explanation to people under 30, who have grown up in a world of touch screens. Among other things, The Telephone is an almanac of this kind of extinct knowledge. Depending on how old you are, you’ll feel nostalgic about different phases of telephone history: Remember dial tones? The Yellow Pages? Pay phones? Calling for the time?

Now the telephone itself is history. By collective agreement, we refer to the devices in our hands at every waking moment as phones—maybe iPhones or cellphones, but never telephones. Phones can do a million things that telephones never dreamed of, while the one thing telephones did is starting to feel unnecessary, even impolite. When a phone rings—an anachronism; phones don’t ring, they vibrate or light up or play a tune—it’s more likely to be a scammer or a salesperson than a friend. If you really want to communicate with someone, you send a text or set up a Zoom call. Fittingly, Gleick ends his book with a visit to a disconnected telephone booth in Japan, which a local man has turned into a shrine of sorts—a place to think about the absent and the dead, who have no phone number.

Duncan Raban / Popperfoto / Getty
A Motorola DynaTAC 8000x, the first commercial mobile phone, circa 1985.

Even if we’ve moved beyond telephones, however, we still inhabit the strange new dimension they opened up. The telephone gave humanity its first experience of what we now call the virtual—a kind of reality that is divorced from presence. By translating the human voice into a signal transmitted through a wire, the telephone gave it the reach of electricity and the abstractness of data. At first, telephone users experienced this as a magic power—“this admirable magic that needs only a few seconds to bring before us, unseen but present, the person to whom we wish to speak,” as Proust described it.

What Bell did not foresee is that once human beings got a taste of this freedom, having a body and being in a place began to feel burdensome, a concession to the limitations of the physical world. Ever since, the information-technology revolution that began in the 1870s with the telephone and phonograph has been steadily lightening that burden. Radio, television, movies, and the internet have claimed an ever larger share of our attention for people and things not actually there.

AI is completing the emancipation from reality that the telephone began. When you had a conversation on the phone, you could at least be sure that the voice on the other end of the line belonged to a real person. Today we are surrounded by sounds and images that never corresponded to anything real—lifelike photos of people who were never born, intimate conversations with machines that can’t think or feel. The great irony of the telephone is that a device born as a tool of communication, bringing people together across distances, has evolved into our most effective tool of solipsism. No long-distance call can bridge the space between two people absorbed in their phones, even when they’re sitting side by side.

If you don’t speak with a relative for years, you’re probably estranged. If you’re dating someone who’s dropped contact for the past month, you’ve been ghosted. You haven’t heard from your boss in six months? Uh-oh. But one kind of relationship seems to consistently defy the laws of interpersonal physics: friendship. When friends reunite, the fabric of time can pleat seamlessly, allowing them to simply, as the saying goes, pick up where they left off.

“Time doesn’t take away from true friendship, nor does separation,” Tennessee Williams wrote in Memoirs. “When we’re together, the years fall away,” Judy Blume once wrote of her best friend, Mary. In the 100 interviews with groups of friends that I conducted for my series The Friendship Files, I often heard similar sentiments. “It was like we’d never been apart,” one person told me of reconnecting with his Army buddies after more than a decade. “It’s as if nothing has changed,” another said of their relationship with friends from kindergarten.

If friendship has a superpower, it is the time travel of reconnection. Before these moments, sometimes weeks, months, or years pass with only sporadic communication, if not complete silence. Yet through these fallow periods, friendship can persist without diminishing in strength.

I’ve come to think of these empty spaces as friendship’s dark matter. It is the part of friendship we can’t see, directly measure, or definitively prove exists—but we know it’s there, and we feel its effects. In a 1991 paper, Stuart Sigman, a communication researcher, emphasized the importance of the part of relationships that happens in the silence between interactions. “Relationships do not cease after the face-to-face events (conversations) engaged in by co-members are terminated,” he wrote. “In a sense, relationships are ‘larger’ than the physical presence or interactional accessibility of the participants.”

Although all relationships have some of this dark matter, friendship is unusual because of how much silence it can survive. Someone doesn’t necessarily lose the title of “friend” just because you haven’t talked to them in a while. With family or romantic partners, a reunion after a long separation could be expected to bring some drama. Yet seeing an old friend is often described as frictionless.

[Read: How friendships change in adulthood]

The nature of friendship allows for this goodwill. “Freedom lives at the heart of friendship,” William Rawlins, an emeritus professor of communication at Ohio University, told me. You can’t choose your parents, but every friendship is created from scratch by the participants themselves. It can take any form they imagine. Cultures have shared expectations for how romantic relationships progress and when families ought to gather. Although couples and families don’t necessarily conform to those scripts, they’re still aware of them.

Those cultural guideposts barely exist for friendship. There is no universal prescription for how often friends ought to talk to or see one another. “Our friendship is going to exist as long as we are living up to each other’s expectations of the friendship,” Rawlins said. Friendships can go dormant for a while without dying, he wrote in his 1992 book, Friendship Matters, so long as all parties still think of one another as friends: “The unspoken in friendship, its shared image, may excuse former neglect.”

So what is happening when nothing is happening between friends? This is the question Kinneret Lahad, a professor of women’s studies at Tel Aviv University, explored in a paper she published last year. The short answer, as she wrote, is that when nothing happens, “everything happens at the same time.” The full answer is more complicated.

“Friendship is imbued with so many sensations, so many intensities that cannot be quantified or qualified or represented in narratives,” Lahad told me. (I’ll try my best to represent some of them in this narrative nevertheless.) Nothing visible or measurable may be happening between two friends; they may be, by all appearances, out of touch. But perhaps they think of each other often. Maybe one helped the other find a job that took them to a new place many years ago; their whole life trajectory remains shaped by that friendship, even though they’re now apart. One friend might see something that makes them think of the other, wonder whether they should reach out, and almost do it, only to hesitate at the last moment. Any or all of that could be happening when a friendship appears dormant. “When nothing is happening in friendship, all of friendship’s ambiguities, possibilities, and constraints are laid bare,” Rawlins said. Lahad’s point is that all of these almosts, maybes, and intangible thoughts still matter. They still affect the friendship and the people within it. This dark matter can, like the universe’s, be the glue that keeps friends together, or it can be the shadows in which misunderstandings and resentments fester.

One indirect piece of evidence for the existence of friendship dark matter is that the feeling of closeness between friends is not necessarily linked to how often they are in touch. In a study of long-distance and geographically close friends, Amy Janan Johnson, a professor of communication at the University of Oklahoma, found that although far-flung friends did fewer “maintenance behaviors”—such as calling each other, joking around together, and offering emotional support—that did not mean they felt less connected or invested in the relationship.

In fact, she told me, some long-distance friends’ commitment to the relationship was “still increasing.” Johnson suggested that in addition to the active maintenance of talking to and seeing one another, people might also engage in passive maintenance of their friendships by, for instance, looking at old photos and feeling fond feelings, or thinking of someone and wishing them well. These things seem small and ephemeral, but they are ways that friendship can be present even in absence. And they may contribute to the feeling, upon reunion, that the friendship was never interrupted at all.

[Read: Want closer friendships? Move away from your friends.]

Lahad recently published another paper, with her friend and co-author Klara Goedecke, about the feeling of reconnecting after a period of nothingness. And in the most scholarly way possible, they basically said that it’s magic: “Sensed suspension of the sociotemporal order that would otherwise cast time apart as rupture has a ‘mythical’ or ‘extra-temporal’ quality,” they wrote, “which we conceptualize as a sense of wonder.”

This wonder is not guaranteed. Let us not over-romanticize the flexibility of friendship; it is completely possible that the dark matter contains not the comforting certainty of continued connection, but confusion and hurt. Friends can drift apart over long separations, and reuniting may be awkward rather than smooth. “Meeting up with an old friend constituted a friendship trial,” Lahad and Goedecke wrote, “a moment where the friendship was put to the test, as it were.” Reconnection comes with risk. Although you may have thought that everything was fine, that it was mutually understood that you still loved each other through the silence, your friend might be upset with you for not reaching out. “Treasured old friendships persist partly as vital (and hopefully mutual) fantasies,” Rawlins wrote. The risk is that the fantasy is not mutual.

As technology has made keeping in touch easier and easier, the dark matter of friendship may feel heavier than it did in previous generations. Rawlins and Johnson both pointed out that for those who didn’t grow up with the internet and social media, and had only calls and letters to bridge the distance, long silences between friends may have felt more benign. Friends have so many ways to reach out to one another now that it may feel more fraught when they don’t.

This sense that reconnection is risky could be why, as a couple of studies have found, people tend to be reluctant to contact their old friends. Even when they say they’d like to, many hesitate to actually do it. But the risk may not be as high as they think. Another study found that people tend to consistently underestimate how happy those in their social circle are to hear from them.

What makes a friendship more or less likely to pick up where it left off? Kristina Castaneto, a psychology Ph.D. student at Simon Fraser University, in Canada, is trying to investigate what makes a person more likely to reconnect with an old friend; her adviser, Lara Aknin, worked on the study about reluctance to reach out. In a recent study, they could not find any personality traits that were linked to someone’s likelihood of getting back in touch. The only thing that seemed to move the needle was a new concept they’ve come up with: friendship resiliency, which they define as “the belief that the friendship will remain despite how much time has passed,” Castaneto told me. That sounds, essentially, like a belief in friendship dark matter. She is still working on developing a way to reliably measure this concept. But it seems possible that the very thing that brings friends back together might be the belief that they’re never truly separated, even when they’re apart.

Yet I can’t help but wonder how much this phenomenon is a special property of friendship, and how much it is a survival mechanism in a culture that puts friendship last. Lahad said that she and Goedecke got a similar critique from a colleague regarding their paper on reconnection and wonder. People should be working on their friendships, this person said, not coasting on the assumption that the time apart will collapse when they get back together. The dominant values of American culture—and many other cultures—still say that you should prioritize your work and your family before your friends. When friendship is expected to make way for these other things, people may get used to tolerating lapses in contact, or think that they have no choice but to accept them as part of friendship.

The “assumption of benevolence” through a dormant period, as Rawlins put it, can be a beautiful part of friendship. But “at the same time, Julie, at some point, you kind of have to live that with each other,” he told me. You have to prove the benevolent assumption correct. You have to actually pick up where you left off. That’s the thing about friendship dark matter—it can hold the visible matter together … so long as there is some visible matter.

Friendship does require taking action, at some point, if you want it to be more than a fond memory. But it is more than the sum of the actions taken. Even if you do make your friends a priority, even if you are regularly in touch, the magic, unmeasurable dark matter remains. Lahad told me about a woman she’d interviewed whose friend had offered her a room to stay in when she was thinking of divorcing her husband. She never took her friend up on it, but just knowing that she had that safety net if she needed it helped give her the confidence to take action and get divorced. Something that wasn’t actually happening was shaping everything. “Friendship also exists in these ways that cannot be boiled down only to how often we meet and how often we talk,” Lahad said.

In the dark matter, there is uncertainty, yes. But, as Rawlins said, “the other side of uncertainty is faith.”
