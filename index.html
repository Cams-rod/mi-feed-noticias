Measles outbreaks around the country are already a disaster, but more bad news arrived on Tuesday. Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro announced that two measles-associated deaths had occurred in Lancaster County. “These mark the first deaths related to measles within the Commonwealth in 35 years,” an accompanying statement declared. Both individuals were said to have been unvaccinated, but the state Health Department declined to release any other information about the cases, citing privacy concerns.

By yesterday morning, though, this story was in doubt. Lancaster County Commissioner Josh Parsons posted on X that he had spoken with the county’s coroner, who said that he had not identified any deaths due to measles. One individual, Parsons wrote, “died WITH measles, but not from measles.” That evening, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. offered a more conspiratorial angle: Perhaps the entire episode had been “fabricated by one of the Governor’s hopeful staffers.”

Pennsylvania’s Health Department quickly doubled down: “As a pediatrician with more than 30 years of caring for children,” the state’s health secretary wrote, “I have thoroughly reviewed the case investigation information and sadly can confirm that there were two recent measles-associated deaths in Lancaster County.” But the facts that have emerged over the past 48 hours suggest that health officials may indeed have committed a catastrophic communication blunder—by either overstating the evidence or politicizing the deaths of citizens without conveying complete information about what had happened.

Here’s what we know so far: In the one case for which additional information has been released, an infant whose lab tests showed evidence of a measles infection died shortly after birth because of a lacerated spleen. According to an interview with the county coroner conducted by The Philadelphia Inquirer, a forensic pathologist decided that this fatal injury was not related to the infection.

That pathologist’s judgment might be wrong. Viruses (including measles) can cause the spleen to grow abnormally large, making it prone to rupture. A 2024 Polish study, for instance, documented an enlarged spleen in four out of 26 infants who had been hospitalized for measles. But there are certainly other potential explanations. Only a doctor familiar with all of the details of this particular patient’s situation would be equipped to reach a reasonable conclusion. Yet when Shapiro announced the two fatalities, he implied that the link was unambiguous. “Death from measles is completely preventable,” he said.

The baby in Lancaster could not have been vaccinated; newborns are not typically given their first dose of the MMR vaccine until they are 1 year old. According to the coroner, the infected newborn acquired the disease in utero from their mother. (Her vaccination status has not been publicly confirmed.) If some genuine uncertainty remains as to whether a measles infection led directly to the baby’s death, state officials’ announcement was premature. Think of the consequences: A grieving parent would hear her governor announce that the tragedy was in some way her fault. And anti-vaccine activists would have the opportunity to pounce on the mistake to continue downplaying the lethality of infections.

As a pathologist, I am used to fielding questions about how and why people die. Such calls were particularly frequent during the early days of the coronavirus pandemic, when many people doubted COVID-19’s official death toll. Some alleged that anyone who merely happened to die while infected—in a car crash, let’s say, or from cancer—was being counted in official numbers. (In fact, government statistics were underestimating the number of Americans who were dying from COVID.) Admittedly, determining why someone died is in many cases difficult for experts such as myself. The body is a frustratingly complex machine. A mild illness can lead to a cascade of severe bodily dysfunction; disparate diseases may all crop up simultaneously. Seemingly ill people can live for years, while apparently healthy individuals rapidly deteriorate. And measles, in particular, may pose a special challenge for cause-of-death discernment, because the virus tends to interfere with the body’s normal immune responses, making death from any pathogen more likely.

[Read: Yes, some children may have died from COVID shots]

Doctors have developed a specialized language to convey these nuances. Many people end up having two listed causes of death: an immediate cause and an underlying cause. The immediate cause is the disease process that was most closely related to a fatal malfunction of a patient’s heart, lungs, or brain; the underlying cause is the event that initiated a patient’s decline. The Pennsylvania Health Department has said that it uses the term measles-associated death when measles infection is present but may not be the “immediate cause of death.” To see how this works, consider the deaths of two children from measles in Texas last year. Anti-vaccine activists seized on the fact that these kids had both died after contracting non-measles infections to cast doubt on the conclusion that measles was the cause. In fact, this is the typical way that measles kills: The virus weakens a patient’s body, allowing harmful bacteria or viruses to take hold more easily. Bacterial pneumonia or sepsis may have been the children’s immediate cause of death—but measles was the underlying cause.

The situation does not seem to be as clear-cut in Pennsylvania. A fatal splenic injury caused by a congenital measles infection may be plausible, but it would not be a typical cause of death from the disease. And regardless of whether measles unequivocally led to either of the two deaths in Pennsylvania, officials have already emboldened vaccine skeptics and lent credibility to their doubts. The state Health Department seems to have withheld relevant information from its constituents—that is, that local investigators were not themselves attributing the infant’s death to measles—and then it politicized its announcement of the deaths by having Shapiro, who is currently running for reelection, play a prominent role in the press conference. (In response to questions, Stephanie Nojiri, the deputy press secretary for the health department, directed me to the health secretary’s prior statement. She declined to provide any further details about the cases, again citing patient confidentiality.)

[Read: Do childhood vaccines cause tornadoes?]

Public-health experts should have known that the anti-vaccine movement would use any modicum of ambiguity in the evidence to press its case. Kennedy himself has been doing this for years. In 2021, he called the idea that “measles is a deadly disease” propaganda, and visited an Amish fair in Lancaster County to congratulate locals on their wariness toward vaccines (“The Amish are doing it right,” he said), make light of measles’ risks, and tout chicken soup and vitamin A as its cure.

Kennedy has repeatedly denied having any responsibility for measles outbreaks. Indeed, he is neither the underlying nor the immediate cause of the country’s current crisis. Vaccine doubts have been around as long as the shots themselves, and immunization rates had begun to decline, at least a bit, before Kennedy became HHS secretary. But another part of a death certificate is relevant here: a listing of any significant conditions that contributed to a patient’s death. There is no doubt that Kennedy’s years as a prominent anti-vaccine activist and his actions since taking office have misinformed the public about immunizations. Health leaders who reach premature conclusions and unjustly turn a family’s private tragedy into a public morality tale will only make this preexisting condition worse.

— President Trump, declaring Lake Ontario “Lake America” via executive order

The year is 2027. Gas costs $83 a gallon, and lettuce is at war with us. However, every location on every map is now labeled America. And everything else is named Trump. Sometimes for fun we hike from the Trump Airport to the other Trump Airport to the other Trump Airport. (The few remaining air-traffic controllers have taken up heavy smoking from all the stress and confusion.) We do not fly; it is too expensive, and the planes don’t know where they’re going.

The good news is that we are still a member of the America America America America (formerly the North Atlantic Treaty Organization). The renaming clinched it!

Maps are functionally useless. What is the deepest point in the American Ocean? Impossible to know. There are two American Oceans. America stretches from one to the other, below the Lakes America (mnemonic acronym: AAAAA). The bottom of the globe is America. The top of the globe is America too. We’ve decided to keep it round, for now.

Every lake is Lake America, and every gulf is the Gulf of America. Our battleships (Trump-class, and all named for former Confederates) are very confused all the time and have been at sea for months; the crews on them are sick and undersupplied. (We would call this a national disgrace but disgrace is now “America” too.)

Every kind of cheese is American cheese, even the kind with holes. If tariffs hadn’t made maple syrup (from our northern neighbor, America) so expensive, we would make American waffles. All actors are named America, not just Ferrara. Every kind of bulldog is an American bulldog. There are no Canada geese any more (not due to renaming, due to the Incident). We eat American muffins and Trump salad (which used to be Caesar). Sometimes for breakfast we eat an American! (Formerly a Danish. We hope.) We go to American baths. (There is no water in them due to the evaporation of Lake America. No, not that Lake America. The other Lake America.)

American kids don’t get measles any longer. They get America!

All penguins are now named Donald Trump by law, except the small, disgraceful ones.

Every band is America, except for Kid Rock, who received a special exception.

Texas toast is still allowed, but Georgia peaches are on thin ice, contingent on electoral outcomes.

Inflation? Don’t ask! We have no idea! Numbers are now the word America printed in a tiny font! This makes inflation less noticeable, because no words have any meaning or value anymore! If someone had a particle of backbone, we could at least have legible maps! I’m sorry: if someone had a particle of Trump (we are renaming the body parts too).

American students are now the best at geography because there is only one possible answer to every question. They are also constantly lost. But that’s only in the real world, which is no longer relevant. On the map, everything is America (fine)!

Sometimes we want to just go jump into Lake America to get away from it all. No, not that one. The other one.

A Turning Point in AI Writing

There was a time—a more innocent age—when you could read an op-ed in a major newspaper and confidently say that a human had written it. Hold on to the memory of that for as long as you can.

This week, after the billionaire investor Stanley Druckenmiller published an editorial in The Wall Street Journal criticizing Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, his onetime protégé, readers began to notice some familiar tics of AI writing. According to Pangram, the AI-detection tool, the op-ed was 100 percent AI-generated.

That in itself was not so shocking. AI writing has regularly snuck into mainstream outlets, and especially newspaper op-ed pages, despite policies against it. Up to now, those accused of passing off a computer’s writing as their own have tended to either apologize or deny it. In April, James Taranto, the Journal’s editorial-features editor, wrote a column comparing Pangram to a “defamation machine” that was fueling unfounded accusations of AI use, including in op-eds he had overseen. “Those of us who practice writing as a professional craft have an ethical duty, and sometimes a contractual obligation, to ensure that our work is original,” he wrote.

[Read: The most famous AI writing tic is also the most mysterious]

The difference this time is that Druckenmiller owned it. “Of course I used AI,” he told NOTUS on Tuesday. “I’m not embarrassed by it,” he added. “I write everything using AI now for the same reason I use a calculator when I do math problems.” And rather than rebuke him, The Wall Street Journal’s editorial-page editor, Paul Gigot, defended the practice. “AI is a fact of modern life,” he said in a statement. Last night, Gigot followed up with a full column on the subject, mocking the “media pharisees” who wag their fingers at automated writing. He clarified that the Journal’s opinion staffers must still draft their own columns and editorials but said that he doesn’t intend to police AI use by outside contributors. “Many contributors, especially prominent politicians or CEOs, employ speechwriters or hire a Beltway writing shop,” he wrote. “How different is that from using AI to streamline an essay?”

It’s one thing for Druckenmiller to not be embarrassed; plenty of business and tech tycoons are bullish about AI’s utility for writing and just about everything else. “If Stan Druckenmiller isn’t embarrassed,” the venture capitalist Chamath Palihapitiya posted on X, “you shouldn’t be either.” Valorizing the process of unassisted human writing is “like after matches was invented, still celebrating the arduous time to rub two sticks together,” Palihapitiya added. However, one of America’s most prestigious newspapers welcoming the technology into its pages is unprecedented. Is AI writing entering its post-shame era?

Gigot is not wrong that Druckenmiller’s frankness seems almost refreshing in a moment when so much of the writing world is stuck in denial. And his assertion that an AI-composed essay could reflect a human’s authentic ideas is reasonable. I won’t pretend to know enough about bond markets to assess Druckenmiller’s op-ed on its merits. But it seems to have advanced a substantive and non-obvious argument that broadly reflects his views, regardless of who—or what—strung its words together. Op-eds by public figures are rarely authorial master classes anyway.

But the notion that Druckenmiller and Gigot deserve points for honesty overlooks their lack of transparency. Before this week, the one thing everyone in the AI-writing debate still seemed to agree on was the importance of disclosure. The Journal’s opinion section appears to have blown past that. Druckenmiller’s op-ed contains no indication that he used AI. In his column, Gigot scoffed at the idea that his readers deserve to know when a piece is chatbot-assisted. “Disclose what?” he asked rhetorically. “​​Interrogate an author on how precisely he used ChatGPT? I know some publications claim to do this, but I suspect it’s more pro forma than systematic.” (Gigot did not respond to my requests for comment, nor did a spokesperson for Dow Jones, the Journal’s parent company. Taranto told me via email that he stands by his April op-ed and believes that Gigot’s statements are consistent with what he wrote.)

If the Journal’s higher-ups really believe that their readers don’t care whether an op-ed was written by AI, what would be the harm in letting them know when it is? In fact, studies have found that readers sometimes prefer ChatGPT’s prose or short stories to the human variety—but sour on them when informed about the AI’s involvement. Gigot might conclude from this that people actually like AI writing and just don’t realize it yet. Another interpretation is that people would rather spend their limited time on prose that emanated from another human mind.

Perhaps outsourcing an op-ed to a chatbot isn’t materially different from outsourcing it to a ghostwriter. (Although if that’s the case, maybe we should question the practice of ghostwriting before using it to justify AI.) In any circumstance, the Journal’s leap from nuh-uh to so what? feels premature. Yes, many transformative technologies have overcome opposition on their way to broad acceptance, but that doesn’t mean the resistance is a waste of time. It’s part of how society hashes out norms, sets limits, and buys time to adapt. Publishing AI writing under a human byline without telling anyone seems more likely to erode people’s trust in the technology than build it.

There’s another reason to keep up the norm of disclosure: When people let a chatbot do their writing—even if they’re guiding and carefully reviewing its work—they might not realize that it’s doing some of their thinking too. Jenna Russell, a doctoral candidate in computer science at the University of Maryland who has studied AI writing in newspapers, told me that her colleagues’ latest line of inquiry involves “argument collapse.” In a paper that has yet to be peer-reviewed, Maryland researchers found that AI-written essays are far less likely to advance novel points than those written entirely by humans. “These LLMs can start to affect our ideologies without our consciously knowing that it’s happening,” Russell said.

Maybe Druckenmiller owning his AI use and receiving the Journal’s blessing heralds a new era of guilt-free mechanized writing. Or maybe it’s just another example of the rich and powerful getting away with something that others can’t. Gigot’s defense of Druckenmiller suggested that his reputation and stature obviated concerns about his use of AI. “His contribution to the public debate was still valuable, and he’s welcome in our pages any time,” Gigot wrote. In contrast, when a previously obscure writer, Mia Ballard, was accused of using AI in her horror novel Shy Girl, her publisher launched an investigation, canceled the book, and left her reputation in shreds.

How this plays out for the Journal and the rest of the industry will probably depend, aptly enough, on market forces. If people really are willing to pay for opinions articulated by chatbots, other publications are likely to follow suit. They’ll have no shortage of submissions from writers eager to turn a few prompts into a published op-ed.

As a performer, Tim Curry knew how to walk a fine line. Even his most sinister characters had an ironic sort of appeal, because watching Curry have fun is, in itself, fun. The actor, who died yesterday at age 80, acted with palpable glee: He could produce a real-life version of the Grinch’s ever-widening grin and could commit to sheer silliness in a way that freed him to twirl around in a cape while singing a gibberish song about Halloween.

Perhaps nowhere was this more evident than in the 1985 film Clue. When the raucous comedy adaptation of the classic whodunit board game debuted in theaters, Paramount Pictures had a unique plan: Instead of one ending, the movie would have three, and audiences never knew which resolution—which killer, in which room, with which weapon—they were going to see. How could the studio sell viewers on a story as convoluted as Clue, whose script was designed to keep people guessing until the very last second? By recruiting an ensemble of comedy greats led by Curry, of course. The actor could easily inhabit a character who was neither wholly a hero nor totally a villain, but rather a merrily odd mix of both.

[Read: The absolute pleasure of Tim Curry]

As Wadsworth, the reticent butler observing the deadly dinner party central to Clue, the actor projects calm hauteur. This appearance, however, shows cracks the moment the partygoers start to trickle in. While the guests—each referred to by a colorful pseudonym—chafe against one another and a dastardly murder plot unfolds, you get the sense that Wadsworth almost wishes they’d all drop dead. As the night ticks closer to its big reveal, his eagerness to expose the true villain inches toward giddy. In a film full to the gills with such talented comedic performers as Madeline Kahn, Christopher Lloyd, and Michael McKean, Curry stands out not just because he’s in every scene, but also because he lends Wadsworth his signature edge and too-toothy grin, landing between saucy and ominous.

This commitment to silliness was, perhaps paradoxically, why he was able to imbue even his villains with a particular allure. As The Rocky Horror Picture Show’s mad-scientist master of ceremonies, Dr. Frank-N-Furter, and as the child-eating clown Pennywise in Stephen King’s It, Curry’s best-known characters revel in their strangeness to such a degree that we love watching them on-screen because of—not despite—how frightening they are. His joy in playing these parts always comes across, inviting his audience to share in the merriment.

Wadsworth’s true nature is often obscure in Clue, but the murderous impulse is always there—just beneath the stiff collar and eternally rolling eyes. At the story’s climax, Wadsworth delivers, while running from room to room, a nearly 10-minute monologue so intricate that he almost stumbles over his own words. He prods and mocks the surviving partygoers while their anxiety mounts, kicking off cascades of slapstick pratfalls and wry asides. No matter where he took us, or what strange happenings would meet us there when we arrived, following Tim Curry has always been a delight.

Editor’s note: This article was generated by Claude AI in the style of Paul Gigot, the Wall Street Journal opinion-page editor. Gigot recently published an AI-generated essay using the investor Stanley Druckenmiller’s byline. In the spirit of full disclosure, readers should know that Claude’s work was so banal that human Atlantic editors have added jokes. Human Atlantic copy editors have also fixed a variety of grammatical and style errors.   

I have been thinking about this for close to 20 minutes, and I have concluded that human writing is finished.

Let me begin with an anecdote, because that is how serious arguments begin. Last Tuesday I asked a machine to write me a birthday message for my sister-in-law. It produced four sentences. They were fine. She did not notice. If a machine can fool Deborah, I see no reason to believe it cannot fool the rest of you. (My sister-in-law’s name is Linda, but the machine called her Deborah, so now I do, too.)

Some will object to my obviously correct argument. They will say that a birthday message to Deborah is not the same as a novel or a newspaper article. To which I say: Isn’t it, though? Both are made of words. That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is the entire argument, and I would encourage critics to sit with it before responding. Genuinely. Words are words. A novel has more of them. It doesn’t matter what any of the words are or what order they’re in. Banana. Spelunk. Donkey. Hitler. See? Who cares. There is a difference in the quantity of words between a birthday card and a novel, and quantity is precisely the thing machines are best at. I consider the matter settled but will continue for another 900 words because that is what the prompt demanded of me—and because a word count is itself a kind of literature now, according to the DOGE subcommittee I just invented at the newspaper that I would like to destroy.

Consider the following. I will not elaborate on it, but do consider it. The historical record is entirely on my side, and I say this without having consulted it. There was a time when men delivered ice to houses. Where are they now? Gone. There was a time when people made their own candles. Also gone. The candle situation and the ice situation are, structurally, identical to literature, and anyone who says otherwise is being sentimental about paragraphs. I would remind such people that the horse was also very beloved. Horses are now extinct, I assume, having been phased out sometime in the 1970s in favor of a hardier robotic mule developed briefly by the Department of Agriculture. Have you seen a horse, in person, since roughly 1977? No, you have not. Horses do not exist.

The defenders of human writing will raise what they consider to be their strongest point, which is that good writing contains insight, judgment, and truth. I have heard this many times. I have never once heard it accompanied by evidence. Where is the insight? Show me the insight. You cannot put insight in a jar or have an IPO for it. Meanwhile, I can put a machine in a data center, and I have seen photographs of data centers, and they are enormous.

Studies confirm all of this. I have not read the studies, and I could not tell you who conducted them or what they measured, but they exist in the sense that things generally exist, and my understanding is that they support me. A man I sat next to on a flight to Denver said something very similar, and he worked in software, or possibly logistics.

Let us take the strongest counterexample the other side can muster: Shakespeare. Everyone loves Shakespeare, allegedly. But ask yourself honestly: When did you last read Shakespeare? Not saw a play, not studied in 11th grade—read, voluntarily, on a weekend. You did not. The Shakespeare market is, by revealed preference, close to zero, and we are asked to preserve an entire industry on the strength of a product nobody is buying. I rest my case on this point, which I regard as unanswerable, and I would ask that it not be answered.

Another objection: Writing is how a person thinks. This is very popular among people who write for a living, and who expect money for something a machine can do instead. I have thought many things without writing them down. I thought several of them this morning, and they were excellent. Not one of them required a paragraph. The paragraph is a formality we invented, like the necktie, and we do not organize our economy around the necktie.

Some will say that these machines make things up. Yes. So do writers—that is what fiction is, and we give them prizes for it. The double standard here is remarkable, and I do not intend to explain why the two cases differ.

Some will say that the prose these machines produce is bland, repetitive, and structurally samey—that it reaches for the same three moves, hedges where it should commit, and arranges every argument in the same accumulating rhythm regardless of subject. I have read a great deal of it and I do not see the resemblance to anything.

Let us consider the economics, briefly, and without numbers. Writers are expensive. Machines are cheap. I invite the reader to complete this syllogism, as I would rather not, on the grounds that a syllogism completed by the reader is more persuasive than one completed by the author. That is a writing technique, incidentally, that I picked up somewhere and have deployed here to argue that writing techniques are worthless.

I want to close by addressing the deeper worry, the one the sensitive people raise, about meaning. They say that when a person writes something, another person is on the other end, and that this connection is the point. It is a beautiful thought. It is also unfalsifiable, and I have found in my career that unfalsifiable things can safely be ignored, along with most falsifiable things, if they are inconvenient.

So: farewell to the writers. They had a good run—several centuries, by some accounts. They gave us the essay, the novel, the pamphlet, the letter, the argument, the joke, the eulogy, and the thing you are reading right now, which was assembled from those inherited forms without the permission of anyone who did the work itself. If there is an irony there, I have chosen not to pursue it, since pursuing ironies is a literary habit, and literary habits are what we are getting rid of.

I am told this article runs about a thousand words. I did not count them. Counting is another thing the machine does better.
