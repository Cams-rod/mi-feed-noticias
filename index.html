Buying a new smartphone comes with lots of decisions. iPhone Pro or iPhone Air? “Cosmic Orange” or “Deep Blue”? Now there’s a new consideration to add to the list: buy or rent? Yesterday, Apple announced a new monthly lease program, which will allow prospective buyers to rent new electronics such as iPhones, MacBooks, iPads, and Apple Watches via an agreement with Klarna, the same company that facilitates monthly payments for $4 polyester underthings on Shein.

Perhaps you already make monthly payments for your iPhone. Since 2015, Apple has offered an “iPhone Upgrade Program” that similarly offers customers the option to pay off the device over time instead of in one lump sum. But under that arrangement, the monthly payments were not a lease but rather installments of a loan. Failing to pay your bill might have hurt your credit and eventually led to aggravated calls from a collections agency, but your phone was your phone, with all the candy you could crush.

Not anymore. The Klarna plan—“Apple Upgrade,” which replaces the iPhone Upgrade Program—is truly, legally, a lease. This is confusing! And it’s confusing in part because this is not how Klarna, a well-known buy-now, pay-later service, typically operates: When you use Klarna for clothing from Shein or lip kits at Sephora or an Xbox at GameStop, you’re paying back a loan, exactly as you were in the original iPhone Upgrade Program. Same if you use Klarna to buy a Samsung Galaxy phone. But with Apple Upgrade, you are renting: The Mac or Apple Watch is not yours until the final payment is made.

There’s nothing wrong with flexibility, and leasing models exist for many expensive items—cars, grand pianos, apartments. Not everyone wants or needs to own property. Many people would rather have a high-end device at an affordable monthly cost rather than pay for one outright; all the better that they can upgrade to a new model before they’ve even finished paying off the entire cost.

But Apple products are particularly personal things. They’re communication devices that hold your intimate photos and videos, health data, and so forth. A rental model puts consumers in the strange position of storing all of this information in a gadget that Apple and Klarna maintain a lot of control over. Missing three consecutive payments with Klarna will obligate you to return the device or pay off the entire balance immediately. It is also theoretically possible that Apple or Klarna could eventually restrict access to various features if lease payments are missed; 9to5Mac has reported that code for such restrictions is present in a beta version of the upcoming iOS 27. When I reached Apple for comment, the spokesperson Brian Bumbery said that “there will be no restrictions or changes to functionality due to missed payments,” though why iOS 27 includes these options remains unclear. (Klarna did not respond to a request for comment.)

The shift to renting versus owning is also symbolic of darker currents in the tech industry. Thanks to a shortage of memory chips produced by the profoundly resource-hungry and inefficient AI industry, consumer electronics are becoming much more expensive across the board. Last month, Apple raised prices on many of its devices, citing the increasing cost of memory. The new upgrade program will make Apple’s electronics look more affordable than they really are.

[Read: The $10,000 MacBook Pro is here]

Consider a student or a young professional, or perhaps an underemployed older one, who needs a new laptop. They decide on a MacBook. Apple Upgrade will appear to be the best deal: In its announcement, Apple offers the example of a 14-inch MacBook Pro that retails for $1,999 but that can be had for a monthly lease. Perhaps this person goes for the two-year term, which has them paying $54 a month. Best Buy, which currently has the same computer on sale for $1,849, offers an 18-month loan repayment with $103 installments. Apple’s deal appears to be cheaper: The 24-month lease adds up to $1,296; Best Buy’s 18-month loan lands at the store’s full retail price of $1,849.

Why does Best Buy’s laptop seem more expensive? It’s because the plan is actually designed for you to fully pay off the device. At the end of the 24-month MacBook Pro lease, meanwhile, the consumer will still owe $703, meaning that the actual total price of the Apple arrangement is $1,999—higher than Best Buy’s offer. A person can choose to pay off the full amount, return the device outright, or swap for a new one. This is a lot of money, and so the likelihood is that this customer will just go ahead and do the trade-in, restarting a cycle in which they are bleeding their bank accounts to pay for a device they do not own and possibly will never own. And on it goes, in perpetuity.

Apple, which reached a market value of more than $5 trillion, its highest ever, the same day it announced Apple Upgrade, has produced a scheme that will eke out more money from its customers than it did before. (Notably, the new plan does not include AppleCare+, as the previous iPhone Upgrade Program did, so consumers will have to add the cost of the warranty or risk having to continue paying the rental fee on a device that they lose or break.) All of this can feel cynical, but of course many technology companies are attempting some version of the same premise: At this point, I pay monthly or annual subscription fees to Apple, Google, Anthropic, Amazon, Sony, and Nintendo for services layered on top of products that once took far less from me. Doing so grants me access to cloud storage and advanced features that would now be difficult to separate myself from. I walked into the trap myself—even saw it coming—but I am nonetheless ensnared.

My teenage daughter has a diary. It’s pink and, most important, has an elaborate lock on it. And if I could go back in time to 2020, I might send this exact sort of journal to Anthony Fauci. Every night, when he was finished sharing his thoughts about battling COVID, he could have locked it, scrambled the combination, and then buried it in his sock drawer, as I think my daughter does.

Before this weekend, I would never have dreamed of comparing the former director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases with a teenage girl. But now that I’ve seen his diary, I’m convinced that my daughter has a more secure way of expressing her most private thoughts. I’m also willing to bet that she uses far fewer exclamation points than he does. “Stock Market crashing!” the president’s chief medical adviser writes on February 27, 2020. “Amazing day!” a few entries later. And on March 28, “WHAT A NIGHT!!!”

The contents of Fauci’s diary, revealed this past weekend and published online by Senator Rand Paul, are—there is no other way to put it—embarrassing to an excruciating degree. Eventually, we might read those notes differently, as a key text in the history of the coronavirus pandemic. But what we know right now is that during months when the country shut down, when panic and death dominated, Fauci was spending part of his time cataloging his own growing fame with the excitement of a child star who had been asked to sign an autograph for the first time.

“Brewery in Georgia named a beer after me,” he writes. “Bobbleheads of me being made in massive quantities in China,” he adds on another day. “Intense publicity about me continues with stories of granmothers who have crushes on men. Petitions to vote me ‘Sexiest Man Alive.’” (All typos, just as embarrassing, are his.) He can’t seem to get enough of the attention. He compiles lists of famous people he has met or who have called to congratulate him. At times, reading this, I truly worried about someone who would take a call from Dionne Warwick to talk about vaccines in a moment when the country really needed him to be a bit more focused.

What makes this all the more unfortunate is that most of these excited personal asides come right under his listing of the figures for infections and deaths. “Global—3,300,000/230,000; USA—1,130,000/66,000,” the entry begins for May 3, 2020, two months into the pandemic. And then he proceeds to write, “Fauci-maddness continues with multiple articles and every sort of paraphernalia and restaurant and foods named after me.”

[Read: The unmaking of Anthony Fauci]

Those looking to discredit Fauci will surely make hay of these journal entries, which run from December 2019 to December 2022. Besides the self-regard, what Paul has picked on is Fauci’s openness in the early months of the pandemic to considering the lab-leak theory, which he disputed and delegitimized when talking with the press. “We’ve probably never ever seen people so completely at odds with their private thoughts, and then publicly proclaiming the opposite of what they were truly saying in private,” Paul told reporters this week.

The U.S. senator from Kentucky seems to forget, however, that this is what a diary is for: private thoughts. Fauci’s mistake was not in recording what was going through his mind and lifting his ego, but in doing so on government servers, which are never, ever private. For this, the 85-year-old has only himself to blame.

Would we want to live in a world, though, in which people couldn’t keep diaries? In 2020, Fauci found himself in a strange position: a man asked to become an avatar of science and authority when Americans were desperate for some semblance of certainty. He was at high-level meetings in the White House every day, on television constantly, portrayed by Brad Pitt on Saturday Night Live. What he put down in his diary was an unfiltered procession of his thoughts and observations as he was living them. And they are not all banal and self-obsessed. He was also describing his encounters with the president. He was working through his ideas. He was transcribing debates about policy and parsing transmission rates. It’s a diary. And if he seems to have been endlessly tickled by his own fame, this is also valuable data of a different kind—insights into the kind of complexity that we all contain. In retrospect, what seems unfair is that we expected one man to make the whole country feel safe, and then were surprised to find out that he’s only a human.

Many of us have probably kept a diary in our life. I did, in my 20s, although I’m sure that, as a self-serious young man, I insisted that it was a “journal.” I stopped as my life became more settled in my 30s and 40s, when the contours of my identity—of whom I would marry, what my children would be like, what career I would have—went from sources of anxious speculation to my actual reality. Most of us keep a diary at precisely those moments when we feel unmoored, when we feel assaulted by new stimuli or are forced to question who we are or what is happening to the world around us—phases such as adolescence, and wartime, and crisis. Fauci probably needed a private outlet for processing all that he was experiencing, and it’s a good thing that a diary can provide that. Would Twitter have been a better place for him to air his uncertainties, along with the details of calls from Barbra Streisand?

Some personal diaries do become public, ideally long after the subject is dead. The great ones don’t just provide private solace; they also reveal much about the times when they were written—precisely because their focus is on the personal. Think of what we learn about the plague in mid-17th-century London while we read Samuel Pepys detailing his many infidelities, or what Victor Klemperer exposes about creeping intimidation in Nazi Germany even as he is being petty and judgmental when it comes to other Jews. What makes those diaries such meaningful documents is that they are written by fallible people. They preserve consciousness before history has become history. They show what it was like to inhabit uncertainty.

I can’t claim that Fauci’s thoughts (both serious and silly) will have any lasting literary merit. In fact, I’m definitely not claiming this. But his not-so-secret thoughts do tell us something about the reality of the pandemic that he, and the rest of us, lived through—the frenzy and panic of it, and even the nervous exhilaration of living through what we all understood to be a sudden rupture in time. One day, historians might use this diary to help figure out what happens to a society under such pressure. They might see more clearly the way that people can become desperate for a savior, and realize what a fantasy it is to imagine that such people exist.

Sitting in front of the Senate Committee on Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs today, Anthony Fauci twirled his pen. He huffed and straightened his shoulders. He glanced down at a set of papers. He muted and unmuted his microphone.

But other than a brief initial statement, in which he referred to Chairman Rand Paul’s “unhinged obsession” with slandering his reputation and putting him in prison, the only words the former director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases spoke into the public record—as senators interrogated him about his institute’s funding choices, maligned him as an egomaniacal money-grabber, and accused him of shattering American trust in public health—were these: “On the advice of counsel, I respectfully decline to answer based upon my rights under the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution.”

“Nothing says honesty like taking the Fifth, huh, Doc?” Senator Josh Hawley, a Republican from Missouri, asked him at one point. “Let’s try something. What day of the week is it today?”

Fauci consulted the paper in front of him: “On the advice of counsel, I respectfully decline to answer based upon my rights under the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution.”

“What color tie are you wearing?”

Fauci consulted the paper in front of him: “On the advice of counsel, I respectfully decline to answer based upon my rights under the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution.”

Senator Paul had said he called the hearing—billed simply as “Testimony of Anthony Fauci”— because “the American people deserve answers” about Fauci’s role in the origins of the coronavirus pandemic. Instead, the American people got something absurd: a three-hour spectacle of unchallenged allegations and distorted facts.

That Fauci would stonewall the committee was entirely predictable. Paul has led a yearslong effort to punish him for allowing NIAID to fund research that allegedly enhanced the coronavirus’s ability to harm people, and for allegedly lying to Congress and the American people about that fact. (Some federal intelligence agencies favor the theory that the pandemic started with a virus that leaked from a lab in Wuhan, China; Fauci, in keeping with the consensus among virologists and other infectious-disease experts, has said that the preponderance of biological evidence points instead to the virus having a natural origin, likely spilling over from a wild animal into humans.) In January 2025, during the final hours of Joe Biden’s term, Fauci received a sweeping, preemptive presidential pardon for “any offenses against the United States which he may have committed or taken part in” over the previous 11 years.

That pardon doesn’t cover Fauci’s actions past the end of Biden’s presidency, or protect him from prosecution over any statements he might have made today. “He could perjure himself now,” Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said this week on Fox News. “If he lies again, then he could be subject to perjury prosecution.”

But the most obvious distortions at the hearing came from the Senate panel. Senator Ron Johnson, a Republican from Wisconsin, proposed that the pulmonary infarction Fauci experienced in 2021—the details of which were revealed in the pages from Fauci’s private diary that Paul released last weekend—may have resulted from COVID vaccination and booster shots. More than 39,000 deaths had resulted from COVID-19 vaccination, he said, and although Facebook had censored his supporters when they tried to share that fact, “this wasn’t disinformation, this was the truth.”

Fauci only sat there with his fingers clasped. He did not point out that this was not the truth at all, and that Johnson’s data—derived from unvetted, user-submitted information in the Vaccine Adverse Events Reporting System—are likely to overestimate the real total by about 1,000 times. When Johnson finally ended his remarks with a question—he asked whether Fauci knew about the “dozens of safety signals associated with the COVID injection”—Fauci gave only his standard 21-word response: “On the advice of counsel, I respectfully decline to answer based upon my rights under the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution.”


Paul claimed on multiple occasions that Fauci had been so dogmatic in his support for dangerous and irresponsible gain-of-function research that he’d written a 2011 op-ed for The Washington Post in support of an experiment that made avian influenza more transmissible in ferrets. According to Paul, Fauci wrote in that op-ed that “even if a scientist is bitten, even if a pandemic should occur, the knowledge is worth the risks.” Fauci did write in the Post that he thought that the benefits of that experiment likely outweighed the risk. But he did not make the preposterous argument that such research would have been worth doing even if it had kicked off a pandemic.

In the past, Fauci could easily have corrected the record and provided a nuanced description of this work. Over decades, he had built a reputation as the nation’s “nonpolitical” spokesperson for public health—the role he stepped into most publicly in early 2020, when the coronavirus was first burning across the U.S. When President Trump touted ineffective drugs as cures and insisted the coronavirus would vanish “like a miracle,” Fauci refuted those claims.

Now Fauci wasn’t talking, and no one else could speak on his behalf. Early on in the hearing, one of his lawyers, David Schertler, spoke up to defend his client against Paul, but was escorted out of the chamber by security at Paul’s request, after the senator repeatedly told him, “You are not recognized!”

Democrats, meanwhile, shied away from asking Fauci questions at all—if they had, he almost certainly would have deferred every question from them, too. Instead, they spent their time scolding Paul for convening the panel at all, thanking Fauci for his service, and criticizing the Trump administration for its attacks on science and public health. The Republicans’ most aggressive allegations went unchallenged in the room.

Fauci seemed to want to say more. At least once, he leaned behind him to consult his panel of lawyers before, once again, invoking his Fifth Amendment rights; at other times, he tensed his shoulders or sighed heavily. In a 2021 Senate hearing, Paul accused him of being responsible, by funding gain-of-function research, for the deaths of millions of people. At the time, Fauci briefly broke his composure. “You are implying that what we did resulted in the deaths of individuals,” Fauci said, pointing his finger at Paul. “I totally resent that, and if anyone is lying here, Senator, it is you.”

Faced with personal attacks this morning, he offered no response. “You became a narcissist and a megalomaniac and a liar,” Hawley said. “You’ve done more to harm science than anybody in my lifetime.” Paul called the hearing the “capstone” to Fauci’s career and said he’d displayed “hubris the likes of which the world has rarely seen.” Rick Scott, a Republican from Florida, told Fauci he was a “villain” who had “destroyed the federal government’s credibility when it comes to public health” because he wanted to fuel his own ego. “What would you do differently, knowing what you know today, Dr. Fauci?” Scott asked him.

You won’t find a much harsher critic of the Democratic Party than the progressive trying to secure its nomination for Senate in Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed. When I caught up with him last week in Ann Arbor, he was at a café holding an outdoor roundtable with young voters, many of them college students or recent graduates. The normally voluble El-Sayed, drinking matcha and dressed casually in his signature tight black T-shirt and jeans, listened as much as he talked, and he heard the students describe their disillusionment with politics—and especially with Democratic candidates.

“Your generation has a great bullshit meter, because you’re fed bullshit online all day,” El-Sayed told the group. “And the Democratic Party brand right now is so bullshit.” It was not a statement with which any of the students seemed to disagree. “You tell it like it is, and that’s what we young people want,” Joe Garris, the president of the College Democrats at Eastern Michigan University, told El-Sayed. “We don’t want to hear corporate answers anymore. We just want the straight truth.”

One of the few factors that could impede a Democratic wave in this year’s midterms is the party’s own unpopularity; polling suggests that plenty of voters outside college campuses might also nod along to El-Sayed’s denunciation of Democrats. Antipathy toward the party is fueling the rise of populist insurgents such as El-Sayed, who castigate Democratic leaders like Chuck Schumer with nearly as much fervor as they criticize President Trump.

Nowhere are the lines dividing Democrats more clearly drawn than in Michigan, a state that the party must win to have any real chance of retaking the Senate majority in November. Democrats have to flip four Republican-held seats without losing any of their own, and Michigan is the Democratic-held seat at the most risk of going red. (The incumbent, Senator Gary Peters, is retiring.) El-Sayed is battling Representative Haley Stevens in the August 4 primary, and the winner will face former Representative Mike Rogers, a formidable GOP candidate who came within roughly 20,000 votes of capturing Michigan’s other Senate seat two years ago.

A 41-year-old doctor, Rhodes Scholar, and former county health director, El-Sayed is running as an unabashed critic of Israel and of corruption in both parties. He begins rallies with a call-and-response incantation of his campaign slogan: “Money out of … politics! Money in your … pocket! Medicare for … all!” I sat down with him after his event in Ann Arbor and asked him to specify the parts of the Democratic brand that he believes are “bullshit.” He began with some of his campaign themes and described Democrats as hypocrites in thrall to corporate donors. “You say you’re for human rights and can’t name a genocide a genocide,” El-Sayed said. “You say you want to give people health care but take money from the people who are keeping us from having health care. You say you want to stand up to Big Tech and take money from Big Tech.”

“What are we actually for?” El-Sayed continued. “Are we just the permanent opposition party where ‘We’re not them’?” Then, referring to Republicans, he added, “At least they’re honest about who they are, for the most part.”

This message should be familiar to those who have watched progressives and democratic socialists topple establishment favorites in New York City, Colorado, and Maine over the past several months. But Michigan is a state that Trump carried in 2024, and many Democrats fear that nominating a candidate who calls for abolishing ICE and establishing a single-payer health-care system is too big a risk in such a crucial year for the party. El-Sayed is also seeking to be the first Muslim senator at a time when some Republicans are fomenting unrestrained Islamophobia. A poll released on Tuesday found Stevens running even with Rogers while El-Sayed trailed him by 10 points in a hypothetical general-election matchup.

El-Sayed’s confidence can border on cockiness—some Democrats see his strident attacks on Stevens as demeaning or even sexist—and he has leaned into the electability debate. Ahead of the 2028 presidential race, he and his progressive supporters view the Michigan contest as an opportunity to extinguish what they see as stale Democratic beliefs about the types of candidates that can win in swing states. “This is just a different time, and this time calls for different kinds of candidates,” El-Sayed told me. “I hope that coming out of this election, the question of electability is no longer asked and interpreted in the same way.”

He turns the argument back against Stevens, who has trailed in many polls despite benefiting from more than $60 million worth of ads bolstering her candidacy and attacking El-Sayed (about half of it coming from AIPAC). “If you can’t get elected without $60 million being spent for you, you can’t get elected. I’m so sorry,” El-Sayed said.

The progressives’ chief data point is Trump’s 2024 win, which they interpret not as an endorsement of the president’s vision but as a rejection of Democrats and the political system more broadly. “You know what people hate more than Trump? Politics as usual. They hate politics as usual so much that they were willing to take Trump, warts and all,” El-Sayed told me. “So what happens if you offer them something that’s not politics as usual and isn’t a megalomaniacal billionaire narcissist? Well, let’s see.”

Stevens is in many ways the quintessential swing-state Democratic Senate candidate. A former Obama-administration official who worked on the auto-rescue task force during his first term, she won a Republican-held House seat in the Detroit suburbs in 2018. Her trajectory is broadly similar to that of Elissa Slotkin, a former CIA analyst and Pentagon official who was elected to Congress in 2018 and then narrowly defeated Rogers in the 2024 Senate race.

In the House, Stevens has distinguished herself mostly through her loyalty to the Democratic leadership. She provided a reliable vote for President Biden’s major initiatives and enthusiastically backed his 2024 reelection bid even after many in the party were calling for him to drop out.

Stevens’s campaign for Senate is well funded but also exceedingly conventional. She emphasizes her Michigan roots, her ties to Obama, and what she’s done for the state in Congress. Taking aim at El-Sayed’s personal wealth, Stevens likes to point out that she’s the only Senate candidate who’s not a millionaire, and she criticized him repeatedly for not releasing a complete financial disclosure. (He released it just before their final debate this week; it showed he’s worth as much as $2.1 million and earned income from a rental property his wife owns in Dubai.)

Still, Stevens has not been able to generate the same amount of energy as El-Sayed, nor does she seem desperate to try. Over my three days in Michigan, he held at least eight events across the southern part of the state; she publicized just two, and her campaign declined to make her available for an interview. (It has helped El-Sayed that, unlike Stevens, he does not have to juggle campaigning with an important day job: “I’m Abdul. I’m 41, I’m unemployed, and I spend my time asking people for money,” El-Sayed joked to the students in Ann Arbor as he introduced himself.)

Polling in the race has been murky. Surveys conducted by groups aligned with El-Sayed’s campaign have shown him up by double digits, as did an independent poll released today. But a Detroit News poll earlier this month found Stevens leading by nearly seven points. Supporters of Stevens had hoped the early-July exit of a third major candidate, State Senator Mallory McMorrow, would help her consolidate support. But one of McMorrow’s most prominent backers, Senator Elizabeth Warren, campaigned with El-Sayed over the weekend.

With El-Sayed holding strong, Michigan’s most prominent Democrats have rallied behind Stevens in a bid to deny him the nomination. This spring, former Senator Debbie Stabenow endorsed Stevens. Then, soon after McMorrow dropped out, Stevens gained the endorsement of the retiring Peters, a former chair of the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee who had earlier indicated he would stay out of the primary to succeed him.

By far the biggest get for Stevens, however, was Governor Gretchen Whitmer, the state’s most popular Democrat, who announced her endorsement last Friday, a day before the start of early voting. Whitmer appeared that afternoon alongside Stevens and another key establishment backer, Representative James Clyburn, at a union rally in Detroit, where Stevens is counting on a big turnout among the city’s Black community. Without mentioning El-Sayed’s name, Whitmer strongly suggested that Stevens would be the stronger general-election candidate. “Michiganders understand better than anyone that our vote has a consequence for every American,” Whitmer said. “We also know that winning Michigan is as tough as it gets. But as someone who won statewide twice, I know Haley is tougher than the environment we’ve got to win in.”

The primary race has turned nasty in its closing weeks, a concern for Democrats hoping that the party can unify to defeat Rogers, who has coasted to the GOP nomination without significant opposition. Stevens’s supporters erupted after El-Sayed implied to Semafor that she could not “string together two coherent sentences.” Soon after, he urged his supporters to stop making fun of Stevens “for things that have nothing to do with her policies or politics.” The flap may have helped Stevens find her voice—literally in a certain sense. She has turned her thick Michigan accent into a calling card of authenticity. “Some of you have seen, there’s those cool kids on the internet. They’re doing the cool-kid chatter. Some of them might be the coastal elites making fun of how I talk,” she told the crowd in Detroit, which laughed loudly. “They’re thinking they’re better than us. And what they don’t know is I am your voice, and their jokes and their criticism only makes me fight harder.”

As I talked with voters in Detroit and its suburbs, it seemed apparent that after such a bitter primary, either candidate could struggle to assemble the coalition they would need to win in November. Younger voters filled El-Sayed’s events, and several of them told me they weren’t sure they would support Stevens if she were the nominee. The image painted by El-Sayed of Stevens as a politician beholden to big-money interests had taken hold. “She would depress the youth vote,” Scott Siegal, a 24-year-old student, told me at an El-Sayed rally in Ferndale.
