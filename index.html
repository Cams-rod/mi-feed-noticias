Since becoming Health and Human Services secretary, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has repeatedly denied that he opposes vaccines and has toned down some of his more extreme rhetoric on the subject. But in a keynote speech today at a conference for Children’s Health Defense, the anti-vaccine organization he founded, the old Kennedy was back. He complained about safety studies, floated disproven theories about a link between vaccines and autism, and all but declared himself an anti-vaxxer.  “You have a strong and steadfast friend at the White House,” he told the crowd.

His choice to take the stage at the conference signaled a shift. Since becoming HHS secretary, Kennedy had distanced himself from Children’s Health Defense as he worked to convince the Senate that he held mainstream-enough views on vaccines. Children’s Health Defense, meanwhile, attempts to convince parents that vaccines are a threat to their children’s well-being. In just the past few days, CHD has used its X account to warn parents against trusting babies with health-care providers who might give them vaccines, claimed that childhood vaccines kill more children than do the diseases they prevent, and alleged that COVID vaccines were designed to cause infertility. The speakers at its conference were a who’s who of the anti-vaccine movement.

Nearly everything the organization promotes runs counter to guidance from the CDC, an agency that Kennedy leads as head of the Department of Health and Human Services. But much of Kennedy’s speech was consistent with CHD’s brand of scaremongering. He suggested that vaccines contribute to chronic disease and disorders such as autism. He bemoaned the fact that more vaccines are recommended for American children now than when he was a child. And he falsely claimed that nothing is known about their safety. “We have no idea what the risk profile is for these products,” he told the crowd.

In one of the more bizarre moments of his address, Kennedy read from the manufacturer’s list of possible adverse events for the vaccine against hepatitis B, a disease that can lead to liver failure. Among those he mentioned was anaphylaxis, an allergic reaction that occurs roughly once for every 1 million doses administered. He ignored the benefits of the vaccine, which has drastically reduced infections and is projected to have prevented millions of deaths. Instead, he implied that the vaccine was merely part of an industry “gold rush.”

The speakers surrounding Kennedy are publicly hostile to vaccination. He was introduced by Mary Holland, the CEO of Children’s Health Defense, who took over the organization after Kennedy’s departure and praised him for taking on “the corrupt medical pharmaceutical complex.” Mark Gorton, the president of a pro-Kennedy think tank called the MAHA Institute, had earlier told the crowd that vaccines are a “public-health nightmare” and that eliminating them would make the country healthier. And then there was Andrew Wakefield, known for his discredited 1998 Lancet paper that alleged a connection between autism and the MMR vaccine. Anti-vaccine activists have spent decades citing Wakefield’s faulty research in an effort to fuel fear of immunizations.

Now is a terrible time for Kennedy to return to public anti-vaccine advocacy. Pennsylvania is in the midst of a deadly measles outbreak. The official count in the state is 731 cases since April and 117 in just the past week; the real number of infections is almost certainly much higher. Four people, two of them infants, have died since mid-August. Much of the suffering has been concentrated among the state’s Amish community, where immunization rates are low, about 25 percent in Lancaster County. Before becoming HHS secretary, Kennedy praised the Amish for declining to get vaccinated, telling a crowd at an Amish country fair in 2021 that, when it comes to vaccines, “it’s so important for us to think more like the Amish think.”

Rather than speaking publicly about how low vaccination rates are the reason that measles is sickening and killing Americans, Kennedy has spent the past month squabbling with Pennsylvania’s governor, Josh Shapiro, over how to define measles-associated deaths. Under Kennedy’s leadership, the CDC has so far declined to list any deaths from Pennsylvania’s ongoing outbreak; this afternoon, while he was onstage, HHS’s official X account called the state government’s demands that the CDC do so “political blackmail.” In his speech at the conference, Kennedy’s only mention of measles was part of a complaint that deaths from the disease “get headlines” while the alleged dangers of vaccines are ignored. (An HHS spokesperson didn’t respond to a question about why Kennedy was appearing at an anti-vaccine conference during a measles outbreak.)

Kennedy reassured the crowd that research into vaccine safety was going on behind the scenes at HHS. “As I said, you have friends in the White House,” he said, “and we are doing this one day at a time.” He claimed that HHS had failed to study vaccine risks and told the audience that he was remedying that. He mentioned making use of the Vaccine Safety Datalink, a collection of Americans’ electronic health records. That database has long been a topic of fascination for Kennedy and other anti-vaccine activists who believe that it might contain hidden evidence that vaccines are harmful.

Kennedy built his reputation on being the country’s most prominent anti-vaccine activist. As HHS secretary, he has often emphasized less controversial issues, such as ultraprocessed foods and artificial dyes. Today, he took to the stage with a message for his longtime allies: He is still one of them.

In a conversation at The Atlantic Festival with The Atlantic’s editor in chief, Jeffrey Goldberg, former Secretary of Transportation Pete Buttigieg warned fellow members of the Democratic Party to resist the urge to revert to “the previous status quo” if they regain power in the midterms.

It would be a mistake, Buttigieg argued, for Democrats to try to reverse “the damage” of the past two years of the Donald Trump presidency by trying to restore the country to how it looked in 2022 or “whenever we think that America was great.” He said that Democrats instead need to build for the idea that “the next quarter century between here and 2050, which is kind of a make-or-break quarter century for the whole American project, is subject to very different conditions and problems than the ones that we inherited.”

Buttigieg—whom many analysts see as a potential 2028 presidential contender—offered examples of what that vision might look like. He called for banning gerrymandering and passing a constitutional amendment to address the deluge of money in politics. “This idea that it is somehow beyond our power to use what is probably the single most elegant feature of the Constitution, which is its capacity to be amended—right?—is a complete failure of ambition and imagination,” Buttigieg said.

When asked by Goldberg about the Democratic Socialists of America, Buttigieg responded that he thinks “there’s a temptation to imagine that being bold is the same thing as being kind of maximally ideological.” Sometimes it is the case, Buttigieg said, that the leftmost idea is the boldest. But other, more ideologically moderate projects may require “radical reform to our institutions,” he noted.

“I know the commentariat wants to get it all fixed on some left-to-center-to-right spectrum,” Buttigieg continued. “To me, what’s more interesting is: Do we have the right package of base hits and big swings, and are we going after both of them, at the same time, all the time?”

If Democrats win control of the House in November, they will face a dilemma: Should they attempt a record third impeachment of Donald Trump?

The president and his allies have warned that this would be a Democratic majority’s first move. “I’m going to be impeached. They’re going to impeach me,” Trump said last month at a rally in South Carolina. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries has been noncommittal. “We haven’t ruled anything in, and we haven’t ruled anything out in terms of impeachment,” the would-be speaker told ABC’s This Week on Sunday. But Representative Jamie Raskin, the lead manager of Trump’s second impeachment, seemed more open. Earlier this month, on Meet the Press, he said that Trump had “undoubtedly” committed impeachable offenses. “We understand it’s not a panacea. It is a tool in the toolbox,” he said. “We will not hesitate to use it if we need to use it.”

Realistically, House Democrats may have a hard time resisting impeachment. Their voters are in a punchy mood. A new CBS News / YouGov poll found that opposition to Trump is the top motivation for Democratic voters in the midterms, and the political consultant Sarah Longwell recently wrote in The Atlantic that the most important priority for Democratic voters is not left-versus-right ideology but a willingness to fight Trump tooth and nail. Jeffries has already faced criticism for being too passive a leader, as well as for meeting with Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner, and he may struggle to ignore a groundswell of support for impeachment.

As a constitutional matter, the case for impeaching the president is solid. Trump has usurped congressional powers, started an unauthorized war or two, defied courts, and used his position for outrageous personal and family enrichment. In 2019, my colleague Yoni Appelbaum made the intellectual case for impeachment as a useful democratic process.

The political case for impeaching Trump is shakier. Regardless of the results in November, Democrats are unlikely to get enough votes in the Senate to convict and remove him, meaning that a third impeachment would hit the same dead end as the first two. An impeachment can still be effective as a way of damaging a president, but Trump is already deeply unpopular and will never face voters again, so the upside is limited, and an impeachment might give him a way to rally his remaining base around him—which would only strengthen his power and resolve.

But what if House Democrats impeached Trump’s Cabinet members instead? The power to impeach secretaries and other executive-branch officers is explicit in the Constitution but perhaps not well known, because it’s been used only twice in history. One reason such proceedings are rare is that a president usually fires a Cabinet member or forces them to resign before they can be impeached, but Trump has shown serious reluctance to fire aides because of public pressure. The congressional scholar Norm Ornstein has argued that if Trump won’t rein them in, Democrats should. On Tuesday, outgoing Representative Thomas Massie, a Republican, brought new attention to the possibility by moving to impeach Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth. GOP leadership promptly shut down all voting until after the election.

In 1876, Secretary of War William Belknap was impeached for corruption, but he resigned the same day and the Senate subsequently acquitted him; a number of senators doubted they had jurisdiction post-resignation. (This historical example resurfaced during Trump’s second impeachment trial, which occurred after he’d left office.) In 2024, House Republicans impeached Secretary of Homeland Security Alejandro Mayorkas on vague grounds, but the Senate voted to dismiss the charges as unconstitutional without a trial.

A Democratic House could plausibly launch impeachment inquiries into several administration officials, many of which would dovetail with investigations that representatives say they already have planned. In some cases, Democratic representatives have already filed articles of impeachment against Cabinet members, including Hegseth, Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr., and Secretary of Education Linda McMahon, but those have gone nowhere in the GOP-led chamber. A new Democratic majority could change that.

The conduct of a few particular individuals makes them likely targets for impeachment efforts. Kennedy has done serious damage to American public health, undermining vaccinations and weakening the CDC. At least one Republican senator has accused him of lying about vaccination rules during his confirmation hearings, when he also falsely characterized a trip he took to Samoa. Hegseth has waged an unauthorized war against Iran, for which he seems to have no realistic game plan; mishandled classified information; possibly violated the Hatch Act by appearing at a fundraiser for a political candidate; and left the armed forces bereft of leadership by firing a slew of qualified generals and admirals.

Attorney General Todd Blanche has been instrumental in turning the Justice Department into a partisan tool for persecuting Trump’s political adversaries, and a federal judge rebuked him for his role in Trump’s attempt to set up a $1.8 billion slush fund for political allies. Multiple sources shared with my colleague Sarah Fitzpatrick serious worries about FBI Director Kash Patel’s leadership, including claims of emotional volatility, heavy drinking, and absence from the job. (Patel denied the allegations and sued Fitzpatrick and The Atlantic for defamation, demanding $250 million.) Russell Vought, the director of the Office of Management and Budget, has led a concerted effort to take congressional powers for the White House.

As a policy matter, impeachment of some of these officers could be more effective than ordinary investigations would be in halting the egregious actions taken by their offices and departments. It might also have a couple political benefits over impeaching Trump. First, such an effort could escape the Trump vortex: By moving the focus to officials who don’t command the same personal loyalty as Trump, impeachment might allow more emphasis on actual administration actions and policy, instead of on presidential personality. Second, conviction might still be a remote possibility, but GOP senators who are fed up with the White House might be more willing to vote to convict officers than they would be to risk taking on Trump himself.

The real question is whether controversial Cabinet secretaries will stick around to see whether Democrats try to target them. A midterm election, especially one that goes poorly for the president’s party, is typically a moment for some secretaries to exit. But Hegseth seems entrenched, continuing to push out rivals; Blanche just won confirmation; and Kennedy and Vought are ideologues pursuing treasured agendas. If neither they nor Trump are willing to change course, then a Congress that has done little for the past two years will have a chance to do something novel: act.

Few reporters understand as much about Donald Trump’s thinking, motives, and behavior as Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan. Haberman has been covering the president closely for more than a decade at The New York Times; she started with his first campaign in 2015, and her record of scoops quickly brought her into the public eye. Swan was, for many years, her chief professional rival: He worked for Axios, where he landed a controversial 2018 one-on-one interview with Trump. In late 2022, the Times announced that it was hiring Swan, turning the rivals into colleagues.

This past June, they published Regime Change: Inside the Imperial Presidency of Donald Trump. The book, an instant best seller, advances a new understanding of the first year of Trump’s second term, relaying in minute-by-minute detail how, since retaking office, he has pursued his personal goals with few limits on his power. The Atlantic staff writer Tim Alberta sat down with both authors at The Atlantic Festival, where he asked them about the 2028 election and the future of the presidency and the Republican Party.

Tim Alberta: Maggie, this country has been through a lot, and I’m wondering if you could help us understand how a “regime change” of this sort came to be.

Maggie Haberman: Essentially, we have had a series of national traumas since 9/11: two forever wars, a fiscal crisis, immense populist anger that started rising in 2010. To me, that was the year that set the tone. It was also the year that the proto-Trump, Carl Paladino, was the Republican nominee for governor in New York State. One of the issues that he ran on was the planned mosque to be built near the World Trade Center; it became known incorrectly as the “Ground Zero Mosque,” and it became a clear avatar of a change in this country after the era of George W. Bush. So you get to 2014, when immigration and particularly border crossings were on the rise under President Obama. Suddenly, this became a massive issue within the Republican Party. You had this mix of anger over the wars, anger over the massive accumulated debt under President Bush, then Obamacare, anger about immigration, and a shifting job market. The time was ripe for a candidate who presented himself as a fighter—and who was already known to most of the Republican electorate, not just as a celebrity on The Apprentice but as a cultural figure.

Alberta: 2016, Trump 1.0, is its own story. So much of what this book is about is how the end of that first term, and then his time in exile after losing in 2020, shapes this dramatic, radical, wholesale change to government and to executive power.

The only other asterisk is that if ever there was an election cycle that was ripe for a Ross Perot type, it will be 2028. There is so much anger at the system. Part of why Trump is struggling right now in polls is that he broke several campaign promises, and voters can feel that. They can feel $4.50 gas. People want to feel like somebody is actually helping them.
