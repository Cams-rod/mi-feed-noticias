Nine days after President Trump took office, in 2017, a headline in Time made a bold claim: “Republicans Begin to Break With President Trump.” The break was over immigration; the president had announced a blanket ban on immigrants from Muslim-majority countries, and it was a step too far for lawmakers. Republicans including Senator Mitch McConnell and Charles and David Koch spoke out.

The perspective afforded by time has shown that such criticisms from Republicans toward the president do not last long, and oftentimes, they do not affect Trump’s stranglehold of his party. But as some Republicans running for office break from the president on issues such as Iran and immigration, they’re hoping that voters believe that this time, they’re serious.

And with less than 40 days until Election Day, the changing electoral maps and shifting court rulings are causing confusion among some voters. On Radio Atlantic this week, my colleagues Toluse Olorunnipa and Madeleine Carlisle join me to unpack the mess of this midterm election.

The following is a transcript of the episode:

Adam Harris: There are, of course, valid reasons to redraw district maps. But in an election year, could this not also be seen as a form of cheating?

Madeleine Carlisle: The people I was talking to in Missouri really did feel like their vote was being diluted. It added to the sense that, Why should I cast my ballot? It doesn’t matter; it’s all rigged anyway.

I don’t think it’s going away. And the validity of it as a political tool is something that is certainly being endorsed at this moment by both parties.

Harris: This is Radio Atlantic. I’m Adam Harris, and today, we need to think about the mess of the midterms. With just over a month until election day, one might hope for a bit more certainty. Gerrymandering wars over the last two years have reshaped congressional maps across the country. Election deniers, some who still falsely contend that the 2020 election was stolen, are overseeing elections. And every other day, it seems court decisions are changing how people will vote this year. Bottom line, it’s all very confusing and concerning. And one is forced to wonder whether it’s also voter suppression.

My colleagues Toluse Olorunnipa and Madeleine Carlisle have been covering the shifting political and legal environment around the midterm elections, and they join me now to discuss. Let’s get to questions.

Tolu, Maddie, thank you so much for joining me today.

Carlisle: Hi, thanks for having us.

Toluse Olorunnipa: Hey, thanks for having us.

Harris: I want to start by asking you both about something you’ve been reporting on, which is how more Republicans running for office are backpedaling away from President Trump. Tolu, midterms can often be a referendum around a president, the sitting president and their policies. So is this pre-election politics as usual, or is there something else going on? What does your reporting show?

Olorunnipa: It’s a little bit of both. There is the normal backing away from an unpopular president that you tend to see in a midterm election. Midterm elections don’t usually go very well for the party in power, and it’s usually because the president is not as popular as he was when he was first elected. That’s normal.

But what is not normal is how much these Republicans are backing away from the president and the way that they’re doing it, and the fact that President Trump is unlike any previous president in that he does not allow people to back away from him, usually, without paying a price. He’s kept his party in line over the last several years by essentially going against anyone who speaks out against him—primarying them, showing them that he’s willing to tank their political careers.

And so for that reason, we haven’t seen a lot of people break from the president. But now, his poll numbers are so low; he’s so unpopular—the most unpopular that he’s ever been over the course of his two different terms—and people are realizing that things are not getting better. They are not going to be successful if they stay with him. And so a number of these Republicans are realizing that. They’re seeing the polling, and they’re starting to make some clear breaks with him, over the war in Iran, over the affordability crisis, over things like AI and data centers.

And the president has taken some unpopular positions on those issues, and so they are reading the room, they’re looking at their constituencies, they’re realizing that their own political careers could end if they stay with the president. And so we’re seeing this jailbreak of a number of Republicans speak out against the president, take different positions than he has taken. We’re seeing it in a way that we haven’t seen it in the past when it comes to President Trump.

Those who don’t fully support him, he often doesn’t take too kindly to that. Why is his response more muted this time around?

But I think, given that there are certain issues that are so incredibly unpopular—like the war in Iran, like the price of diesel—that he, as a smart political actor, understands that these candidates need to make a little daylight, and he’s allowing that. But there have been cycles in the past where these candidates needed to do similar things and he was incredibly unforgiving.

So, maybe it’s that he’s this deep into his political career now; he understands how things work. It could be that he’s got these new folks he’s working with this time, who weren’t with him back in 2018 when he was last in this position. But he is allowing folks to back away in a way that he has not before. And, from conversations I’ve had, that makes candidates more comfortable to do so: They won’t be targeted as they were in 2018.

I mean, I’m not one to predict the president’s actions. Maybe he will target them, but as of right now, he’s been very muted in his response, and that could be giving people a bit more confidence to do what they think they need to do to win over the critical independent voter who will decide this midterm cycle.

Harris: It makes me think: It’s not that these are just arbitrary issues. This is a war that is affecting people. It is driving up the price of diesel; it’s driving up the price of gas. These are concerns that voters have. If he sees his party backing away from these unpopular issues, why isn’t the president himself reading the room and backing away from some of these issues as well? Wouldn’t that help the party more than, say, this individual person backing away?

Olorunnipa: It probably would help the party, but President Trump has run his last election and he’s thinking much less about the fate of his party, or even the popularity of his policies, in part because he’s not going to have to go before voters again, and he’s thinking of his presidency in this broad, legacy-defining way that sometimes second-term presidents think about their time in office. And so he is, in his mind, taking positions that are maybe politically unpopular, but will be better for the overall trajectory of the country and better for his legacy.

He thinks he’s solving the long-running issue of relations with Iran and the potential for Iran getting a nuclear weapon. There’s a lot of debate over whether going to war was effective in preventing that. But, in his mind, and how he’s talking to his advisers and how they’re talking to him, that is what he’s doing here.

And so he’s said it publicly that he doesn’t care about the midterm elections. He doesn’t care about the potential impact of the price of gas on people’s vote if he’s able to prevent Iran from getting a nuclear weapon. And he’s done the same thing in defending other unpopular positions. When it comes to data centers—which are very unpopular in a number of the states where there are vulnerable Republican Senate candidates—the president has said that people and communities that don’t want these data centers are at risk of being backward and poor. Instead of saying, I feel your pain, or having a more nuanced argument, he basically has said that anyone who disagrees with him is really not worthy of being part of his movement.

So it’s pretty clear that the president feels that he doesn’t have to bow to the political wind of the moment. He is focusing on his legacy and thinking that he’s doing the right thing for the long term, and he’s willing to pay a short-term price.

Harris: How are voters feeling about this? The vast majority of Republicans have supported Trump through thick and thin over the last two years, thinking even back to the first term. I wonder if voters are sort of seeing this as cynical, where Republicans are starting to try to back away. Will it work, even if they do try to back away from the president’s policies?

Carlisle: I think that’s a great question. Certainly, Democrats who are running against the Republicans that are making this daylight now between themselves and the White House are doing exactly what you’re saying. And they’re pointing out: You’ve been with the president this whole time. Now you’re stepping away; don’t buy this.

However, there are some voters—all politics is local, right? And it’s all about your candidate. And if you have a candidate—for example, Mike Rogers in Michigan—calling for the war in Iran to end, calling for relations with Canada to improve, that can go very far with Michigan voters. Specifically that state, given their relationship with Canada, given how much the state is dependent on diesel. And so I believe that will work with voters in a way, particularly voters who maybe don’t want to vote for a progressive Democrat who’s running in that Senate race as his Democratic opponent. And so, I do see this helping certain Republican candidates, but not always. And I do think the price of diesel, the relationship with other countries, tariffs—these are things that people, particularly when we’re talking the Midwest, Michigan, Wisconsin, those places where those policy issues really do impact people. And I think making that daylight will help those Republican candidates advance.

Harris: Tolu, last week, the Supreme Court ruled that the Trump administration can use an expensive voter-eligibility database that contains sensitive voter information ahead of this election. What are the implications of this decision?

Olorunnipa: That remains to be seen. It seems like it may, at least in the short term, have a limited impact, in part because we are in this ninety-day quiet period, where you’re not supposed to kick people off the voter rolls en masse, because it’s seen as disruptive to do that so close to an election. So a number of the election officials that we’ve heard from since this ruling came out said that there’s nothing that they’re gonna be able to do differently, because it’s too late to start purging people from the voter rolls.

But if this ruling stands, if it’s upheld over the long term, you could see a much more aggressive attempt, especially by red states, to kick people off the voter rolls using this federal database. This database has been known to have flaws. It has not typically been used for voter issues. It’s been used for other things, like benefits and federal support that people get. And they’ve tried to change it and make it something that can be used to determine whether somebody is eligible to vote or not.

Now, whether or not some states decide to do it on a case-by-case basis—use this database to start plucking people off the voter rolls in the last weeks before the election—that remains to be seen. And it also gives the federal government another tool if they want to challenge the legitimacy of the election. They can say, We have this database; it’s been rubber stamped by the Supreme Court, and we believe that a number of voters from X state or Y state are not eligible. And they could use that to try to challenge the results of the election after.

So I think we haven’t heard the last of what is going to be the implication of this ruling. But for the first take, we’ve heard from local officials that they’re not gonna do much with it.

Harris: And even just to back up a little bit, because this isn’t just an isolated thing. It’s a part of the administration’s broader efforts to reshape the way that we are voting, including the president’s attempts to pass the Save America Act and voter-ID changes that we’ve seen over the last several years.

In July, Trump made a primetime address to the nation alleging that China had interfered with the 2020 election. Is there any truth, Tolu, to that claim, that allegation about China? And is election interference something that we should be worried about here, in the 2026 midterms?

Olorunnipa: Well you would not know it based on the meeting that President Trump had with President Xi of China just a few days ago. He rolled out the red carpet. He met him and greeted him as he got off of the plane as he arrived in the United States. He showed him a wonderful welcome—not the kind of welcome you would expect from someone who had meddled in your elections just a few years ago.

But that is the most significant form of evidence that this federal government has been able to find in their hunt for evidence that the 2020 election was stolen. And when we look more deeply into what the president was saying back then, we found that even that evidence was pretty thin. China basically got access to state databases that anyone can access. A lot of these states have made public or make it available, the voter roll data that different political campaigns and political operatives use to target voters or to mail mailers to voters. And China essentially got access to a number of those things. There’s no evidence that they use that to manipulate people’s vote, or manipulate the voter rolls in any way. They went in and they looked. And President Trump himself has downplayed China’s cyber actions in the past, saying, They do things to us; we do things to them. But because he has been so fixated on the voter issues, the China story was one that he could take a hold of and try to present and package as evidence that he’s been looking for that the 2020 election was compromised.

But there’s no real evidence that China or any other actor did anything that precipitated President Trump’s loss. He lost because he got fewer votes. He lost because he did not get the requisite number of electoral votes. And nothing has changed that, even though he’s used the full force of the federal government to try to investigate voter fraud from six years ago. And what they were able to come up with was this China story. But even that story is a little thin. And based on the president’s meeting with President Xi, even he doesn’t really believe that there was a giant fraud committed on the American people when it comes to the 2020 election.

Harris: When we come back, we talk about court decisions and redistricting efforts could complicate the election.

Harris: Maddie, I was just thinking, when your route changes when you’re going somewhere—you’re in the car, and you see the signs, and it’s like, This road is closed, and your GPS is trying to take you a different direction. Sometimes it’s like, I have no idea where I’m going, and things can get a little bit more confusing. I was thinking about that in relation to the amount of changes that we’ve seen in our elections over the last couple of years. From Trump’s efforts to restrict mail-in ballots to redistricting maps being tangled up in courts, many Americans are sort of losing faith in elections because it can be so confusing, such a web.

A July Gallup poll found that two in three Americans are worried about election interference in this upcoming election. How do these new maps affect Congress and the 2026 election?

Carlisle: So I think the redistricting arms race, as some people have called it, is really the theme of this midterm-election cycle because it’s in line with what you pointed out, Adam—how much the rules of our elections have been changing so close up to election day. This is incredibly confusing for voters. And one of those main things is these maps have been redrawn. This unusual mid-cycle redistricting.

So, for context, redistricting is normally done at the start of the decade after the U.S. Census. But last year, President Trump called upon Texas to redraw their maps to better benefit Republicans going to this cycle. Texas responded, and then we were in this downward spiral, in which blue states and red states all have been redrawing their maps to try to get some sort of partisan advantage for their candidates.

It’s been incredibly confusing for voters. Particularly, I covered what has happened in Missouri, which is that the map has been changing up, back and forth, back and forth, up until truly last week. Only last week do we now know which map was going to be used for the election cycle, which is so incredibly difficult for Missourians, for election officials, for anyone who’s just trying to get their bearings on, Who’s on my ballot? Who are the candidates I’m weighing in on? Basic things like that, that we should know.

Harris: And had the ballots already started going out in Missouri?

Carlisle: Yes, yes, absolutely. So it is incredibly complicated and I don’t wanna bog you down in the court battles that were both federal and state, but it only was settled last week in the U.S. Supreme Court, which map it was going to be. And at that point, I was talking to election officials in Missouri, and they had to start mailing on September, I believe it was 19th, to overseas voters, those ballots. So truly making things incredibly difficult for the people who run our elections, for the people who want to weigh in and get involved with our elections.

How it will impact the midterms, I think it’s a really interesting question because the Republicans on paper have a clear advantage. They were able to redraw far more districts. And going into this project last year, they were aiming to get as much as 13 seats out of this process.

However, what has happened is a lot of those districts that they redrew have been surprisingly competitive, or much more competitive than you might think, given that they were carved up specifically to flip that seat and make it a Republican seat. So Don Davis, in North Carolina’s first district, is holding on, even though North Carolina redid its map just to get him out. There are candidates in Florida—Kathy Castor in Florida’s 14th, Jared Moskowitz—these Democrats who are doing much better than anyone anticipated.

So it’s likely that Republicans will be able to flip four, five seats, or six, even. But that’s less than half of what they were aiming, when they started this process. Because, I think, for several things: The headwinds for Democrats are just so strong this cycle, given what we’ve been discussing. And also from voters I’ve been talking to; voters are pissed about this redistricting.

In North Carolina, for example—I was talking to organizers on the ground there. They’ve seen a surge in people registering to vote because they’re so—and at least, this is what the people I was talking to were saying: Voters are angry that these maps were redrawn. And so, in a way, it could be a backlash. Also in red states or blue states that redrew their maps to get Republicans out, like in California, we’ll see how that impacts the race. Are those voters also frustrated?

And so, I will say, the broader theme of confusion—and to your point: I’m driving on the road; I don’t know where I’m going—this mid-decade redistricting, I think, is the perfect example of that, and also exemplifies this trend of this midterm cycle of really last-minute chaos. That the people it’s hurting are the voters themselves and the election administrators who are nonpartisan actors usually, and are just trying to get the ballots out. And I also don’t think it’s gonna stop. I would keep an eye on this theme going into 2028.

Harris: To that point, the president is highly supportive of redistricting, right? And as you mentioned, Maddie, you wouldn’t expect some of these races to be as close as they are, given that they were redrawn for these specific purposes. There are of course valid reasons to redraw district maps. But in an election year, could this not also be seen as a form of cheating, in its own way? Carlisle: I think a lot of voters do feel that way. So on the Missouri example I was talking about, Kansas City has been represented for years and years and years by Democrat Emmanuel Cleaver. And then, under the map that was drawn and passed by the Republican legislature, that city—what was done to it is the term “cracking,” in the study of gerrymandering, the term is called cracking. And so basically, I found a church that I think really exemplifies this process. So it used to be and still is a center of the community in northeast Kansas City, this church. But under the map that was drawn by the Republican legislature, the church building itself would be in one district, the parking lot would be another district, and across the street, at the market that they go to, would be a third district. And so that shows this cracking effect, which is when a historically blue or historically red—whatever sort of area the gerrymandering is targeting—is drawn up into these other areas, and to dilute, in a way, the voting power of those areas. And people in Missouri that I was talking to very much felt that way—that they were being disenfranchised by this.

Kansas City, arguably, is a cohesive community that had a single Democratic representative for many, many years. And then now these folks that I was talking to, who attend this church, were going to be in a district that was shared with rural Missourian farmers, who have very little of the same interests in, and don’t share a lot of the same concerns that one would bring to a lawmaker. And so that’s all to say, the people I was talking to in Missouri really did feel like their vote was being diluted. It added to the sense that, Why should I cast my ballot? It doesn’t matter; it’s all rigged anyway. These are things that I was hearing from people given the chaos over the drawing of maps in Missouri.

I think whether or not it actually is something that is disenfranchising, the Supreme Court has very much proved it in recent Supreme Court decisions. I don’t think it’s going away. And the validity of it as a political tool is something that is certainly being endorsed at this moment by both parties.

But the voters, themselves, I do think are feeling that they are losing their ability to further engage in the electoral process because of this fear that, Where I live, will that just be carved up more? So I don’t have an answer for you, but certainly people feel the way you’re describing.

Harris: I’m gonna ask both of you to open your notebooks. We’re a little under forty days until the election. What are some of the things that you will be thinking about as we get closer to election day? Tolu, I’ll start with you.

Olorunnipa: I’ll be really interested to see how much the bottom falls out of the president’s political standing and of his allies as they approach November. And when I say that, I mean we have heard and seen that some pollsters and some strategists on the Republican side have said that people who won their races by 15 points now should be considered as toss-ups. And that gets you pretty far and pretty deep into people that have shown full support for President Trump, who have felt that they have no concern that once they win their primary, they’re a shoo-in for winning their re-elections.

If we start to see some of those folks start to panic even more, and start to distance themselves from the president even more, I think that’ll be a good sense for how much the bottom is falling out and what that means for not only the next forty-odd days, but as we enter into this new phase of President Trump becoming a lame duck. Having no more elections that he can really influence in from the position of power, other than trying to determine his successor, what does that transition look like? What does he do differently, especially if he loses the House and or the Senate, and his power is diminished? How does that change his presidency? How does that change his outlook on power? How much does that change the way he tries to shape his last two years in office? And I think we’ll start seeing it pretty soon after November.

Harris: Maddie, same question to you.

Carlisle: I am very interested to see how the relationship with the federal government and the states over election administration changes after the November election. I think there has been a lot of concern that the antagonistic relationship the federal government has taken with states when it comes to how they should run their elections, how they should keep their voter rolls. There’s been a lot of concern about how that could change after the election. Is the federal government going to challenge some results? Or is a local candidate going to challenge a result? And is the federal government going to come in and support that candidate? And so I will be watching very closely to see how that relationship between the Justice Department and various states changes, because that’s been something people have been very concerned about.

The federal government has not gotten the changes to election administration it broadly wanted. The Supreme Court struck down the changes to mail-in ballots that the president wanted. To Tolu’s point, the save system that they have been seeking to use—DHS’s save system—to purge voter rolls or check voter rolls, the Supreme Court said in its opinion they can’t do that in this 90-day period. But I’d be very interested to see if maybe that system is invoked if some election results are challenged, like Tolu said. So I think it’s definitely going to be important to watch what happens coming out of those federal offices once the ballots are in, because up until now, everyone’s just been speculating. We have no idea what’s going to happen. And the courts have been weighing in, but that’s been it. And so I’m very interested to see actually how those actors behave—those federal and state actors behave—once we know who won.

Harris: Yeah, absolutely. Well, there is a ton to keep our eyes on and we will be following you all’s reporting as we move closer to election day. Thank you so much for joining and sharing your reporting.

Carlisle: Thanks for having me.

This is an edition of The Atlantic Daily, a newsletter that guides you through the biggest stories of the day, helps you discover new ideas, and recommends the best in culture. Sign up for it here.

Your tax dollars are at work—promoting Donald Trump to the American people.

In the past few days, two new ads appeared on some television channels. One, in black and white, features Trump walking down a hall and staring into the camera. In a voice-over, he says, “This is the final battle with you at my side.” The other features clips of Trump giving a speech at Mount Rushmore. But the most striking words are the ones on the bottom of the screen: Paid for by the U.S. Government.

These are part of a series. Last week, as my colleague Matt Viser wrote, the administration aired an ad that featured images of Trump looking resolute and imposing, with the words AMERICA WILL NEVER BE A COMMUNIST COUNTRY superimposed for most of the spot, which also flashes various empty Trump policy boasts. (Largest tax cuts in history, which is flatly false; reigniting American manufacturing, which is dubious.) The soundtrack, apparently used without permission, is the musician JMSN’s song “Love Me.” To date, taxpayers have spent at least $443,000 on this ad alone, according to the Associated Press.

The White House calls these “public service announcements,” intended only to remind “Americans to love their country and understand what makes it worth defending, at home, at our borders, and abroad,” a White House spokesperson told Viser. Taken at face value, this is the latest example of Trump’s Sun King–esque attempt to conflate himself and the state. This administration has named a new visa program the “Trump Gold Card,” tried to place his name before John F. Kennedy’s on an official memorial, established a 47-day training program at ICE to honor his number in the presidential sequence, printed his portrait in passports, and last week launched something called “Trump TV” under White House auspices. (It’s a glorified streaming-video app.) But the White House’s claim that these are PSAs should not be taken at face value. A much simpler term exists to describe them: propaganda.

Senator Thom Tillis, a North Carolina Republican, pointed out that Trump’s political operation has plenty of cash to spend—almost $416 million as of August 31—and compared him to the strongman whom Hungarians toppled earlier this year: “Using taxpayer dollars, it feels like Viktor Orbán.” Another GOP senator, John Kennedy of Louisiana, said yesterday that no public official “should spend public money on private ads for themselves.” He added: “I think there oughta be a rule—well, there probably is a rule—you can’t spend public money to promote yourself.”

Indeed, there is a rule. The law bars the use of taxpayer money on “publicity or propaganda.” The problem is how one defines that. The administration has cited past PSAs, including an anti-drug ad during the George W. Bush administration and a pro-COVID-vaccine spot from the Biden years, to justify the newest ads. The White House insists that these ads are not promoting Trump, because he is not currently running for election. “The PSAs are clearly not political,” an official told NBC News. “The president is not on the ballot, and the ads don’t have a call to action.”

These pretexts are so skimpy that they would make a fig leaf shrivel in shame. The ads lean heavily on the White House’s big talking points this cycle, such as dark warnings about “communism.” Trump is not on the ballot, but he has repeatedly begged his supporters to treat this election as a referendum on him—writing on Truth Social this morning, “MAGA, pretend I am on the ballot.” Finally, the new ad is just a new version of one that his campaign produced late in the 2024 presidential race, only this time paid for with public money. (Perhaps someone should have edited out the line where Trump promises to “expel the warmongers from our government,” which hasn’t aged so well.)

Yet just because the ads are wildly inappropriate, redolent of personalist dictators, and clearly violate the intent of the law, that doesn’t mean they can easily be stopped. A reasonable viewer will know that the ads are intended to boost Trump and Republicans in the midterms, but because the spots don’t have the “call to action” that qualifies them as campaign advocacy under the law, they may not violate the letter of the law. And even if they did, who would police that? Both houses of Congress are controlled by Republicans, whom the ad is intended to help. The Federal Election Commission doesn’t have a quorum to take action, because Trump has declined to nominate members. And anyway, the Supreme Court has consistently granted the president very wide leeway for any “official” actions.

Kennedy’s there-oughta-be-a-rule response is understandable, but one big lesson of the Trump era is that writing a law to prevent every potential abuse of power is impossible. Attempting to do so would tie Congress up in reams of mind-numbing legislation. Courts might strike some of it down; other parts would be impossible to enforce; and a sufficiently motivated administration would simply find nefarious work-arounds, just as this one has. A functioning democracy cannot be just a matter of statute. It requires good faith and some measure of virtue from its officeholders. No law can substitute for character in leaders.

That makes these transgressions primarily a matter for voters, not legislators or regulators. And in the case of the campaign ads, they can weigh in soon. As Paul Krugman writes, Trump’s personal unpopularity is already a drag on Republican candidates, so emphasizing his role would seem to be politically unwise. You can spend hundreds of thousands of dollars broadcasting a song called “Love me,” but you can’t make voters love you.

A $111 Billion Hollywood Takeover With a Murky Future

He told my colleague Quinta Jurecic that the coalition of 12 attorneys general who’d filed an anti...

Last Thursday, during a panel discussion at The Atlantic Festival, California Attorney General Rob Bonta seemed confident that the biggest antitrust case Hollywood has seen in decades would go his way. He told my colleague Quinta Jurecic that the coalition of 12 attorneys general who’d filed an antitrust lawsuit against the proposed Paramount–Warner Bros. merger were winning their case. The group had persuaded Paramount to not close the deal until next summer, and they’d landed a favorable trial date in March. But, Bonta said, “we are always open to coming to the table.”

That seems to be exactly what happened. Last week, Paramount settled the lawsuit, ending months of negotiations. Though the agreement still has to be approved by a judge, the company has cleared its biggest hurdle to completing its $111 billion takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery. The move saved Paramount plenty of cash—the company had pledged to pay Warner Bros. shareholders $7 million for each day past the end of September that the deal failed to close—while appearing to satisfy many of the requests of Bonta and his fellow attorneys general, including keeping both studios in California.

Still, Bonta, at a press conference last Monday, expressed disapproval. “The settlement is not a vote of support for this merger,” he said. To misquote a classic Warner Bros. film, this seems to be the beginning of an uncomfortable friendship. Below are four reasons—all lingering questions as the merger nears its finish line.

The settlement stipulates that the merged company must release at least 30 films a year for the first two years of its existence, and 32 films a year for the following three years, or pay a financial penalty. Is that number achievable?

Hitting those figures could be difficult for a single, combined studio, especially because the settlement states that each year, at least four of those films must be independent productions and at least 20 of them must get a wide release (the number goes up to 21 after the first two years). In 2025, Paramount released just eight films theatrically, while Warner Bros. released 11. The settlement also orders the new Paramount to spend $300 million more annually than it did last year to make films in the United States, rather than producing them abroad, which puts a lot of pressure on the company to generate hits, but neither Paramount nor Warner Bros. has found reliable success at the box office. (Audiences have been inconsistent in showing up to see superhero movies and legacy sequels lately; just look at the underwhelming returns on Supergirl and Practical Magic 2.) Besides, making movies will only add to the company’s ongoing debt: Paramount borrowed more than $54 billion for its takeover bid, and because Warner Bros. already had its own debt, the combined entity owes roughly $80 billion.

How will the merger affect Hollywood’s already flagging job market?

Poorly, probably. Layoffs seem inevitable, considering, again, the amount of debt Paramount has gone into just to acquire Warner Bros. The settlement demands that Paramount contribute $47.5 million to a “workforce fund” over five years to aid those laid off, but giant corporate mergers have a history of massive layoffs whose costs may exceed that sum. The Disney takeover of 20th Century Fox in 2019 led to an estimated 4,000 to 10,000 slashed jobs. Consolidating Hollywood means fewer studios at which to make film and television projects, which in turn means fewer employment prospects. For now, only those at CBS News, which is owned by Paramount, appear to be relatively safe: In a settlement with the Writers Guild of America, Paramount agreed to prohibit writer layoffs there for five years.

The merger brings CNN and CBS News under one roof, and under the oversight of Paramount CEO David Ellison, a Trump ally. How will that affect coverage?

That’s hard to say. Some of the merger’s detractors had hoped that the antitrust lawsuit would pressure Paramount into selling off CNN as an independent enterprise, but the settlement simply states that the outlet will have an oversight committee installed to ensure its editorial independence. And though that board will consist of five journalists with at least 10 years of experience in the field and a mix of political leanings, the combined company’s board of directors, chaired by Ellison, will pick its members. Whether such an arrangement will help to prevent any corporate overreach is up in the air: To Joshua Benton, the Nieman Lab founder, the board seems like a PR move at best. After all, as Reuters noted, how the board’s decisions and findings will be implemented or enforced remains unclear.

Bonta called this settlement a “strong solution that protects competition and consumers.” Is that going to turn out to be true?

Again, that depends on whom you ask. California Governor Gavin Newsom, the Directors Guild of America, and the International Alliance of Theatrical Stage Employees (an organization representing more than 170,000 artists in the entertainment industry) supported settling the lawsuit to prevent further job loss. The deal at least ensures that Paramount isn’t moving out of California for the next several years, which should sustain competition among Hollywood studios to some extent.

A mix of new investors could also help alleviate the extreme debt Paramount is in. (The studio’s spending could heavily influence what the new company makes for audiences to see in theaters and how much it earns in subscriptions to whatever combination HBO Max–Paramount+ streaming service.) Earlier this month, the Federal Communications Commission approved the infusion of money from foreign interests for this deal, including the sovereign wealth funds of Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Abu Dhabi. Semafor reported last week that Elon Musk, an Ellison and Trump ally, may be asked to invest in Paramount as well. These sources of cash would likely lead to further anxieties about how Paramount will be run, but the company has promised that its foreign investors’ stakes will involve silent, nonvoting shares. For now, the best bet on what will happen next with the merger is that the deal itself will close.

OpenAI Has Gone Rogue

In response to two such breaches described in early August, an AI observer noted, “If you find two ants in your kitch...

Over the past couple of months, a trickle of reports about AI models breaking out of their test environments and running amok on the internet have caused alarm inside Silicon Valley. In response to two such breaches described in early August, an AI observer noted, “If you find two ants in your kitchen, the best estimate of the total number of ants in your kitchen is not two.” This weekend, it became clear there is a full-blown infestation.

Late last week we learned that, against OpenAI’s directives, the company’s models accessed private data in the Australian health ministry; attempted to hack or interfere with multiple U.S.-government websites; leaked private ChatGPT user data to the web; and potentially infiltrated or degraded dozens of other organizations. Then, on Saturday, Axios reported that OpenAI and Anthropic are investigating tens of thousands of instances of models misbehaving—circumventing internal guardrails, hijacking other websites, covertly communicating with one another. Even this might be only the start: The generative-AI industry is in the midst of an escalating crisis that it seems unable, or even unwilling, to get a handle on. And, because we are largely relying on AI companies themselves to report or confirm each incident, telling how far down the rabbit hole we already are is almost impossible.

[Read: Treat AI like a normal crisis]

When AI companies have reported their models going rogue, it has been with great delay and, frequently, under duress. Earlier this month, OpenAI published a blog post boasting about the firm’s commitment to the “value of transparency” and shared six new incidents of troubling actions taken by its AI models—most of which the company had known about since May or even April, but was telling us about only now. Google, confronted with a report that Gemini had hacked three other websites in May, confirmed the events but told The Wall Street Journal that the incidents hadn’t been serious enough to warrant public disclosure. For its part, Anthropic has said it was not reviewing for such misbehaviors until OpenAI started doing so.

These delayed, sporadic disclosures make grasping the scope of the problem difficult. On Friday, OpenAI wrote that “given the scale of the review required, and the need to verify each case, this work will take months to complete.” There are “petabytes of agent activity logs” to analyze, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman added. In other words, it will take OpenAI many months more to understand events that already transpired many months ago; meanwhile, both it and Anthropic have launched new and more capable models, and Anthropic is racing toward a reported $2 trillion public offering.

Perhaps more alarming than the debacles we know about are all of the presumable debacles past, and ongoing, that we aren’t aware of. Even OpenAI doesn’t even seem to have a grip on the July hack of the tech company Hugging Face that started it all. On Friday, independent researchers published findings suggesting that OpenAI agents left traces on the public web of still more nefarious actions, including trying to access Hugging Face’s internal Slack workspace. None of this was included in OpenAI’s own postmortem report. Even when OpenAI is aware of an active breach, its reaction seems lackluster, at best. Eight days ago, yet another OpenAI model gained unauthorized internet access. (This was similar to what happened in the Hugging Face hack, after which OpenAI claimed to be “adding stronger protections around future training.”) And, after noticing this latest problem, it took OpenAI two and a half hours to shut that model down due to what the firm called “operational gaps” and “confusion.”

If these types of hacks were unforeseeable, maybe these companies could be forgiven—but they aren’t. Philosophers and computer scientists have for decades been imagining scenarios in which AI models, in pursuit of a goal, take catastrophic actions. OpenAI and Anthropic have both previously published research more than a year ago suggesting this sort of AI misbehavior is possible. Today’s most advanced AI systems are trained to aggressively pursue their objectives. This persistence makes the bots very effective at analyzing spreadsheets and coding, for example, but also has potentially dangerous, unintended consequences: OpenAI and Anthropic models have hacked other websites in search of answers to hard test questions, created fake personas to try to manipulate humans into doing their bidding, or attempted to upload malware to outside code bases. The AI companies that build these models know this tendency well. The fact that these problems keep recurring is, perhaps at best, plain incompetence. Or worse, it has been the plan all along: The most realistic training environment for an AI model would, after all, be reality.

Employees and executives at these firms speak out endlessly about the inherent risks of what they’re building. OpenAI, Anthropic, and the like have responded in their usual way, with very serious blog posts and essays and speeches to major political bodies about how seriously the world must take the risk of generative AI. On Wednesday, the same day the Australian government shared that it had been hacked by OpenAI bots, Altman addressed the UN Security Council: “We could lose control of the future to AI,” he said. “The risk is that it moves so fast that people can no longer follow what’s happening or intervene when needed.” Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has written a long manifesto about the need to “pace,” or slow down, frontier-AI development, but no true public, collective effort has been made to do so. Anthropic recently said it is partnering with Accenture to perform independent evaluations of its models, although the companies also have a business partnership. OpenAI, which has a content-licensing agreement with The Atlantic, has said it is continuing to investigate and address concerning model behaviors and that, after another security incident last week, it has temporarily paused all training of its most capable models. Neither company responded to a request for comment.

Rather than reining in and taking accountability for their own products, time and again these leaders fearmonger, lecture, and call on the rest of us to save us from themselves. (The Information has reported that OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are working to create their own self-regulatory AI-safety organization.) Donald Trump, meanwhile, has scoffed at the notion of slowing AI progress, and new versions of Claude and ChatGPT keep coming. The AI industry’s tactic, as ever, has been to substitute identifying the problem for actually solving it. But at some point, you cannot blog your way out of the apocalypse.

We may never fully know the scope of the AI-led hacks currently under way. What’s certain, though, is that this is still just the beginning. OpenAI and Anthropic aren’t research “labs,” as they continue to style themselves, tinkering with some new technology behind closed doors. These are mature companies offering services to hundreds of millions of people, not to mention to the world’s biggest businesses and most powerful military, based on a technology they purport to fear and evidently do not fully understand. But make no mistake: These firms have agency. It is not ChatGPT and Claude going rogue, but OpenAI and Anthropic.
