Jensen Huang Isn’t Worried. But You Should Be.

Last week, Anthropic went public. The IPO was heavily oversubscribed, spurring the company to sell more shares at a higher price than it had originally intended. The five-year-old start-up took in a record-breaking $81 billion from investors around the world, giving it a gobsmacking $1.5 trillion valuation and minting an estimated 600 centimillionaires.

Nevertheless, the IPO arrived at a dour moment on Wall Street, and did not exactly brighten the mood. Investors had been selling off equities for weeks, spooked by rising interest rates, the midterm landslide, concerns about AI’s capacity to boost corporate profits, and Nvidia’s ouster of its allegedly honey-trapped CEO. When Nvidia’s board announced that it had unanimously reinstated Jensen Huang yesterday, its share price surged. Then it slouched back down, joining Anthropic stock, the S&P 500, foreign-equity markets, gold, crypto, and Nasdaq in the red.

The AI boom seems to be turning into an AI bust. Maybe we’ll end up thinking of it as an AI bubble. Maybe it will spur an AI recession. The worst possible outcome would be an AI financial crisis—and it is not a far-fetched outcome at all, given the astronomical sums, convoluted deals, and cavernous regulatory lacunae involved.

The question is whether the AI whatever-it-is will play out more like the dot-com bubble of the 1990s or the housing bubble of the aughts. In the former, Nasdaq lost 78 percent of its value and the country entered a brief recession. Still, the banking system remained solvent and the credit markets liquid. In the latter, the housing market faltered, the stock market collapsed, and the entire global financial system seized, precipitating the worst recession since the Great Depression.

[Read: A shameless valley]

The difference wasn’t the amount of money at stake. The collapse of the dot-com bubble wiped out more wealth than the collapse of the housing bubble, as former Fed Chair Ben Bernanke observed in a 2012 speech. The difference was that the dot-com losses were transparent, widely distributed, and easily understood. People got excited about the World Wide Web; they bought stocks at absurd valuations; some of the companies they invested in proved to be duds; they lost money. The losses from the housing bubble, in contrast, were opaque, concentrated, and impenetrable. Where to even start? The erosion of lending standards, the explosive growth in derivatives contracts, the mispricing of mortgage-backed securities, the misguided faith in market self-regulation, the insufficient capital buffers, the regulatory gaps, the Sun Belt speculation, the low interest rates? I suppose it came down to one thing: Investors seemed like they were making a lot of different bets. But they were making one bet, on housing prices. And when that bet went sour, they panicked.

However the AI story ends, it is starting the same way that the dot-com and housing stories began—with trillions of dollars. Normally, software is relatively inexpensive to develop and cheap to scale. (Mark Zuckerberg wrote most of the code for Thefacebook.com by himself in his dorm, shelling out $85 a month for a server.) But ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude Code require a tremendous amount of “compute”—basically, computing power. So AI start-ups and Silicon Valley giants are spending $600 billion this year alone on silicon chips and data-center construction.

They aren’t footing the bill themselves. They are issuing shares, as Anthropic did last week. They’re selling bonds: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle issued $223 billion in debt in the first eight months of the year. They are also making arrangements with private creditors.

Here’s where things start to feel very 1996. The markets are making a massive wager on AI revolutionizing the business world, just as they made a massive wager on the World Wide Web revolutionizing the business world. The value of AI-related stocks has climbed $27 trillion since the launch of ChatGPT, according to Goldman Sachs. Even given the recent correction, the country is in the midst of a hell of a bull market.

Yet the markets are making that bet on the basis of some awfully rosy assumptions. By 2031, AI-linked companies will need to generate $6 trillion a year in revenue to justify the cost of the build-out, according to calculations by the consultancy Bain & Company. That is six times as much as American businesses and households spend on gasoline and other petroleum products each year, to give a sense of scale. It is eight times as much as U.S. firms shell out for software. It’s more than the country spends on health care.

AI revenue seems highly likely to fall short of expectations, while AI overhead costs seem highly likely to exceed them. Anthropic is committed to spending nearly $1 trillion on compute in the coming years. And many AI firms have hefty borrowing costs to deal with, as well as hefty infrastructure costs. CoreWeave, an AI cloud-computing firm, is spending a quarter of its revenue servicing its loans.

[Read: The economy is a hostage situation]

The disconnect between the two sides of the balance sheet is making the markets feel bearish. Some AI companies are having a harder time raising the cash they need to refine and run their algorithms. In time, that could force them to cancel construction projects and miss payments to their creditors, deepening the market rout. So would we end up in 2000, facing a sharp correction and perhaps a soft recession? Or in 2007, staring into the abyss?

The first big issue, well known at this point, has to do with concentration and circularity. Much like during the housing bubble, today’s investors are all-in on one big bet: that AI will pay off. Moreover, they are staking that bet on a small family of companies with downright Habsburgian relationships with one another. Amazon has invested $33 billion in Anthropic, which has committed to buying $110 billion in Amazon web services. Microsoft has spent $13 billion on a stake in OpenAI, which is signed up to purchase $250 billion in cloud-computing services from Microsoft. One firm’s investment is another firm’s revenue is another firm’s operating budget is another firm’s interest payment is another firm’s profit. “The chip maker, the data-center developer, the person who’s leasing the data center, and, also remember, the end customers too—if any of those things start drying up, for any reason, everybody is affected,” Evan LeFlore, the director of AI, innovation, and economic opportunity at Better Markets, a nonpartisan research and advocacy group, told me. “You will then see, potentially, a chain reaction.”

Which firms would get hit the hardest? That is the second issue. Nobody knows with any precision. AI companies are “outsourcing a lot of the data-center development and increasingly the investment in IT as well—subcontracting it to private companies, third-party private landlords,” Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, an economist at Columbia, told me. They are inventing financing mechanisms, using things like graphics processing units as collateral. (Cool, cool, cool.) And they are keeping these deals off their balance sheets. The Wall Street Journal recently estimated that Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta have $604 billion worth of conventionally tallied liabilities and $2.4 trillion tucked away in the footnotes. “It’s a mix of a shell game and a Ponzi scheme,” Phillip Basil, the director of economic growth and financial stability at Better Markets, told me.

That gets to the third issue. The financiers staking the AI boom are “reasonably sophisticated,” says Douglas Holtz-Eakin, the president of the American Action Forum and a member of the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission. They know that “these are high-risk, high-yield opportunities,” he told me, and “are correctly trying to hedge their own books.” Still, markets do not always discipline themselves, as the world found out in 2008. They’re especially terrible at disciplining themselves when dealing with imperfect information, borrowed money, and heady doses of irrational enthusiasm.

If traditional banks were the predominant financiers of the AI build-out, the country’s regulators would have ample insight into counterparty exposure, asset quality, and other fiddly-but-important metrics. They’d have the authority to conduct stress tests. They’d have tools to reduce leverage and prevent contagion. But traditional banks aren’t the predominant financiers of the AI build-out. (Nvidia, the chip maker, is known as the “central bank of AI,” making Jensen Huang today’s Bernanke, I guess.) Much of today’s dealmaking is happening in the loosely regulated shadow-banking system. When an AI “neocloud” takes out a loan from a private-credit company, for instance, “even the Fed doesn’t really monitor that,” Van Nieuwerburgh said. “They’re not responsible for the supervision of non-bank financial institutions.”

A number of AI firms do fall under the purview of the Securities and Exchange Commission. But Donald Trump has hobbled the agency: Its case volume is half of what it was three years ago, and its penalty assessments are a quarter of what they were. The Office of Financial Research, created after the housing crisis and tasked with assessing novel risks, has monitoring responsibilities and subpoena power. Trump has not so much hobbled it as gutted it. The Financial Stability Oversight Council, chaired by the Treasury secretary, is meant to coordinate among regulators and promote market discipline. It is busy convening AI stakeholders for “cross-sector dialogue on high-value AI use cases, practical approaches to scaling innovation, and regulatory impediments” to the technology’s adoption. As for Trump himself, he’s pulling strings at Nvidia, a company he was directly invested in as of the end of September.

“God knows what is happening in the regulatory structure and the supervisory structure right now,” Phil Angelides, the chair of the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, told me. AI’s creditors and debtors seem to be pretty much free to make deals as they see fit, and to keep the details to themselves, too.

[Josh Tyrangiel: Three theories of Jensen Huang’s resurrection]

If AI revenue fails to take off in the way investors expect that it will, the tech industry might be able to act as its own backstop, Van Nieuwerburgh said, preventing a surge of defaults and write-downs. Alphabet, for instance, generates close to $1 billion a day from advertisements, and has a 21 percent profit margin. It has essentially unlimited capacity to raise money by issuing bonds and shares. The company could easily cover enormous AI-related losses, as could firms such as Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon. We could end up in a dot-com scenario, with most of the pain coming from falling equity prices.

Or a chip manufacturer or a hyperscaler could go bankrupt. A software firm might allow its debt-laden data-center subsidiaries to go under, defaulting on their liabilities. Financiers could write down the value of their investments, meaning a flurry of margin calls and asset sales. Chaos could spread throughout the financial system, amplified by leverage and amplified again by a lack of clarity about who owes what to whom. American banks, which have $1 trillion and counting in exposure to the build-out, could falter. Households would watch their retirement savings evaporate and pull back on spending. The engine of GDP growth would sputter. It might look like the housing bubble, if perhaps less severe.

At least the head of the “central bank of AI”—a man who reportedly romanced a few of his subordinates while being romanced by a Chinese spy, a man who Trump describes as “a Great Patriot and National Treasure”—is not worried. “The money we’ve invested is going to generate tremendous returns,” Huang promised on CNBC a few months ago. “I think the risk is low.”

A Good Gift Is More Ordinary Than You Think

The holidays are the worst time to give a gift. Expectations are just too high. Chocolate or socks received on a random Wednesday in August would be incredible; the same socks on Father’s Day or chocolate on Valentine’s Day veer toward cliché. A gift is a paradox: As it becomes more socially expected, it also becomes harder to give well.

I learned to give gifts from my late mother, who was terrible at gift giving no matter the time of year. She would agree to buy a specific thing for my kids but renege at the last minute, for mysterious reasons. Or she’d ask for the kids’ clothing sizes, then send garments in different ones. Once, she bought me a new set of flatware—nice!—but not in sufficient quantity to actually supply the kitchen, so I had to buy more to make the gift useful. Over the years, my wife received innumerable scarves and folk-art objects that matched Mom’s tastes but not her own.

Mom didn’t mean to be terrible at gift giving, and I now understand her effort as a struggle rather than a slight. She seemed to recognize that she couldn’t conceptualize the desires of other people. Often, she tried to make up for that deficiency with sheer volume. A birthday parcel would contain lots of little junk—the kind of stuff you’d collect as trade-show swag. Each delivered the earnest desire to make a gift connect, along with the tragedy of connection’s failure. In the end, by getting the practice so wrong, Mom gave me the gift of understanding gifts better. The best gifts give something greater than any one thing: the message that you know the recipient in a nuanced and personal way. To give well is first to see, and to receive well is to allow yourself to be seen.

This is very hard to do. Over time, people have developed ways to reduce gift giving’s burden: Wish lists, for example, were once limited to kids but now cross generations. Telling someone else exactly what you want is good, in that it disambiguates desire. But it also frees a person from the difficult work that can make a gift truly meaningful.

Think about the people closest to you—your spouse or children or parents, for example. You know so much about them, but when it comes to thinking of a gift, you might find yourself stumped. My wife likes knitting. I could try to learn about yarn or yarn-wrangling tools, and doing so might connect me to her through a deeper understanding of her interests. But it could also lead me astray. When I think about my own hobbies and avocations—home espresso and gelato, for example—I closely guard my tools and materials and don’t want someone else getting involved. Similarly, my adult son is really into photography, and because of that passion I know that he already owns a version of almost anything I might think of.

Setting aside the most obvious interests of your gift recipient can help avoid this mess. But doing so makes giving a gift even harder, because now you must find an even less apparent feature of the other person to develop into an opportunity for communion.

Unfortunately, this is where many people settle on options from the gift-guide mainstay, “the person who has everything.” That usually means things nobody wants or needs—superfluous curiosities such as a neck massager or an electric gravy warmer. An indulgent or expensive gift might also not fit the bill. You might feel formidable or magnanimous when you give a lavish gift, but receiving one can be disquieting. Or, if a wish list is on offer, it might entice you to choose a variation on the category of item listed. Do not do this; this is very bad. (Everyone remembers getting the wrong version of the popular toy as a kid.) Understandably, you will want to make your mark on their wishes. But the best gift is not about you at all—not even your generosity in the giving. A gift entails the attempt to set yourself aside as much as possible, and to wrap a bow around the most nuanced and tender understanding of someone else that you can muster.

To do this, you will have to dig deep. Think back over the past year or so, and try to recall when your gift recipient may have revealed a desire, problem, aspiration, or fact that felt distinctive. I’m not talking about a specific yearning for a consumer good, although that is one option. I mean a moment that, when revisited, reveals something fundamental about their current self. Focus on the most ordinary events and encounters, the ones even the person on your mind may not remember. Those moments can offer the richest information about what makes someone who they are. Perhaps a friend delighted at a specific jam served at a brunch out. Maybe, while unloading the dishwasher, a family member noted that their favorite old teaspoon had lost all its counterparts over time. Maybe you witnessed a parent’s struggle to haul out a large appliance, which a smaller version could ease. Many times, these gifts are the most meaningful, yet also decidedly affordable and easy to find besides.

These gifts need not be substantial or even useful. You might give a friend who couldn’t come on a group vacation an especially smooth stone found at the destination. You could bake your own rendition of a cookie your spouse had recalled buying from a long-closed shop as a child. Identifying the pattern for a singleton china teacup purchased from an antique store might be more appreciated and enduring than splurging on the rest of the set. Likewise, finding an exact replacement for a wind chime that fell in a storm, or secretly arranging the repair of a zipper on a favorite sweater, will make them feel seen rather than simply checked off a list. (Imagine how delighted you would be to unwrap that old sweater you’d tossed aside to discover that it had been revived.)

Giving a gift truly well is so hard to do, it happens very infrequently. You may not give or receive a good one this year, or any year. I wish we had a better name for when it really works (or that gift had not become so hollowed out as a noun). In addition to the thought and the act, giving requires an alignment of circumstance and timing. It rarely succeeds. That’s okay. Think of gifts not merely as good intentions or as things offered but as a way to practice the act of giving—and to appreciate the sanctity of those moments when we get it right.

PS

A long time ago, I made a ridiculous game called Cow Clicker, which was about clicking cows (or pictures of them anyway). I’ve written about it before for The Atlantic, and I’ll send you to that story instead of trying to explain it. I drew a lot of cute and unusual cows for the game, and they became a sort of trademark.

That year, my wife had a custom cookie cutter made in the shape of my cows, and then she used it to make dozens of detailed cookies that looked like them. We still have the cookie cutter and bring it out for most holidays, a reminder of my bovine period. It’s one of the most memorable gifts I’ve received.

Early in his tenure, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick so alienated members of his regular security detail that some of them quit. He didn’t have to look far for replacements. The Department of Commerce’s own Bureau of Industry and Security employs law-enforcement agents who carry weapons and have the power to arrest people. The department reassigned some of them to serve as Lutnick’s personal bodyguards, pulling away from their duties agents who might otherwise have prevented the smuggling of AI chips to China. Instead, they would now travel on his private jet and guard his various homes, according to five people with knowledge of the matter.

Some commerce officials were baffled that Lutnick had diverted personnel for his own protection as the United States is racing to stay ahead of China on advanced artificial intelligence. BIS is the nation’s leading bureau for the enforcement of export controls, and its agents are trained to keep prized U.S. technology out of the wrong hands. In theory, they should be on the front lines of the effort to prevent illicit AI-chip trading—the issue at the center of the controversy over Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and his relationship with an alleged Chinese spy. (Huang has admitted to the affair but said that he hadn’t known that the woman might have been a Chinese spy.)

BIS agents are trained in many aspects of law enforcement, including crime-scene investigation and counterterrorism. They are not, however, trained in protecting VIPs and dignitaries. The Commerce Department has its own Office of Security, and secretaries have been provided with protection since at least 1986. But that protection has traditionally been lighter than what’s provided to senior officials who are more closely involved in national security, who are considered more likely targets. The assignment of BIS agents as bodyguards, former officials told us, seemed driven by Lutnick’s desire to have a detail comparable to that of other Cabinet members, such as the secretary of defense and the attorney general.

Democrats and some Republicans have questioned why Lutnick hasn’t done more to prevent illegal sales of advanced chips from Nvidia and other firms, or expanded the list of companies that are barred on national-security grounds from making such purchases. In particular, lawmakers want to know more about Lutnick’s approach to Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that’s under investigation by Commerce and the Justice Department for potential chip smuggling. Even after U.S. intelligence, about a year ago, identified the Megaspeed executive Alice Huang as a Chinese-intelligence asset who was in a romantic relationship with Jensen Huang, the Commerce Department didn’t put Megaspeed on its trade blacklist. The company was added only yesterday, the same day that Nvidia’s board restored Jensen Huang to his roles as the company’s president and chief executive, under pressure from President Trump. The belated addition to the blacklist appears to have been more symbolic than substantive, however: One trade expert suggested that because of the way the regulation adding Megaspeed to the list was written, the company would still have access to many types of AI chips.

The Bureau of Industry and Security has fewer than 200 special agents; most of them work in 10 field offices across the United States, and about a dozen or so work overseas. It wasn’t clear how many agents were affected by the rotations onto and off Lutnick’s protection staff. Two former officials told us that the assignment usually involved three to four agents at a time. A Commerce Department official said in a statement that “at a time when the Secretary was facing the highest level of credible threats in the Department’s history some agents were temporarily detailed to augment executive protection.” The official added that the number of agents at BIS has increased, though she didn’t provide numbers.

As artificial intelligence has boomed, the bureau has struggled to keep pace with the global trade in U.S. chips, suggesting that it could ill-afford the secondment of agents elsewhere. The Commerce Department’s Office of the Inspector General, in a June report, found that BIS has not taken adequate action to enforce export controls on goods destined for China and Hong Kong. In a report a month earlier, it found similar enforcement failures regarding Russia and Belarus.

[Read: The limits of positive thinking]

Overall, the number of public penalties and enforcement actions for violating U.S.-export rules has fallen since Lutnick took office in February 2025. Across 2023 and 2024, the annual case rate was close to 100; in the 17 months from March 2025 to the start of August, the annualized rate dropped to about half that, according to The Export Practitioner, a trade publication. Amid the furor over Nvidia, the inspector general last week began an evaluation of the bureau’s oversight of advanced-computing chips.

Current and former commerce officials we spoke with put the blame for the bureau’s lackluster performance on Lutnick and his team. They contend that Lutnick and senior officials have repeatedly stressed Trump’s interest in a détente with China and have placed commercial interests above national security. Trump has made clear his desire for a new China trade deal and last week sought to further improve relations with President Xi Jinping at a summit in Shenzhen. Trump’s approach runs counter to the administration’s aggressive posturing about the need to restrict Chinese trade.

The diversion of agents to serve in Lutnick’s protective detail has sapped expert resources and prevented agents from doing the high-stakes work they are hired and trained to perform, current and former officials told us. “They’re not trained for protective detail,” one current official told us. “These are professional investigators.” A former official called it “completely inappropriate” to pull agents to protect Lutnick.

The Commerce Department, meanwhile, is seeking an increase in staffing. In April, Lutnick told the House Appropriations Subcommittee that BIS needed additional resources and enforcement personnel, including 290 new enforcement officers, to prevent the diversion of semiconductors to China and to protect American innovation. He didn’t mention the redeployment of some of the officers whom BIS already employed.

Lutnick, a friend of Trump’s and a former Wall Street executive, is one of the Cabinet’s wealthiest members. His most recent financial-disclosure form lists his holdings, which include a $25 million mansion that he purchased in 2024 in the Berkley neighborhood of Washington, D.C. The house, modeled after France’s Château du Grand-Lucé, was until recently the most expensive home ever sold in the capital. Lutnick also owns properties in New York City, the Hamptons, and South Florida. He made much of his money as the CEO of Cantor Fitzgerald, a New York financial firm, where his two sons, Brandon and Kyle, hold senior leadership positions. Cantor also held approximately 1.8 million Nvidia shares as of the end of June, which were valued at the time at $362 million, according to the company’s financial disclosures. (The firm has said that it has no material proprietary position in Nvidia, and its holdings mainly arise from its services for clients.)

Lutnick no longer has a financial interest in Cantor Fitzgerald, but some in Congress have argued that his close family ties to the firm represent a conflict of interest. Some White House officials have privately expressed similar worries, according to several current and former administration officials. Despite these concerns, and Lutnick’s well-documented feuds with others at the top of the administration, Trump has consistently rebuffed pushes to drop his friend from the Cabinet.

The relatively smooth functioning of the midterms did not end President Trump’s threat to American election integrity. After initially accepting the results, Trump has begun calling them into question, and House Republicans have named the outspoken 2020-election denier Jim Jordan as their next leader. Ensuring a free and fair 2028 presidential election and a peaceful transition of power remain pressing concerns for anyone who wants the country back on solidly democratic footing.

In this episode of Autocracy in America, the Democratic election lawyer and founder of Democracy Docket Marc Elias speaks about his work safeguarding the midterms, the continued threats to our democratic system, and why restoring the Voting Rights Act won’t be enough to solve our problems.

At the center of any functioning democracy is the right to vote. A representative government is only truly representative if it somehow reflects the will of the people. The surest sign of a failing democracy, by contrast, is a flawed, manipulated voting process or one that always produces contested results. Americans have been trying to manipulate their voting system for decades.

The word gerrymander was invented in 1812 when then–Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry drew a voting district that resembled a salamander. During the Jim Crow years, some American states wrote laws deliberately designed to prevent Black people from voting. It’s not a celebration of democracy; it’s a failure. I have promoted—and this has not been adopted anywhere, but I’m quite serious about it—we should pass a federal law that says that no voter waits in line for more than 30 minutes to vote, and if they do, the government pays the voter for their time.

We have this with airlines, or at least we used to, I think, until it got gutted recently. But the fact is that if you wait in line for an hour, the county should have to pay you for the time that it took you to wait in line ’cause they failed you. They are in the business of making sure you can vote.

And this brings me to the last thing. We need a voter-centric jurisprudence and a voter-centric understanding of voting. Voting laws don’t exist for the convenience of election officials. There are a lot of people, including my friends in the reform community and on the left and in the Democratic Party, who celebrate the election officials.

I celebrate the voters. So the courts should be adopting a jurisprudence that treats voters and their inconvenience and their suppression as the most important thing in election cases, not the administrability, not the Is it too late in the election cycle?

But it’s kind of what you were saying about redistricting—what’s kind of behind your sense that gerrymandering is wrong. It’s the idea that the voters are sort of afterthoughts in the process, and it should be the other way around. They should be centered.

And I’d like to see the centering of voters in the way in which courts apply law. It would have a huge impact on gerrymandering, by the way, but it would also have an impact on voter-registration laws and these affidavit ballot laws and the college students who can’t have polling locations at their schools. All of those things would get thrown out because they’re anti-voter.

And we would not be trying to balance, Well, yes, but it’s inconvenient for the state or, The state doesn’t like this or like that or, The election officials don’t have enough staff.

And so I think that is where we need to go in any future reform legislation. It’s what I’d like to see the left adopt as a unifying theory of both voting rights, redistricting, and campaign finance, because I think it would actually center the debate about democracy where it should be, which is not on the candidates, not on the election officials, but on the people who we are actually trying to get participating.

Applebaum: It would also solve another problem in the United States, which is that I feel that for different reasons, Americans right now feel this lack of agency.

So their lives are organized by algorithms on the internet, which show them stuff whether they wanna see it or not. And data centers are built in their communities without their input. And so many things seem to happen very quickly without them being able to affect anything. And one of the—always the causes of decline in democracy is when people feel it’s pointless. You know, My vote is pointless, or my action is pointless, or I don’t have any power in the process.

But you’re right that giving people the sense that, We want you to vote and your vote is important, and here, we’re gonna make it easier for you—it doesn’t solve all the problems, but it’s one way of making people feel that citizenship is important and it’s an important part of what you do and who you are.

Elias: Absolutely. If you look at exit polls, the second-most motivating issue for voters in this election was the state of democracy.

It followed the economy. It was above health care, immigration, and other things. And you cannot understand the public’s high prioritization of data centers or the Epstein files, which were two of the most salient issues in the election.

Neither of those were economic. I mean, neither of them are primarily economic. Both of them are a sense that there are these group of people for whom the rules don’t apply, and they can just do whatever they want to your community. They can do whatever they want to young girls. They act with impunity, and that you as an individual are powerless. And in order for a democracy to work, there has to be a way that people can feel like by voting, it changes the material conditions of their lives. It changes the policy outcomes they experience.

And I think that the data-center debate very much is about the disconnect of communities feeling like they have that power from elites who act like they don’t have that power.

Applebaum: Marc Elias, thank you very much.

Elias: Thank you.

The pictures show men splayed on a highway, their faces concealed in pools of blood. A pickup truck riddled with bullet holes. An assault rifle lying by one man’s outstretched hands. Ecuadorean Defense Minister Gian Carlo Loffredo posted the grisly images as proof of his country’s deepening partnership with the Trump administration in its campaign against drug cartels in Latin America. The photos, he said on social media two months ago, stemmed from an operation conducted in cooperation with U.S. Southern Command. “We promised: ‘Jail or hell’ and this one didn’t make it to jail,” Loffredo wrote on X on September 23, saying that the target was a cartel leader known as “Charlie.” Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth reposted Loffredo’s message the same day, adding that the U.S. military was “proud to stand w/ the Armed Forces of Ecuador.”

But the Trump administration is now investigating whether Ecuadorean forces committed crimes or human-rights abuses during that operation—including whether the suspects were killed while in Ecuadorean custody—and whether U.S. forces played any role, one senior administration official and one former official told us.

While administration officials look into the raid, the U.S. military has suspended joint operations with Ecuadorean troops, one of the people familiar with the matter said. But the U.S. military has not stopped its push to embed itself in Ecuador’s fight against cartels. (Hegseth visited the country last week.)

Ecuador is Exhibit A of the Trump administration’s expanding militarized campaign to strike powerful drug and other criminal groups in Latin America in partnership with Donald Trump–aligned right-wing governments. Stephen Miller, Trump’s longtime immigration adviser, has driven that effort. Miller met with Ecuadorean President Daniel Noboa the day before the “Charlie” raid, while Noboa was in New York to address the United Nations General Assembly. With Miller’s recent appointment as White House chief of staff, those in Trump’s orbit are preparing for an escalation of U.S.-military operations in Latin America in the coming weeks and months. “This is all Stephen’s thing,” the senior official said, referring to the expanding partnerships. “This would not have happened without Stephen.”

The U.S. military has forces in Ecuador to train and advise local troops and conduct what it calls “partnered operations”—joint raids or other counter-drug missions. A Pentagon official, who spoke on the condition of anonymity under ground rules set by the department, told us in an emailed statement that U.S.-military personnel did not take part in the September 23 operation, but declined to provide further details or comment about any review. But officials are still working to establish whether U.S. forces provided intelligence assistance or any other kind of support to the operation, sources told us.

The Defense Department “takes allegations of misconduct by our partners seriously,” the Pentagon official said. “The Department remains committed to working alongside sovereign partners, including Ecuador, to confront narco-terrorist networks, strengthen collective security, and defend our shared neighborhood.”

Officials from Ecuador’s embassy in Washington did not provide comment before publication.

A White House official told us in an email that the drug gangs that the United States is battling alongside Latin American allies represent “a clear and present danger” to U.S. security, and said that the actions taken under those partnerships “have saved countless lives.” The official added that, at the time, Miller served as the homeland-security adviser and was responsible for interagency coordination.

“The Trump Administration is the first administration in history to adopt a comprehensive strategy to dismantle and defeat narco-terrorists that are responsible for the deaths of hundreds of thousands of Americans,” the official wrote. When we asked the White House which agency or agencies were leading the examination of the raid, it didn’t respond.

Trump in his second term has taken a special interest in Latin America, a region that has been largely overlooked by U.S. policy makers in recent decades. The administration wants to choke off the flow of migrants and drugs. That has brought a Trumpian corollary to the Monroe Doctrine—which the president proudly calls the “Donroe Doctrine,” a modern version of the 19th-century principle that the Western Hemisphere must remain under American influence.

Miller’s vision for combatting cartels has expanded accordingly. He is a principal architect of the administration’s campaign of missile strikes against suspected drug traffickers at sea, which legal experts and critics have said is likely illegal. (The White House has cited a classified opinion from the Office of Legal Counsel that it insists authorizes the strikes.) The campaign has killed at least 240 people in more than 70 air strikes since last fall, according to a tally of Pentagon announcements. Some of those attacks have occurred in the Caribbean, the military has said, but the majority are in the eastern Pacific, where there have also been a number of interdictions off the coast of Ecuador.

[Read: A chief of staff who ‘will crack skulls’]

The administration has at times been willing to overlook its Latin American partners’ records on human rights and democracy, depicting the battle against criminal groups as an existential struggle for all in the region. “You have to make a choice here. We’re going to choose on the side of leaders that are going to be serious about taking these groups on and eliminating them once and for all,” Secretary of State Marco Rubio told reporters during a visit to Ecuador earlier this year. “Otherwise, those groups—those terrorist groups—will eat these countries up.” The U.S. military touted its relationship with Ecuador in March when it announced the launch of joint operations against cartels.

Noboa has put addressing rampant criminality and violence at the center of his presidency. He is among the most public of Trump’s Latin American allies in courting U.S.-military involvement. In his own social-media post within hours of the September raid, Noboa echoed the White House’s view of the fight against cartels as a hemisphere-wide campaign against criminality. “This battle doesn’t end at our borders,” he wrote.

The slain cartel leader known as “Charlie” was Carlos Germán Muñoz Jara, according to Ecuadorean media. He was believed to have links to the Ecuadorean gang Los Choneros and the Mexican cartel Jalisco Nueva Generación. The Trump administration has designated both groups foreign terrorist organizations.

The administration’s campaign is not limited to Ecuador. It is also seeking to accelerate counter-drug operations with Colombia, historically the most important U.S.-military partner in the region, and Venezuela following the January ouster of the dictator Nicolás Maduro. Hegseth visited Bogotá and Caracas last week to tout expanded military cooperation. The administration’s desire to intensify counter-cartel operations in Mexico, potentially on a unilateral basis, has caused friction between Trump and Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum. Mexico captured a top drug boss, El Chapo Isidro, last week, in a sign of Sheinbaum’s determination to show Washington that her country is taking action.

[Read: Claudia Sheinbaum’s impossible dilemma]

Ecuador has approved a dramatic expansion of the U.S. military’s role in the country, including joint operations and the positioning of U.S. troops on the ground to train and advise their Ecuadorean counterparts. At least two MQ-9 Reaper drones are now based in Ecuador, along with other equipment provided by the United States. Noboa has faced accusations of authoritarian tactics employed against political opponents. Human Rights Watch alleged in a report that a March Ecuadorean military raid had resulted in the torture of farm workers. Ecuadoreans have put some limits on Noboa’s efforts, rejecting a 2025 referendum that would have permitted U.S. military bases in the country.

Ecuador’s counter-drug partnership with the United States goes beyond the military. In recent months, the Drug Enforcement Administration, FBI, and Department of Homeland Security have also supported the country’s efforts.

With the Pentagon and White House pushing for more military action against narco-terrorists, U.S. troops are likely to be busy in Latin America. That cooperation could be a boon for the administration’s vision, but it comes with its own peril—U.S. troops are operating alongside local forces answering to their own commanders, and playing by their own rules.
