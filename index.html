I tally the dead. I tally today’s dead. I seek a tally of the dead or seek
yesterday’s dead. I try to count them, the dead. Reports tell me there are,
today, more dead. I announce the dead—the house is quiet. I try to
count. I seek numbers. I provide numbers. I wake trying. I cover
myself in numbers, trying to keep still.  

I seek some stillness beyond the dead—I try to just manage. I tally the dead,
each pair of lungs, I wander through numbers, the daffodils nevertheless
fly up. I hide the numbers, paper over what I know. The warm house fills.  
What are you doing, he says. I say prayers for, try to put faces to the dead

as if to anchor the dead—I condemn the dead, their utter defenselessness.  
Again I try to spare no numbers, the ones who have died, not the ones
about to die, and when I wake they are there, counted and uncounted.  
When life takes more away, I keep the dead inside me, the ones not
injured but gone, although many are injured. My counting—
what even matters? I don’t feel like going out. Soon I am counting.  

At the gate of the house I feel the faint dusk resolve. I bless the dead,
I forget the dead from last year, I lose more dead, I tally what I can.  
I count through the morning. Light drifts across the thick rug. I move
my hands as if I am counting the dead. As if I am altering the dead
in my hands as I count.

The sight of military troops patrolling in a peaceful American city would once have been strange, if not outright alarming. But since last August, when Donald Trump first deployed the National Guard to Washington, D.C., residents have become accustomed to seeing men and women in camouflage military fatigues on duty throughout the city and its public-transit system. Sometimes, they trail federal officers from agencies such as the FBI and U.S. Marshals Service, dispatched by Trump to patrol the district in bulletproof vests. More often, the troops wander around unaccompanied, in small groups. 

D.C. has now entered its second year with federal troops on its streets. President Trump initially ordered the Guard into the capital city, sent federal agents to work as beat cops, and briefly seized control of D.C.’s local police department following the attempted carjacking of a former member of Elon Musk’s DOGE team. The early days of the D.C. Safe and Beautiful Task Force produced dramatic headlines. As the year dragged on, though, its activities faded from national news coverage, even as armed troops and federal agents continued to patrol. The Pentagon says it plans to keep the Guard in D.C. through January 2029—which will cost taxpayers more than $1 billion. 

For all that money, the Guard’s purpose here is largely inscrutable. “They don’t bother nobody; they just go back and forth, and back and forth,” Aundrea’ Pulliam, a Washington Metropolitan Area Transit Authority employee at the Dupont Circle Metro stop, told us. (As we spoke, a few Guard members walked by, heading toward the escalators.) Occasionally, Pulliam said, she has seen Guards help a rider who’s “super drunk,” or check out an abandoned backpack. But most of the time, a station manager, Deidra Foster, told us, it seems like “they’re really bored.” 

Whereas other American cities targeted by Trump with National Guard deployments found success in court, D.C.’s lack of statehood limits its ability to fight back. The presence of the task force represents a particularly Trump-era indignity for the city: both comic and menacing; abusive and ineffective; appalling to some and, as time goes on, exhaustingly commonplace. Antidemocratic though the federal takeover may be, D.C. residents have been forced to live with it.  

Matt McClain for The Atlantic
A National Guard member yawns as he and others stand near the Korean War Veterans Memorial on Monday, August 3, 2026, in Washington, D.C.

At first, the 800 or so troops called up were locals: members of the D.C. National Guard, which, unlike other state and territorial Guard units, is under the president’s direct control. Then, governors from around the country, mostly Republicans, began volunteering their own troops. The total head count was about 2,000 before ballooning to 5,000 before the America 250 celebrations—enough that, in some neighborhoods, walking to the Metro or picking up groceries without encountering Guardsmen became nearly impossible. 

The Trump administration has promoted the Guard’s presence as an overwhelming success. “Washington D.C. went from High Crime to NO CRIME — Thank you President Trump!” the president posted on Truth Social in early July. (This month, Trump claimed that crime in the city had dropped by 92 percent, citing a favorite number of his.) True, crime has largely fallen in the capital over the past year—but it was on the decline before Trump’s surge, and cities across the country without any comparable federal presence have seen similar positive trends. (Numbers reported by D.C.’s Metropolitan Police Department suggest that violent crime in the district may, in fact, be slightly up.) A recent report by the moderate Niskanen Center found that deployment of the National Guard led to a roughly 25 percent decrease in petty property crime over the first six months, but had “no measurable effect on violent crime.” Another analysis, published by the left-leaning Center for American Progress, reported “strong evidence that the deployment of the National Guard did not result in a measurable decrease in violent crime rates.” In response, Trump threatened to sue the organization.

The National Guard may not have done much in part because there isn’t much for them to do. Troops have been sworn in as deputy U.S. marshals, which gives them some powers of law enforcement, but the terms of their appointment state that they cannot independently conduct searches or make arrests. (They can, however, detain suspected lawbreakers until either local or federal law enforcement arrives.) According to a recent analysis of court records by Reuters, troops have been mentioned in only 1.3 percent of criminal cases prosecuted in D.C. Superior Court over the past year—and in some of those cases, they were the victims. The media-relations page for the Guard’s D.C. presence includes stories about picking up trash and reuniting a lost boy with his parents on the National Mall.

Perhaps most notable, the Guard has begun carrying the overdose-prevention drug naloxone, commonly known by its brand name, Narcan. Early this month, the D.C. National Guard’s interim commanding general told reporters that Guardsmen had administered the drug 325 times. Sergeant Hare, a Guardsman outside the Woodley Park Metro station, who declined to give his first name, citing National Guard policies, told us that he had lost track of how many drug-related emergencies he’d helped with, but said it was “the main thing” he’d done during his deployment.

Andrew Leyden / Getty
Air National Guardsmen work clearing leaves and debris from McPherson Square on August 28, 2025, in Washington, D.C.

“Could this be handled without having the National Guard in D.C.? Probably,” Regina LaBelle, an addiction-policy professor at Georgetown University, told us. But “since they are here,” she said, “it’s really good that they are using naloxone where it’s needed.”

Whatever the troops are or aren’t doing, their presence is a reminder of Trump’s power to shape the world as he alone pleases. America’s Founders understood domestic deployment of the military as the tool of a tyrant, and put in place legal protections against it—protections that Trump has now set about eroding. The president treats America’s cities as though they’re populated by scofflaws to be subjugated rather than by citizens with a say in their own future, and deploying the Guard to D.C. is a way to make that vision into a reality.  

“They’re an armed occupying force in our city,” Merawi Gerima, an organizer with the D.C. Alliance Against Racist and Political Repression, told us. Gerima and other activists we spoke with emphasized that they understood the Guard’s deployment as only one aspect of the larger federal presence in D.C. As part of the D.C. Safe and Beautiful Task Force, federal agents have been reassigned from their normal responsibilities to act as beat cops, dispatched from agencies such as the FBI; the Drug Enforcement Administration; the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives; Customs and Border Protection; the Secret Service; the Park Police; the U.S. Marshals Service; the Diplomatic Security Service; and, oddly, the IRS. 

Although Guard troops are the visual signature of Trump’s seizure of control over the city, they comprise the least energetic component of the task force. The federal-law-enforcement officers deployed across D.C. at Trump’s directive have been far busier, and more aggressive. Twice this past fall, agents with ICE’s Homeland Security Investigations division fired their weapons during traffic stops. Months later, a deputy U.S. marshal shot and killed a robbery suspect. After the administration lifted restrictions on car chases by Park Police, the agency launched a flurry of high-speed chases; multiple crashes and at least two deaths—of a bystander and a fleeing driver—soon followed. During the first six months of the surge, immigration enforcement and other federal agents roamed the city, detaining residents off the street. 

Andrew Leyden / Getty
A National Guard unit patrols the National Mall on August 30, 2025, in Washington, D.C.

ICE has been less prominent since December, when a D.C. federal court enjoined the government from carrying out mass immigration arrests without a warrant. But Sandra Benavente, the advocacy manager at the immigrant-aid nonprofit Ayuda, told us that immigrant communities still feel the aftershocks of those arrests. “We’ve seen since last summer this climate of fear around all uniformed law enforcement,” she said—including the National Guard. Some of the group’s clients, including those relying on Ayuda for food or assistance with seeking legal status, have missed appointments because they are afraid of being stopped on their way downtown. 

Although ICE activity remains limited in D.C., other federal agencies continue to police the city in unprecedented numbers, sometimes alongside officers with D.C.’s local Metropolitan Police Department. The “lockup list” in D.C. Superior Court, which records people detained overnight by law enforcement for arraignment the next business day, routinely hovers near 100—far greater than the pre-surge norm of 60 or so. “There’s a difference in the intensity of the policing,” Gerima said, particularly “in Black and brown neighborhoods.” (All three of the men shot at by task-force members, including the person who died, were Black.) The additional federal presence provides law enforcement with more resources, he said, “and what they’re using that to do is to grab anybody for anything, any semblance of a crime.” Minor violations of local laws that might previously have brought about nothing more than a ticket—such as drinking from an open container—are now resulting in arrests by federal agents. 

Early in the National Guard’s D.C. deployment, especially after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered that troops begin carrying firearms, some residents worried about potential violence. So far, the troops deployed to the capital have not been responsible for any civilian deaths—the bare minimum of what one might hope for from a military deployment on U.S. soil. In fact, the only bloodshed related to the Guard involved troops as victims, when an Afghan man who had served with U.S. Special Forces in Afghanistan shot two members of the West Virginia Guard near the White House, one of whom later died.

Apart from this burst of violence, the troops’ stay in the capital has thus far been largely peaceful, albeit the ominous sort of peace that results from uniformed men and women strolling around with guns. In one notable exception partially caught on video, three troops were physically aggressive in detaining a woman outside her home—pushing her to the ground and zip-tying her hands so tightly that, she later wrote in a claim against the Guard, the plastic cuffs “ruptured one or more ligaments in her wrist,” requiring surgery. The National Guard says that the woman, Anna King, had thrown “unknown liquid” at other Guard members. Prosecutors brought assault charges against King for the liquid incident and for allegedly harming a member of the Guard while being detained; she has pleaded not guilty. 

Trump’s America is a land of perpetual indignation, where scandals that might once have ended a presidency have become commonplace. After a year of federal presence, D.C. is suspended somewhere between the routine and the outrageous. Still, activists we spoke with described continued energy to fight Trump’s overreach. Some organize protests; others quietly keep tabs on federal agents in their neighborhoods. The protest group Free D.C., which has grown prominent over the past year, encourages residents to voice their complaints by writing to governors who have sent their National Guard troops to the city. 

“I feel like it’s just better for me to ignore them,” Jill Robinson, a health educator who has lived in the area for four decades, told us. Yet “I feel angry and annoyed.” Robinson was attending a Free D.C. event, one of nearly a dozen planned by the group in the days leading up to August 11, the first anniversary of the National Guard deployment. She had attended previous Free D.C. training sessions, including on how to stay safe if law enforcement becomes aggressive at a protest. 

This event was meant to honor Nolberto Meza, the bystander who was struck and killed in Northwest D.C. during a Park Police chase of a suspected stolen car. (Meza had arrived in the U.S. from Venezuela, seeking asylum.) Amid torrents of summer rain, several dozen people had gathered on the bridge where Meza was hit, wearing ponchos, clutching umbrellas, and carrying signs with slogans such as END THE OCCUPATION. The attendees included more seasoned protesters like Robinson, but also newcomers such as Ella and Ant Peels, who’d moved to Washington in May from Annapolis, Maryland. They’d read about the deployment before their move, but seeing the Guardsmen out and about—“maybe three times every time we take a walk”—had made them uncomfortable. “It definitely gives an eerie feeling to living here,” Ella said. “It doesn’t make me feel safe.” 

Matt McClain for The Atlantic
A demonstrator holds a sign referring to the National Guard deployment in Washington, D.C., as people gather for a Free DC event entitled “One Year of Occupation: 13,000 Arrests and Pirro’s Lies” outside the H. Carl Moultrie Courthouse on Monday, August 3, 2026, in Washington, D.C.

Those opposed to the increased federal presence have notched some wins. U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro’s office has been continually stymied in federal court by opposition from D.C. residents sitting on grand and petit juries. This spring, during D.C.’s Democratic primary, the mayoral candidate Janeese Lewis George won a decisive victory after advocating for a more adversarial approach to the president than that of the current mayor, Muriel Bowser. The D.C. city council passed emergency measures requiring the MPD to keep records of federal officers’ role in arrests and incidents involving the use of force.  

But the city’s status as a federal enclave starkly restricts its ability to respond to Trump. Last month, all 13 members of the council signed letters calling on governors to withdraw troops from the city. A top Justice Department official dismissed the letters as “impotent theatrics.” (In response to a legal challenge filed by D.C. Attorney General Brian Schwalb, a federal judge ruled that both the D.C. and out-of-state Guard deployments were illegal, but that ruling has been paused while an appeals court considers the question.) The council can push MPD for transparency, but it has little power to constrain federal law enforcement, especially if those agents are patrolling separately from local police. These limitations underline the authoritarian tilt of the federal presence in D.C. The troops and federal agents are here without the city’s say, at the pleasure of one man only, and there is little the city can do about it.

The legal restraints on D.C. are unique, but the city is not going through this experience entirely alone. To the southwest, Memphis has a similar federal presence, alongside a deployment of hundreds of Tennessee National Guard troops sent by the state’s Republican governor. Trump announced the Memphis Safe Task Force a month after the D.C. task force began its surge, touting Memphis as a “replica of our extraordinarily successful efforts” in D.C. Now both the Guard and a contingent of federal agents have patrolled Memphis for almost a year. In four separate incidents, city residents died after being shot by members of the task force: Two National Guard troops were responsible for one shooting; the agents in the other three incidents were with the DEA and ICE’s Homeland Security Investigations division. (In one incident, it remains unclear whether the person died of the gunshot wound or a self-inflicted stabbing.) Memphis, a red-state “blue dot” whose autonomy has been continually rolled back by Republican state legislators, mirrors D.C.’s vulnerability.

In both Memphis and D.C., people harmed by the task force have limited options for legal redress. Although federal civil-rights law allows lawsuits against state and local officials for violations of constitutional rights, options for suing federal officers are far more limited. In one case, the ACLU is pushing for accountability after MPD, called in by an Ohio National Guard sergeant, handcuffed and detained a D.C. resident who had been protesting the Guard by trailing troops while blasting “The Imperial March,” from Star Wars, on his phone. D.C. settled, but litigation continues against the sergeant, who argues that he was acting in a federal role.

The activists we spoke with said they see their push to rid D.C. of this military presence as part of a larger effort to oppose authoritarian overreach. The “Imperial March” case makes the metaphor for them. It is absurd—a Star Wars joke that ended with someone in handcuffs—but also unnerving. In a city, or country, with fewer and fewer checks on presidential authority, a small act of dissent can provoke a big abuse of power.

For years, Iran confronted a balance of power in the Middle East that tilted against it. The region was dominated by a hostile American superpower, a nuclear-armed and militarily superior Israel, and significant local actors—Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates—that were strategically and economically aligned with the United States.

Today finds Iran in entirely new circumstances. It has gone head-to-head with the superpower and Israel, withstood a pounding from their most advanced weapons, and more than survived. As a result, Iran has, with American help, completely reconfigured the power structure of the Middle East and the Persian Gulf. The dominant power in the region going forward will be Iran, not the U.S. or Israel. Neighboring countries are already making their adjustments to this new reality. More distant powers will too. For years, the world has been dealing with an Iran constrained. It will now discover what Iran unleashed looks like.

Iran doesn’t need a deal with the United States. The common assumption that a weak and impoverished Iran would eventually have to make some accommodation to American power, open the Strait of Hormuz, and set about tending to its badly damaged economy has proved mistaken. The Islamic Republic has rarely appeared weaker or more vulnerable than it has over the past two years. Last February, President Trump renewed his “maximum pressure” campaign of international sanctions, which severely damaged Iran’s already-weak economy and cut billions of dollars in state revenues. Israel and the United States then launched the 12-day war that June to destroy Iranian nuclear facilities. The war significantly damaged Iran’s program and revealed just how vulnerable the country was to Israeli and American strikes. Then, in February, the United States and Israel launched the most recent war with the killing of the regime’s supreme leader and the great majority of its senior officials, followed by more than five weeks of unremitting attacks on Iranian military capabilities.

[Tom Nichols: Donald Trump effectively just told Iran to get a nuclear weapon]

Yet through all of this, the regime has survived and made no concessions. On the contrary, Iran exposed America’s Achilles’ heel: the vulnerability of Iran’s neighbors. A major turning point came in mid-March, when Israel attacked Iran’s South Pars gas field, and Iran retaliated against Qatar’s Ras Laffan complex, the world’s largest liquefied-natural-gas facility. The attack showed that although Iran obviously could not defeat the United States in an air war, Iranian missiles and drones could inflict enough damage on the oil-and-gas infrastructure of the Gulf to plunge the world into a prolonged economic crisis. The Gulf States understood this immediately, and so did Trump. He halted attacks on Iranian energy targets, ordered a reluctant Israel to do the same, and soon brought the larger campaign to a stop without extracting a single Iranian concession. Ever since, and despite many threats, Trump has not once ordered another strike against Iranian energy infrastructure.

This alone was enough to alter the balance of leverage between Iran and the United States. From April through last month, Trump repeatedly threatened to launch another massive attack against Iran. Each time, Iran threatened retaliation, Gulf States warned of the consequences, and Trump backed off. By his third or fourth bluff, Tehran had reason to conclude that the United States was no longer prepared to follow Iran up the ladder of escalation. Trump made this clear when he noted in mid-June that continuing the war would lead to “economic catastrophe,” possibly even a “depression,” and that he did not want to become President Herbert Hoover. Ever since, Trump has been trying, with notable desperation, to end the conflict.

The memorandum of understanding that the U.S. and Iran adopted in June reflected this reality. Although phrased to save face for the United States as much as possible, the MOU was a clear Iranian victory. It declared that the Islamic Republic of Iran, and no other nation, would “make arrangements” for the “safe passage of commercial vessels” through the Strait of Hormuz. In dialogue with Oman, without intermediaries or other participants, Iran would determine the strait’s administration “in line with the applicable international law and the sovereign rights of coastal states.”

The MOU did not call for a return to the prewar status quo. Neither did it provide the United States any say over future administration of the strait. Rather, it designated Iran as the only actor other than the weak Oman, and it even allowed Iran to charge nations for safe passage after 60 days. The MOU thus codified the defeat that the United States had suffered back in March, when Trump apparently realized that he either could not or would not risk further attacks on energy facilities in the region.

In retrospect, Iran’s leaders do not seem to have believed that they needed the MOU. Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei reportedly had to be persuaded by President Masoud Pezeshkian to sign it. Tehran’s relative indifference was understandable: The American concessions reflected such desperation to end the war that the Iranians could well have expected that the U.S. would eventually retreat, with or without a deal.

Iranian leaders certainly did not hesitate to blow up the cease-fire almost immediately when the U.S. Navy began shepherding tankers through the Omani side of the strait. Trump may or may not have understood the full extent of the concessions his negotiators had made regarding control of the strait, but from the Iranian point of view, the Omani route was an attempt to recapture what the U.S. had given away. For a while, the United States and Iran engaged in tit-for-tat strikes, culminating in intense American attacks in mid-July. When these had no effect, Trump warned on July 15 that the United States was “going to knock out all their power plants” and “all their bridges” unless Iran negotiated. Yet again, no attack followed.

Then the world, and the Iranians, learned one of the main reasons: Revelations that American stocks of crucial munitions were dangerously low, and that no less than the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff himself was looking for an “off-ramp,” sent a clear signal about the future course of American strategy. A major assault on Iran would threaten American interests elsewhere by weakening the United States in the possible event of a Chinese move on Taiwan or a Russian onslaught against a NATO member.

Missile shortages would also increase the hazards involved in a massive attack on Iranian infrastructure. The U.S. would be forced to send more manned aircraft within range of Iranian air defenses, and it would have relatively few interceptors on hand to protect the Gulf States and its own regional bases. The risk of American casualties would be high, as would the danger to the region’s energy infrastructure. And nothing would guarantee that such an assault would be any more effective than the five-plus weeks of bombings were earlier this year.

American actions have made plain that Washington fears escalation more than Iran does—not just for now, but for the future. This places limits on what the United States can do even at low levels of conflict. Because any military action against Iran risks stepping onto an escalation ladder that the U.S. does not want to ascend, even tit-for-tat strikes seem no longer to be part of American strategy. The president has reportedly told his advisers that he does not want to order more strikes. The Iranians have struck at several UAE ships this month and even fired ballistic missiles at the country, but the United States has not responded since July—a fact undoubtedly noted by the UAE and Iran’s other neighbors.

[Vivian Salama: From ‘indispensable’ ally to target of annihilation]

Given the evident American reluctance to fight another major campaign, Iran has reason to wonder what it can get from a deal that it will not get anyway without one. Senior Iranian officials are now calling for the withdrawal of American forces, the lifting of the American naval blockade and all sanctions, the freeing of Iran’s frozen assets, and the payment of war reparations. The conventional wisdom is that Iran’s leaders know that these demands are nonstarters for Washington and that they are therefore either rubbing Trump’s nose in defeat, angling for a better deal, or overplaying their hand. Many observers still assume that control of the strait is a bargaining chip to squeeze more concessions out of the United States or get Trump back to the MOU. But we are well past all that. A victor does not have to go back to the bargaining table except to set the terms of surrender.

Iran has now laid out those terms. They may appear maximalist and excessive, but Tehran must hardly believe that it has reason to ask for less. Iran has made clear for months that control of the strait is not negotiable. The notion, constantly floated by the Trump administration and the media, that the Iranians can’t make up their minds and that they keep shifting their positions is nonsense. As far back as April, Mojtaba declared that Iran would implement “new legal frameworks and management” of the strait and that “foreigners who come from thousands of kilometers away” had no place in the Gulf, “except at the bottom of its waters.” On a day commemorating Iran’s defeat of Portugal in the strait in 1622, he declared that “by the will and power of God, the bright future of the Persian Gulf region will be a future without America.”

The idea that, having survived both military strikes and sanctions, Iran will now succumb to sanctions alone is deluded. Mojtaba has overhauled Iran’s national-security leadership and installed veterans of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps in top positions. Former IRGC Commander Mohsen Rezaei now heads the Supreme National Security Council, and other hard-line security officials have been elevated. The regime emerging from the war is being reorganized by men who believe that Iran survived an American-Israeli attempt to destroy it and that defiance, not compromise, produced victory.

But this is not just triumphalism. For Iran, control of the strait is neither a luxury nor a bargaining chip. Given the likelihood that, at some future point, Israel or the United States will attack again, deal or no deal, the strait provides Iran’s strongest and surest deterrent. It is also the key to unlocking many of the shackles that a U.S.-dominated world order placed on Iran over the course of decades, and to undoing a regional and global economic system designed to exclude and impoverish it. Some so-called moderates within the Iranian establishment may well regard the strait as a bargaining chip, but they are not winning the argument, for reasons that are not hard to understand. An IRGC spokesperson stated the regime’s perspective plainly: “For us, the Strait of Hormuz is not merely an economic waterway, but rather a key component of our geopolitical and strategic power.”

Iran has been establishing the mechanisms of control for months and instructing shipowners and nations as to how the system will operate. It created the Persian Gulf Strait Authority and demanded that shipping companies register with it, obtain permits, follow Iran-designated routes, and pay for Iran-provided insurance and security.

Neither is Iran claiming merely the right to collect money. It is also claiming the right to decide who may pass. It has proposed excluding vessels associated with governments it deems hostile, such as the U.S. and Israel, and it has demanded compensation from nations that participated in attacks against it. Iran is establishing the principle that passage through the strait will henceforth be a privilege that Tehran can grant, condition, delay, or deny. Other nations have already begun acquiescing to the new arrangement. India, Pakistan, Iraq, Turkey, China, and others have sought to come to terms.

Iran’s control of the Strait of Hormuz is a more useful instrument of power than a nuclear weapon would be. A nuclear weapon might deter an attack, but control of the strait gives Iran leverage every day, over dozens of countries. With it, Iran can reward friends, punish enemies, extract revenue, force the loosening of sanctions, and make governments think twice before opposing Iranian policies. It can do all of this without firing a shot, and while preserving the ability to threaten violence when necessary.

The sanctions strategy that for decades served to isolate Iran rested on international cooperation. Nations that depend on access to the strait—Japan and South Korea, for example—will have to choose between access to a reliable energy supply and participation in American-backed sanctions. Will they choose solidarity with Washington—this Washington—over their economic interests? In the new order that Iran is creating, a global sanctions regime is likely to be unsustainable.

Iran will probably sustain its control over energy flows for at least a couple of years. The most optimistic projections suggest that establishing alternative pipelines and export routes that circumvent the Strait of Hormuz will take this long. But one wonders whether these will even come to fruition. New pipelines, ports, and energy facilities will be just as vulnerable to Iranian missiles and drones as the old ones were. Will Iran simply stand by while neighboring states construct the means to escape its control? The small states that must build and protect such routes are going to be wary of incurring Iran’s wrath, and American protection has proved inadequate. Iran is already trying to draw Iraq away from developing alternative oil-export corridors, in part by offering safe passage for Iraqi tankers on Iranian terms.

The regional adjustment has begun. Gulf rulers who once counted on an American security guarantee must now make their peace with the power that proved capable of defeating their protector. Saudi Arabia’s rejection of the U.S.- and Israel-backed Abraham Accords in favor of a strategic compact with Pakistan and Turkey hints at the shape of the future. The Abraham Accords represented the consolidation of Israeli power and influence in the region and the further containment of Iran. The Mecca pact is something else entirely. Neither Pakistan nor Turkey regards Iran as a major threat; Pakistan, like Iran, has close relations with China; Israel regards Turkey as a regional threat second only to Iran. Instead of an American-Israeli order, this will be an alignment less implacably hostile to Iran, less friendly to Israel, and more independent of the United States. The signatories also have nuclear weapons in common: Pakistan has them, and the Turks and Saudis want them.

Meanwhile, Iran will be free to resume its own nuclear-weapons program without fear of another major attack. This fact in itself will transform the regional balance. Last year, Israel and the United States bombed Iran’s nuclear facilities for 12 days without facing serious Iranian retaliation. Now Iran has been emboldened to use its control of the strait, and threats against neighboring states and their energy infrastructure, to deter any such strikes for the foreseeable future. American missile and interceptor stocks will take a long time to replenish, and may well be needed in other parts of the world in years to come; Iran will all the while be rearming with Russian and Chinese help, potentially leaving the U.S. in a worse position a year from now than it is in today.

The effects will extend far beyond the Middle East. American allies in Asia and Europe can see that a relatively brief war against a second-rank power has consumed much of America’s supply of the weapons needed for missile defense. They can see that the United States began a war it could not finish, exposed its partners to retaliation, and then stopped when the costs became too great. Beijing’s conclusions about Taiwan, and Moscow’s conclusions about Europe, may be impossible to know. But America’s allies will inevitably be asking whether Washington has the weapons, stamina, and political will to sustain another major conflict. In fact, they already are.

That the Iranian regime could fall is, of course, possible. It is corrupt, repressive, economically incompetent, and widely hated by its people. But it had all of these weaknesses before the war. What it did not have was control of the world’s most important energy choke point, the prospect of growing revenues and influence, the demonstrated ability to deter the United States, and the prestige of an impossible victory.

A regime that survived what much of the world believed would destroy it, and then forced the world’s dominant power to retreat, will likely emerge strengthened, at least for a time. Such success tends to breed confidence, and perhaps overconfidence. But it is not likely to make Iran’s leaders less ambitious or less committed to the faith that saw them through this miraculous triumph. Recently, senior Iranian officials began talking publicly about adopting a “fully offensive” posture, and reports have emerged of Iranian plans to expand its targets to include European countries hosting American bases. To see stories in The New York Times asking, “Who will blink first?” is amusing, given that the Iranians until now have never once blinked, while the Trump administration has done nothing but blink. Iran may even start fighting again to force the U.S. to lift the naval blockade. Given the American administration’s proven fear of escalation, the hope is not unreasonable. The world has spent decades worrying about what Iran might do if it acquired a nuclear weapon. It should now worry about something even more consequential: an Iran that has defeated the United States, no longer fears American attack, and controls access to the Persian Gulf. Nuclear weapons alone could never have given Iran this kind of power. The failed U.S. and Israeli war with Iran has.

The Hatred That Broke American Politics

“If you want to defend the South and Western civilization from the Jew and his dark-skinned allies, be at Charlottesville on 12 August.” Nine years ago, that message spread across the internet. “If you want to defend the South and Western civilization from the Jew and his dark-skinned allies, be at Charlottesville on 12 August.” Nine years ago, that message spread across the internet. The call was answered by several hundred white supremacists, who marched through the University of Virginia. The rage-filled men carried torches, chanting, “Jews will not replace us.” They swarmed around the historic rotunda, arriving at a statue of the school’s founder, Thomas Jefferson. There they met a tiny band of student counterprotesters who responded with their own chant: “Black lives matter.” That evening turned violent, the first act in a deadly weekend. Most Americans remember the torches, the rage, the death. But those clashing chants also revealed a gap between the targets of hatred and the words available to name it.

Race is the wound America sees. Anti-Semitism is the one it doesn’t. Jews have frequently been imagined as a colorless minority whose great persecution took place elsewhere, or as a group whose Christian-adjacent faith flourished with the religious freedom they found in the United States. But their in-betweenness has always been part of the danger: Jews have never fit neatly into America’s frameworks of identity, and that failure to fit has marked them as suspect, out of place, or impossible to categorize. Depending on the audience, Jews are dismissed as beneficiaries of white privilege or treated as not quite white at all—too assimilated and not assimilated enough, too powerful and too marginal. That ambiguity has placed them, again and again, at the center of America’s battles over free speech and equal protection. When hatred does not fit existing categories, we tend either to ignore it or to overcorrect—both responses that distort the boundaries of free expression.

Our failure to reckon with the anti-Semitism of Charlottesville—on the right and the left—was not merely a missed opportunity. It helped create the opening into which illiberalism has since rushed.

[David Frum: Anti-Semitism is becoming mainstream]

August 2017 marked a turning point, not just for racial populism but for American democracy. That crisis seeded a surge in anti-Semitism. It also helped produce a government campaign against such bigotry that has become a tool for undermining freedom, championed by the Trump administration only when it serves the White House’s purposes. Meanwhile, the progressive left has struggled to sustain its critique of Israel’s war with Palestine—and of American responsibility for a moral catastrophe—without letting that critique curdle into conspiracism.

Americans appear to face an impossible choice between protecting free speech and safeguarding equal rights. But that is a false dichotomy. Honoring both will, however, require de-exceptionalizing anti-Semitism. And that, in turn, necessitates returning to Charlottesville, and reckoning with how both the left and the right failed in its aftermath.

Over the course of 2017, three separate racist rallies took place in Charlottesville. In May, the alt-right provocateur Richard Spencer led a small rally in downtown Charlottesville to protest a city-council decision to remove two century-old Confederate monuments. A University of Virginia alum with a passion for Wagnerian opera, Spencer dressed old hatred in new clothes. He spoke grandly of restoring white supremacy, and of defeating liberal conspiracies to change America beyond recognition. President Trump’s election gave him cause for hope that Black Lives Matter and antifa would be crushed. “We will not be replaced from this world,” Spencer screamed as plumes of green and gray smoke from flares rose into the sky. “Whites have a future! We have a future of power!”

Two months later, the Ku Klux Klan paraded through town. Although they had come to protest the removal of a statue of Robert E. Lee, they held banners denouncing Jewish communism and the satanic Talmud. They were met with a massive, raucous counterprotest, inspired in part by a newly formed Black Lives Matter chapter in town, staged by citizens determined to oppose the hatred.

August brought round three: Spencer returned to town, accompanied by a who’s who of the far right, answering the call to join what they labeled the “Battle of Charlottesville.”

Officially, Unite the Right was a free-speech march in defense of the monument. But several organizers had ties to violent extremist groups and online they made their intentions clear. “Next stop: Charlottesville, VA. Final stop: Auschwitz,” wrote Andrew Anglin of The Daily Stormer. The planners fantasized about mowing down protesters with cars, and repeating the Holocaust—even as they denied it happened. They wrapped retrograde hatred in constitutional pretense. Free speech became both shield and sword.

The weekend brought mayhem: dozens of injuries, the murder of Heather Heyer, the death of two state troopers. Yet it also exposed the entanglement of race and religion in the racist imagination, which conceived of Jews as invisible power brokers, racial chameleons, white imposters, and anti-Christian infidels.

Charlottesville shocked the country. Yet in its aftermath, America had no vocabulary for describing anti-Semitism in relation to other hatreds—let alone for responding to it. Then–UVA President Teresa Sullivan denounced the violence and “racist, anti-immigrant, homophobic and misogynistic chants,” but made no mention of Jews. The great majority of other statements issued by Virginia-college presidents repeated the same failure. The local school superintendent discarded her back-to-school speech and instead spoke of the need to name “the hate that had visited our city,” which had targeted “our Latino, Muslim, bisexual, transgender, Christian, and Black students.” Jews, again, went unmentioned.

These educators were not anti-Semites. They were well-meaning liberals for whom other forms of hatred eclipsed anti-Semitism; anti-Black racism, for one, loomed larger. But anti-Semitism was hardly new to Charlottesville. The poet Karl Shapiro wrote of his time at UVA in the 1930s: “To hurt the Negro and avoid the Jew is the curriculum.” The dean once tracked applicants in three categories: “Virginians, non-Virginians, and Hebrews.”

And that was not true of just Virginia. Americans have long been accustomed to seeing Jews as a model minority whose success proved their belonging, and to dismissing anti-Jewish hatred as something that happened elsewhere. Without having reckoned with their nation’s own long history of anti-Semitism, Americans met it in 2017 with confusion and silence.

The centrist liberal response constituted a form of occlusion. White supremacy was understood strictly in racial terms, and anti-Semitism was blocked from view. The chants in Charlottesville fixated on Jews. The response did not.

What liberals failed to see, the populist right first denied, then exploited. Trump could not condemn the marchers without asserting there were “very fine people on both sides.” For two years, his administration shrugged off any serious response.

Then the strategy shifted. Trump seized on anti-Semitism—but only anti-Semitism from the left. A 2019 executive order focused on college campuses, targeting anti-Zionist expression while lifting anti-Semitism out of the broader landscape of hatred for special treatment. This marked the first moment in which Trump threatened to defund American universities without even a proper investigation. Anti-Semitism became a cudgel for the administration to wield against political enemies and liberal institutions, not a hatred to be fought on equal, consistent terms.

The same pattern played out in Virginia. Glenn Youngkin tried to ignore Charlottesville entirely while running for governor in 2021. On his first day in office, he issued several executive orders. One of them banned “inherently divisive concepts” such as critical race theory from classrooms in the name of free speech. Another announced a commission on anti-Semitism whose final report reduced Charlottesville to two paragraphs out of 13,000 words.

Many Republican politicians chose to attack civil-rights protections for most minorities while selectively championing them in the Jewish case. Genuine anti-Semitism was met with a sledgehammer instead of a scalpel, violating other rights in the process. UVA faced a wholesale attack as part of the wave of disproportionate punishments inflicted on institutions of higher education in the aftermath of October 7, 2023. But the institution’s vulnerability had deeper roots. The progressive left had its own failure to reckon with—one that played out on campus and left universities unable to articulate a coherent defense when the attacks came.

The progressive left failed by exclusion. After the weekend of terror in 2017, UVA’s Jewish Leadership Council worked with the Minority Rights Coalition on repair and renewal. Yet when they applied to join as a member organization, they were rejected on account of their pro-Israel affiliate group. Remove that Zionist organization, they were told, and they could join. After objections that Jews had been prime victims, a compromise was floated: Jewish students could join as an honorary minority group without voting rights. Jewish student leaders, among them a young woman who had fled fearing her life on August 11, denounced the moral myopia.

As this conflict escalated in spring 2018, pro-Palestinian protesters stormed a Hillel event featuring Israeli Druze and Jewish reserve soldiers. Shouting slogans through a megaphone, they denounced Zionists as fascists and refused “dialogue with racists.” Their heckler’s veto muddled their critique of Israeli military propaganda. Jewish students claimed the rhetoric threatened them; protesters said the soldiers’ presence did the same to Palestinian students.

Everyone claimed the mantle of free speech while denouncing the other side as perpetrators of hate speech. The progressive left, for all of its commitment to confronting hatred, rarely turned that lens inward. The question of where anti-Zionism ended and anti-Semitism began was dismissed, not deliberated.

That moment was a rehearsal for every debate we’ve lived through since. How do you balance free speech and equal rights when competing groups on campus have political and personal ties to a live conflict? Where does legitimate critique end and hatred begin? All of it was there, seven years ago, before October 7, before the campus protests of 2024. When war erupted again, Americans were unprepared. Each side had made its cause exceptional—anti-Semitism a unique hatred, Zionism a unique evil—and that rhetorical brinksmanship left no room for the harder work of ensuring uniform equality and pluralism for all. From silence to chaos, we found ourselves without a coherent language to combat hatred.

The past several years have illustrated an old Yiddish saying: Lekh mikh nisht, un lakh mikh nisht. Literally: “Don’t beat me, and don’t lick me.” But in effect: Don’t demonize us, but don’t fetishize us, either. Since Charlottesville, Jews have been both demonized and tokenized across the political spectrum. Building a way forward requires de-exceptionalizing anti-Semitism—not erasing its distinct features, but treating it as one hatred among others, deserving the same protections, no more and no less.

[Yair Rosenberg: The anti-Semitic revolution on the American right]

This wisdom extends beyond Jews. Every group deserves protection from hatred without becoming a political football. Every group deserves to be seen clearly—neither as villain nor as mascot. That includes Palestinian Americans, who have watched their own dehumanization dismissed while anti-Semitism is used against their speech.

This is why we need a new model—one that treats no group’s claim to protection as special, one that recognizes free speech and equal protection as complementary values enabling all citizens to participate fully in democratic life. Building that model will require Jewish communal leaders to resist the politicization of Jewish fear—and progressive coalitions to make room for Jewish particularity without imposing a litmus test on Jews alone.

What would this look like in practice? Rejecting the warping of anti-Semitism for partisan ends. Acknowledging anti-Semitism from all directions rather than only the versions that serve our politics. Building democratic coalitions across differences rather than demanding ideological purity as the price of admission.

Nine years after Charlottesville, we have seen the stumbles and overcorrections, the cynicism and despair. What we haven’t done is imagine a better future in which fighting anti-Semitism strengthens civil rights for all.

That work begins with honesty about how we got here. The bigots who marched in Charlottesville were real. So is our capacity to build something better. In 2017, two chants collided in the dark. We can still answer them together.

U.S.-Canada trade talks collapsed last night. Punitive Trump tariffs will now go into effect. Canada will reciprocate with retaliatory tariffs of its own.

The story may not be over. Donald Trump is notorious for issuing threats, then reversing them. Wars are on, off, on again, off again, then on a third time—but only as a “little detour.” This is not an administration that thinks even one move ahead. But without a supersized Trump TACO, the U.S.-Canada impasse likely won’t resolve soon.

The Trump administration has one big idea about Canada: The U.S. is larger, stronger, and richer than Canada, so Canada must sooner or later surrender to Trump’s demands. That one big idea is wrong.

Yes, Trump can hurt Canada more than Canada can hurt Trump. That part of Trump’s thinking is true. But wars are not decided only by the question Who can inflict more pain? Wars are also decided by the question Who can endure more pain? Trump’s failure to accept this truth is why he lost the Iran war—and why he is losing his trade wars.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has much more political permission to accept pain from a U.S.-Canadian trade war than Trump does.

To read current Canadian polling is to see a country united under its political leadership—and energized by its dislike and distrust of Trump’s United States. A majority of Canadians view Trump’s America as a more immediate threat to their security than Russia or China. Two-thirds favor the government taking a hard line in trade talks; fewer than one-quarter regard the United States as trustworthy. Meanwhile, Carney is backed by the Canadian public, polling at about 60 percent approval, a remarkable figure in a multiparty political system.

The Canadian economy has definitely suffered because of Trump’s hostility. Exports were down last year, and growth slowed. But this year, Canada got an unexpected bailout: Trump’s Iran war and inflation have boosted prices for Canadian oil, gas, minerals, and food products. Canadian incomes are rising again. Meanwhile, Canada’s low levels of public debt have kept inflation low. Whereas the U.S. government must pay 5.3 percent to borrow money for 30 years, Canada pays 4.2 percent.

By contrast, Trump is facing an omnishamble. “Trump’s Economic Challenge: $40tn Debt, 6.7% Mortgages and $5 Diesel.” That’s a headline in today’s Financial Times. The paper could have added one more: 33 percent job approval, and falling fast.

Trump’s tariffs are costing the typical American household $1,100 a year in both direct collections and indirectly in higher prices, according to the Yale Budget Lab. Although teasing out Canada-specific costs is tricky, one indicator is the price of aluminum. In 2024, about one-fourth of the aluminum consumed by Americans came from Canada, the U.S.’s single largest source of imported aluminum. Result: Whereas European and Japanese consumers now pay about $3,000 a ton for aluminum, Americans pay almost $5,000, driving up the cost of everything from a can of beer to the construction of a new hospital.

Although Canada’s economy is much smaller than the U.S.’s, Canada’s government has a more rational and intelligent leadership—and can therefore target its retaliation in ways that better serve national ends. U.S. alcohol exports to Canada have collapsed by 80 percent as Canadian provincial liquor stores ban U.S. wine and spirits. Canada’s booze boycott hurts more than one might expect given the comparatively small size of the Canadian market. Alcohol exporters to Canada prepare special labels for their products to meet Canadian legal requirements. More than 1 million bottles of wine intended for Canadian markets now slumber in U.S. warehouses, unsellable anywhere else without costly repackaging.

More than 30 percent of Ohio’s exports go to Canada, and almost 40 percent of Michigan’s. Both states will this year elect a U.S. senator and fill open governor’s seats. Not only is Trump putting at risk his party’s position in purple Michigan, but suddenly the races in beet-red Ohio look in jeopardy too. These states may turn control of the Senate—and Trump’s chances of facing meaningful accountability next year for his law-breaking and corruption.

Trump’s theory of his trade war, like his theory of his Iran war, is that the bigger bully always wins. Size counts for a lot, but not for everything.

As Trump has declared over and over again, his ultimate goal in this trade war is to add Canada as a 51st state. Why? To make the United States look bigger on the map. For that goal, which excites ultra-MAGA crackpots, Trump is hazarding not only America’s most important trading relationships but very possibly every other political equity he holds, including avoiding investigations and accountability by a Democratic House and Senate after January 2027.

Against Canada, Trump is fighting a trade war that very few Americans support, under leadership that most Americans reject, to achieve results that virtually all Americans would dismiss as pointless if not crazy.

Against Trump, Canadians are fighting a trade war that the great majority support, under leadership that commands broad assent, to defend their independence and self-respect. That’s a war that even the weaker side can win, especially if it needs to hold on only a few months longer to survive.
