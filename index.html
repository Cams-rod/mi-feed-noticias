Trump’s Antitrust Gambit May Backfire

In March, one of the most important antitrust cases in recent memory went to trial. The Department of Justice and 39 states plus the District of Columbia had sued the ticketing-and-entertainment giant Live Nation, alleging that its 2010 merger with Ticketmaster had created an effective monopoly on live shows. According to the suit, Live Nation controls 70 percent of what the government defined as “major concert venues” in the United States, and Ticketmaster handles 86 percent of concert ticketing at those venues. The DOJ accused the company of exploiting this monopoly by compelling venues to use Ticketmaster as their exclusive seller, jacking up prices, and saddling concertgoers with unavoidable junk fees. This certainly looked like an archetypal case of a company’s size leaving consumers worse off—precisely what our antitrust laws are designed to prevent.

This is what made it so disappointing when, a week after the trial began, DOJ announced that it had reached a settlement with Live Nation. Although the agreement mandated changes to some of the company’s practices, including granting access to other booking agents at some venues and limiting fees, the deal fell well short of the more aggressive remedies that many people had hoped for, including—most obvious—breaking up the company.

This odd turn of events makes sense only because we know now that the decision to settle was made not by anyone in DOJ’s Antitrust Division but by the president himself, according to a recent report in The Wall Street Journal. Just before the trial was set to begin, Donald Trump met with the CEO of Live Nation in the White House a couple of times and allegedly told a senior DOJ official, “Settle it.” In other words, the federal government abandoned one of its strongest antitrust lawsuits in years because Trump wanted it to.

[Quinta Jurecic: DOJ enters a new, even more aggressive phase]

This may then seem like yet another story about how Trump has wrecked norms and corrupted the rule of law. But in this case, there’s a twist. After the Department of Justice announced in March that it was settling with Live Nation, many observers expected that the states involved would drop the suit and sign on to the deal, as typically occurs when federal regulators reach a settlement. But in this case, 32 states—including Republican-led Florida, Tennessee, Texas, and Utah—and D.C. refused to settle. In April, they won their case: A jury found that Live Nation had illegally monopolized the live-events business and overcharged consumers. The judge is still determining the remedy, but it will almost certainly be tougher than the deal Live Nation got from DOJ.

This will not be a one-off. A peculiarity of federal antitrust law is that state attorneys general can bring lawsuits on behalf of their citizens for violations of the Sherman Antitrust Act, and many states also have their own antitrust laws on the books. This gives them a great deal of leeway to act on their own. And because of the Trump administration’s attitude toward antitrust laws, what we saw in the Live Nation case—states refusing to defer to the feds, and instead pursuing litigation—will become more common.

This is because Trump, despite having campaigned as a populist who would fight to lower prices, has presided over an administration that’s been almost completely deferential to the interests of businesses looking to get bigger. Since his return to office, the Department of Justice has filed exactly one lawsuit to block a merger—the $14 billion acquisition of Juniper Networks by the Hewlett Packard Enterprise Company—only to quickly reach a settlement. Stanley Woodward, a top DOJ official who oversees antitrust enforcement, appears to be an active opponent of antitrust litigation. Bloomberg reports that he’s wondered aloud in meetings why DOJ can’t simply stop reviewing deals. That’s not exactly the guy you want in charge of preventing monopolies.

As the Live Nation case shows, even when a lawsuit does move forward, CEOs can simply go to Trump to get his support. After describing the settlement as “shocking” to lawmakers on Capitol Hill in May, Roger Alford, a Notre Dame law professor and former Antitrust Division senior official in Trump’s first term, observed that Trump’s interference in the case both undermined norms and set a new precedent for behind-the-scenes advocacy: “The rule of law quickly becomes the rule of lobbyists.”

[Read: Even when Ticketmaster works, it doesn’t]

Presidents historically avoided interfering directly with antitrust cases. The exceptions were few and notoriously corrupt (such as Richard Nixon’s meddling in an antitrust lawsuit against the International Telephone & Telegraph Corporation in exchange for a financial donation to the Republican National Convention). But Trump views the entire executive branch as an extension of his will, and he has turned regulatory agencies and DOJ into tools he can use to dole out favors, reward friends, and punish enemies. The only companies that have to worry about a federal antitrust prosecution at this point are those that Trump fiercely dislikes.

But as the Live Nation case demonstrates, any sighs of relief among leaders of America’s oversize businesses would be premature. In an ideal world, antitrust regulations wouldn’t be fragmented and enforced by a panoply of states. But when the president effectively turns national antitrust laws into dead letters, the virtues of federalism become apparent: States will do what DOJ fails to do. This arrangement also makes it harder for companies to simply lobby their way to good deals across multiple states.

The paradox is that by trying to hand out sweetheart arrangements to companies that are either trying to merge or flexing their monopoly power, Trump has made it more likely that those companies will have to navigate a gantlet of state lawsuits. And in asserting total control over the federal government, Trump has ended up making state governments much more aggressive and influential than they were before.

The Vicious Cycle Trump May Restart

By the time he retired from the Marine Corps, in 1931, Major General Smedley Butler had served in Mexico, Haiti, Cuba, the Philippines, Nicaragua, the Dominican Republic, Honduras, and China. It was the golden age of American imperialism, and Butler was one of its golden boys, among the most decorated fighters in U.S. history.

But over time, Butler began to have doubts about what he was doing. “I spent 33 years and 4 months in active service,” he wrote in 1935. “During that period I spent most of my time being a high-class muscle man for Big Business, for Wall Street and for the bankers. In short, I was a racketeer; a gangster for capitalism.” The costs of these excursions, he wrote, were paid in taxpayer money and soldiers’ lives. The wars that Butler fought also left scars around the world, as the journalist Jonathan M. Katz wrote in a 2022 biography. Little remembered in the United States, these interventions were formative for the populations they affected, fueling enduring anti-Americanism.

The Trump administration seems determined to re-create the cycle in Venezuela today: military action, puppet government, exploitation of oil reserves, and almost certainly more resentment, fueling further conflict in the future. In January, U.S. troops descended on Venezuela and seized President Nicolás Maduro. Although President Trump offered federal drug-trafficking charges against Maduro as window dressing, he made no secret of the real reason for the incursion: He wanted to seize the country’s huge oil reserves. This week, the president claimed that he would refill the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve with Venezuelan oil, “a Gift from Venezuela to the People of the United States.”

The Trump administration has, as usual, offered few concrete details, but as my colleague Jonathan Chait writes, this is less a “deal” than “a payment by Venezuela of tribute at gunpoint.” The Venezuelan people are in no position to consent to handing over the country’s most valuable natural resource to a richer regional superpower for free, because Venezuela has not had fair elections in years. After deposing Maduro, the United States did not clear away his government or try to install the pro-democracy dissident (and Nobel Peace Prize laureate) María Corina Machado; in fact, it has created barriers to her return. Instead, it has enlisted a former Maduro lieutenant as president, while Secretary of State Marco Rubio oversees her as a sort of viceroy.

Besides, nothing in international relations is as simple as a gift. The current arrangement involves Alejandro Betancourt López, a Venezuelan oil oligarch who has been under investigation in several countries. Betancourt managed to maintain connections with both Maduro’s regime and the U.S. government, and to dodge some charges thanks to help from Rudy Giuliani. Chevron is expanding its footprint in Venezuela, although other oil companies have been reluctant. Investors from other sectors, including crypto, are also getting involved.

Although much money stands to be made, whether and how much ordinary Americans will actually benefit is less clear. Rebuilding Venezuelan production will take years, and the country’s oil is heavy and difficult to refine. That means no near-term relief at gas pumps and years before the U.S. reserve is refilled.

Venezuelan oil and U.S. interests have a long and rocky history. American oil companies invested heavily in the country after World War II, but in 1976, the Venezuelan government nationalized the oil industry. In the early ’90s, the government once again opened to private companies. But Hugo Chávez, who portrayed himself as an anti-colonial hero and liked to use “Gringos go home!” as a slogan, became president in 1999. In 2007, he expropriated foreign assets once more, in what his energy minister called “an act of sovereignty for our country, for our people.” Trump cited the expropriation this year as another reason for U.S. intervention.

Chávez’s approach, maintained under his protégé Maduro, failed. The oil industry, along with the rest of the national economy, has atrophied. In an article last year, my colleague Gisela Salim-Peyer eloquently recounted how Venezuela slid into authoritarianism under Chávez and Maduro. International observers described the 2018 presidential election as rigged, and evidence gathered by Machado convincingly showed that her party had won the 2025 race. A huge number of people fleeing Venezuela have flocked to the United States.

For now, under the rump Chávez-Maduro regime, Venezuelans don’t have much say, but guessing how this might play out doesn’t require a great deal of creativity—just a working knowledge of history. Trump’s expropriation of Venezuelan oil won’t be well received by most people, nor will a Trump-driven gold rush by American businesses, no matter how much Venezuela needs assistance standing up its oil industry. Railing against the deal and foreign oil interests will be an easy populist stance for future Venezuelan politicians; perhaps one will even feel moved to nationalize assets once more—and so the cycle can begin again.

You don’t have to be an anti-colonial peacenik to worry about the risks of Trump’s oil raid. You can just grasp that having hostile demagogues in power in the hemisphere is bad for U.S. interests, driving global instability as well as the kind of mass migration that Trump has described as a crisis.

Such downsides help explain why anti-interventionism had become popular among Republicans over the past decade. Until the Venezuela raid, Trump portrayed himself as “America first,” but the foremost voice was J. D. Vance, who cited his experience deployed in Iraq in his speech accepting the GOP nomination for vice president. “As always, America’s ruling class wrote the checks. Communities like mine paid the price,” he said, sounding much like his fellow Marine Smedley Butler. “For decades, that divide between the few, with their power and comfort in Washington, and the rest of us only widened.” Yet now the Trump administration has embarked on just the sort of adventure Butler led and then decried. Wealthy Americans will benefit today, ordinary Americans will see little immediate reward, and the United States will face the blowback for decades to come.

I felt a little of that shock when I learned of the death of the singer Cassandra Wilson this week. She was just 70. To my ear, Wilson was also the greatest blues singer of our era. Her rendition of “Death Letter” is as haunting, visceral, and deep as any recording in the genre’s long history.

How the Early Dreams of the Internet Changed

This is an edition of Time-Travel Thursdays, a journey through The Atlantic’s archives to contextualize the present. Sign up here.

During the 1984 Super Bowl, an advertisement launched a technological revolution. If you were alive, you probably know the one. Directed by Ridley Scott, the minute-long spot showed men in gray uniforms, heads shaved, eyes blank, marching in unison toward a lifeless auditorium. A Big Brother figure speaks from a giant screen, droning on about “the first glorious anniversary of the Information Purification Directives.” All the while, a woman in red shorts and a white tank top is running to the auditorium. She looks like she’s just come from an aerobics class, but she has a sledgehammer, and authorities are in pursuit. Entering the room, she hurls the sledgehammer into Big Brother’s face. There’s a flash of light, and a wind blows over the stunned, awakened audience. A different voice—a comforting one—announces, “On January 24th, Apple Computer will introduce Macintosh. And you’ll see why 1984 won’t be like 1984.”

Although the ad did not show an actual Macintosh computer, it worked. Apple went on to sell 250,000 of its user-friendly, mouse-driven little machines in that year alone. The ad also asserted what was to be a defining idea of the emerging digital age: that personal computers, far from massive instruments of centralized control, were vessels of individual freedom. The coming of the internet, with its invitation to universally accessible knowledge and the free exchange of ideas, seemed to affirm the promise: Our machines wouldn’t enslave us; they would liberate us.

The dystopia depicted in the ad came by way of George Orwell’s imagination, but the fears underlying it had deep American roots. As mass consumer culture expanded in the early 20th century, American artists, intellectuals, and critics worried over the problem of “conformity” and the loss of individualism that came with it. Most didn’t see the dark totalitarianism of 1984; instead, they saw a world of ready-made desires, a soulless existence brought to you by corporations, advertisers, media, and politicians. As The Atlantic’s review of Sinclair Lewis’s Babbitt put it in 1922, “the ‘standardized American’” was becoming “as much a national product as the Ford car”—a person like Babbitt himself, whose entire life and very personality were shaped by forces beyond him. Such criticism formed a fault line that ran from the cultural commentary of the ’20s to the outbursts of Beats, folkies, and academics in the ’50s.

The earthquake, of course, would come in the counterculture of the 1960s. No longer would people need to be stuffed into “Little Boxes,” as the folk singer Malvina Reynolds worried early in the decade. The counterculture promised liberation from such confinement. Visiting San Francisco’s Haight-Ashbury district for The Atlantic in 1967, the novelist Mark Harris wondered whether he was seeing Babbitt’s descendants finally rebelling against conformity in ways that Babbitt himself could not. The counterculture’s sensibilities migrated into the mainstream, where popular culture and advertising absorbed its language of liberation and transgression, so that admen could pitch 7 Up using hippie iconography or Virginia Slims cigarettes by way of the women’s-liberation movement.

Digital culture would prove especially receptive to these countercultural ideals. Northern California, after all, was the center of both the counterculture and the tech industry, and the cross-pollination between the two was considerable, as the historian Fred Turner has shown. By the 1990s, as the internet was becoming a mass medium, some of its first boosters (many of whom had roots in the counterculture) were presenting it as the realization of countercultural ideals. John Perry Barlow, a poet and sometimes Grateful Dead lyricist, wrote his 1996 “Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace” in response to an early effort to regulate the internet. It reads like a Port Huron Statement for digital culture, describing a realm of individual freedom and free-flowing ideas that would break from existing forms of cultural and institutional authority. According to Barlow, a new “civilization of the Mind” was coming.

In the dot-com and digital revolutions that followed, many of the people who sold the hardware, coded the software, and built the platforms were seen as iconoclasts themselves. Digital entrepreneurship became a form of transgression, not just in the casual dress and informal culture of the Silicon Valley office but also in what it promised to users. Tech visionaries pledged to create a world that would make possible precisely the kind of self-expression, open exchange, and communal connection that the counterculture had promised. It was no coincidence that by the late 1990s, Apple’s advertising—again with almost no reference to the machines themselves—simply pictured groundbreaking figures such as Gandhi, Martin Luther King Jr., Bob Dylan, and John Lennon, alongside the slogan “Think Different.”

In many ways, those promises were fulfilled. The internet did splinter 20th-century mass culture, and the effects have been every bit as revolutionary as promised. We don’t all watch the same networks anymore; we don’t see the same movies; we don’t get our news from the same places or read the same books (mostly, we just don’t read); we “do our own research.”

Even as the conditions of mass culture and conformity that shaped the counterculture and the early dreams of the internet have faded, we continue to live in their shadow. In the fractured age of social media and the unknowable algorithm, our devices give us the feeling of freedom, even as they serve the interests of corporate forces that track our movements, catalog our interests, and profile our wants. The oppressive monoculture has been replaced by countless minicultures, each with their own devoted acolytes; we still inhabit little boxes, partially of our own making.

The transgressive ethos, meanwhile, continues to thrive. Subversion is perhaps the dominant language of digital culture, spoken across the political spectrum by podcasters “just asking questions,” wellness gurus swilling at-home health hacks, manosphere kingpins lashing out against wokeness, and lefty influencers denouncing the billionaire class. Everyone, it seems, is hurling a sledgehammer at something.

Like late-summer humidity, Hasan Piker hangs heavy over the discourse.

At a rally on Monday, Vice President Vance brought up the hard-left streamer in an attempt to paint the Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed as anti-American. Last week, House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries called Piker’s recent statements about Jewish Americans  “dangerous and anti-Semitic.” Senators Jon Ossoff, Cory Booker, and Jacky Rosen said much the same. Facing questions about a totally different set of Piker comments, Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez hustled into an elevator.

Some Democrats are trying to dismiss the 35-year-old political commentator as irrelevant. He’s just “a streamer in California,” El-Sayed said on Saturday, waving off questions from reporters. Piker himself has spent the past week casting himself as the victim of hysteria. “I have become this media cycle’s ‘trans athletes’ or ‘migrant caravan,’” he posted on X.

But it was El-Sayed who first made Piker central to his campaign by bringing him out on the trail, partying poolside with him at an influencer event, and celebrating with him during his Election Night rally. In his thousands of hours of streaming, Piker has produced a dense catalog of comments spanning the spectrum from stupid to incredibly offensive, including several iterations of the phrase “I hate America” and, perhaps most memorably, that “America deserved 9/11.” (He quickly attempted to walk it back.) Piker also flaunts his authoritarian sympathies, calling Mao Zedong “one of the great leaders of this world” and counting the collapse of the Soviet Union among the “catastrophes” of the 20th century. Last week, he warned that “eventually someone is going to come around and take action” against American Jews who are “singularly invested in Israel.”  

[Read: What El-Sayed does next matters ]

Hasan Piker is part of the conversation now. He has become central to the Republican strategy in Michigan, where El-Sayed will continue to be asked about him. So, too, will Democrats across the country. The question ahead of November is whether their answers will satisfy voters.

El-Sayed has moved away from Piker since winning the primary last month. The streamer has not been on the trail in Michigan. “I have spoken with the good doctor,” Representative Jim Clyburn told reporters of El-Sayed, who has a medical degree. “He has apologized. He says he will not campaign with Piker anymore.” After Piker’s comments last week about American Jews, El-Sayed said in a statement that anti-Semitism is a “scourge” and that “I stand against any rhetoric that puts the community at risk.” When asked about Piker at a meeting with a group of Jewish Democrats on Saturday, though, El-Sayed declined to disavow him. “If you want to make this race about a streamer in California, I just think you’re missing the boat.”

Anyone could have predicted the Piker blowback. Plenty of people did. In March, one of El-Sayed’s primary opponents, Mallory McMorrow, compared Piker to the white-supremacist influencer Nick Fuentes, and leaders of the centrist think tank Third Way wrote a column urging Democrats not to associate with Piker. “Everyone saw it coming from a mile away, so they should’ve been better prepared to handle it,” one Democratic strategist told me. Even some of El-Sayed’s own backers felt similarly. “Not all socialism is toxic to the Democratic brand, but Piker certainly is,” one progressive strategist told me.

(None of the Democrat-aligned operatives I reached out to would agree to speak on the record for this story, citing their support for El-Sayed as well as their fear of reprisal from Piker’s rabid online fans.)

The Piker drama is a ribbon-wrapped gift to Republicans in a year when so much else—inflation, high gas prices, an unpopular president—is working against them. They seem well aware of this. Mike Rogers, El-Sayed’s Republican opponent, refers to Piker as El-Sayed’s “running mate.” “When I see Abdul El-Sayed campaign around this state with somebody who said that America deserved 9/11, what I see is a man who has sympathy and compassion for people who share his religion, but has nothing for people who don’t,” Vance said at the rally on Monday. “What I’d ask you to remember is that whoever you send to Washington, whatever policy views you have or you don’t have, send people of good heart who focus on the right issues, who fight for the right people, and who love the United States of America.” The strategy is obvious: Identify every repellent thing Piker has ever said, tie Piker to El-Sayed, and advertise the relationship at every opportunity.

There is more than a hint of irony in this situation. President Trump has shown a strong affinity for shock jocks and trolls. In 2020, he awarded Rush Limbaugh, who had a recurring segment celebrating the deaths of gay people from AIDS, the Presidential Medal of Freedom. Laura Loomer, a far-right activist and an ally of Trump’s, last week referred to a Senate candidate as a “ghetto black communist bitch.” Some Democrats, seeing Trump suffer little political consequence for such associations, believe it’s unfair that El-Sayed should have to answer for Piker’s actions.

[Read: Laura Loomer is a warning]

But politics, like life, is famously unfair. Democrats are constantly being asked to condemn Piker in cable-news hits and hallway interviews, even outside Michigan. Several Jewish Democrats have weighed in too. Through a spokesperson, Ossoff said he “unreservedly rejects reckless and dangerous rhetoric suggesting that policy advocacy invites violence against the Jewish community or any community.” Representative Josh Gottheimer of New Jersey said that “anyone who campaigns with Piker or elevates him is complicit in the rising antisemitism and anti-American hatred in this country.” Moderate Democrats are seizing the moment as a chance to engage in a little centrist posturing. Senator John Fetterman went on Fox News and delivered a challenge to El-Sayed: “I’m offering $50,000 for you to do a rally with Hasan Piker” on September 11, he said. Piker was not immediately available for comment for this story. But last week, after several Jewish Democrats criticized him, he dismissed their concerns by implying they reflected an orchestrated conspiracy against him, writing on X that an “aipac boss call was made.”

Maybe El-Sayed could quell some of the frenzy himself. In 2008, controversial comments from Barack Obama’s former pastor Jeremiah Wright threatened to upend Obama’s bid for the White House. In a sermon shortly after September 11, the pastor said that “America’s chickens are coming home to roost.” Later, Wright said, “No, no, no, not God Bless America. God damn America—that’s in the Bible—for killing innocent people.”

Obama chose to address the controversy directly, and delivered a speech condemning Wright’s remarks while offering context about America’s racial tensions. That speech “was a masterpiece of empathy, and at the time it felt that it changed the trajectory of his campaign,” Michelle Goldberg wrote this week. At the very least, some Democrats argue, El-Sayed could more plainly denounce his friend. “Say, ‘Here’s what I condemn; here’s what I disavow. I disagree; it was wrong and hurtful,’” the progressive strategist said. “If Piker says something new that’s bad—which, if I was a betting man, I’d say will happen—you come out against it swiftly.”

Michigan, a state that Trump won in 2016 and 2024, was never going to be an easy win for El-Sayed. His relationship with Piker, who is paid to provoke, not persuade, isn’t going to make that task any simpler. The good news for Democrats is that most people don’t actually know Piker; polling from earlier this year showed that 60 percent of likely voters have never heard of him. The bad news for Democrats is that Republicans will spend the next two months working diligently to change that.

We’ll start this conference looking back on 2026 Washington Architecture by being honest: We have no idea what happened here. The snapshot of this planet that my comrades and I managed to obtain on our brief visit left us, if anything, more confused about Earth than we had been before. What follows are theories only.

All we know of 21st-century human political figure Donald J. Trump is what we can guess from the architectural record and a few bits of digital evidence. (Due to a misunderstanding, we ate most of the written records of this planet before figuring out that they contained information.) Was he a builder or a destroyer? There are also ruins of an arch—commemorating a triumph, but over what? Perhaps the successful defeat of 250 years of democracy, a system of rule that he despised?

Did he have access to the internet (a human technology that seems to have been primarily used for the sharing of pornography and misinformation)? Impossible to say. Some records suggest that a female of his species accessed the internet on his behalf. We don’t know his exact relation to this female, but we can observe that she was swift of foot and skilled at concealment in small enclosures, probably a trait admired by others. Were such relationships common? Did many human leaders consider it an honor to be chased by another human carrying a printer? (Did George Washington have a “Human Printer” of his own? Perhaps Benjamin Franklin fulfilled this role?)

Although other human figures enjoyed construction, Trump seemed to prefer the wrecking ball (what we would refer to as Grogon’s Tail) as a mode of expression. From a forensic analysis of these structures, we know that the Smashing of the Residence was first to take place, followed by the Desecration of the Pool.

Donald Trump seems to have smashed the Residence deliberately, as a warning to rivals. As his nestlings were at that time all mature and dwelling independently, such a gesture posed no danger to his young. Some papers we found suggested that there were plans to put something in the enormous hole, but these drawings are on an unlikely scale, and their suggested purpose is inconsistent. Ballroom? Bunker? No one can seem to agree, not even the plans themselves.

The situation of the pool is puzzling. We may never know why Donald Trump hated the pool (pond?) so much, but his action to strip it of its water, paint over its interior with a humiliating sealant, and fill it with a thick green algae speaks of immense disrespect. Had the pool or pond offended him? We know from the many banners he left bearing his visage around the city that he seems to have been incapable of smiling; a golden idol located in Florida represents him as having a slightly melted appearance. Perhaps glimpsing his reflection in the pond reminded him unpleasantly of these facts? Or were its ducks hostile to him? Some records show that even when the pool was drained of water, it was patrolled by an armed guard; what danger lurked in its shallows?

The structure with the giant head in it puzzles us most. We think this site on the Potomac may once have been used for entertainment and functioned as a kind of temple to the performing arts. Others suggest that it was an oversize dwelling place for mice. Some scholars believe that people really went inside the building and sat in red chairs while someone stood and bellowed in Italian. Perhaps this hall was used for punishment? Were wrongdoers subjected to the “tuba”?

Letters found near the site marking it “The Donald J. Trump and the John F. Kennedy Memorial Center for the Performing Arts” suggest that the enormous bronze head may represent either Donald J. Trump or John F. Kennedy, in whose joint memory the center seems to have been erected. Who were these men? They must have loved the arts. Perhaps an American Harmodius and Aristogeiton? Curiously, we do not see them commemorated jointly in other locations.

Some scholars argue that Donald J. Trump was in fact alive at the time of the center’s commemoration, but other scholars reply that this claim is ludicrous—where else is there evidence of a Memorial Center constructed while its subject was still living? The same theory suggests that he added his name to the center decades after its construction, halted all the shows, removed the public artworks, and threatened to demolish it if his name was not kept on, but other scholars reply that smashing things unless you can write your name on them is hardly the behavior of a full-fledged adult. We truly don’t know what to make of this place. (Just for the record, though, we didn’t do the pyramids.)
