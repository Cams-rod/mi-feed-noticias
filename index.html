If you want a snapshot of how the right has changed over the past decade, here’s one data point: MAGA activists think Sarah Huckabee Sanders is too woke now.

That’s the same Sarah Huckabee Sanders who is the daughter of the GOP fixture Mike Huckabee and a former White House press secretary for President Trump. Now governor of Arkansas, she’s facing criticism from right-wing influencers over an ad boosting the state’s imposition of work requirements for recipients of the state’s Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act. These requirements are a long-standing Republican priority, but my colleague Annie Lowrey wrote last year that they are “a terrible idea”: The requirement creates new taxpayer costs and red tape without really solving any problem, because most Medicaid recipients who are physically able to work already do.

That’s not why the activists are mad, though. They’re upset because the face of loafing in Sanders’s ad is a cheese-ball-snarfing couch potato named Brad who is a young white man. The “Pizzagate” influencer Jack Posobiec labels it “Antiwhite.” Sean Davis of The Federalist calls it part of decades-long, systematic discrimination against white men. The openly bigoted investor James Fishback, fresh off getting trounced in a GOP primary for governor of Florida, traveled to Little Rock this week to rail against the “false and disgraceful ad.”

Assessing the accuracy of a fictional character in an ad feels a little absurd, but for the record: More than 60 percent of Medicaid-expansion enrollees in Arkansas are white, and 18 percent are Black, according to The New York Times. (The state overall is about 75 percent white and 15 percent Black.) A little more than half are women. Sanders rebuffed criticism, writing, “Next thing you know they’ll say I should have used cookies instead of cheese balls.”

The immediate and obsessive focus on a throwaway character in a 30-second ad is a lurid reminder of how identitarianism has become influential in segments of the American right, particularly young voters. This is a matter of how members of the movement understand politics, as well as how they attempt to communicate to the American public.

For decades, the right—as well as some centrist Democrats, such as Bill Clinton—criticized progressives for embracing identity politics. They argued that a focus on demographic segments was divisive and unhelpful. Ronald Reagan preached that “a rising tide lifts all boats” (though in practice this often meant support for trickle-down policies that helped mostly the already-thriving) and called for “color-blind” policies (which also tended to result in worse outcomes for people of color). This impulse persists in some segments of the Republican Party, most notably in the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court, led by the former Reagan aide John Roberts.

This didn’t mean that people on the right hesitated to use race as a political wedge. They just did it elliptically. The avatar of this strategy was Lee Atwater, an adviser to Reagan and George H. W. Bush, whose 1981 explanation was as vulgar as the impulses he courted. “You start out in 1954 by saying, ‘Nigger, nigger, nigger,’” he explained. “By 1968 you can’t say ‘nigger’—that hurts you, backfires. So you say stuff like, uh, ‘forced busing,’ ‘states’ rights,’ and all that stuff, and you’re getting so abstract. Now you’re talking about cutting taxes, and all these things you’re talking about are totally economic things and a by-product of them is, Blacks get hurt worse than whites.”

In 1980, for example, Reagan delivered a controversial speech at the Neshoba County Fair in Mississippi, not far from where the civil-rights workers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner were murdered in 1964, and spoke about “states’ rights,” a phrase that segregationists had adopted. In 1988, a pro-Bush group aired a commercial that attacked his Democratic opponent, Michael Dukakis, for allowing a convicted criminal named Wlliam Horton furlough from prison, during which he nearly killed someone. The ad didn’t mention Horton’s race and didn’t need to; it included his mugshot and called him “Willie,” which Horton (among others) interpreted as a dog whistle. “It was created to play on racial stereotypes: big, ugly, dumb, violent, Black—‘Willie,’” he said later.

And, perhaps most relevant to the “Brad” brouhaha, Reagan often spoke about someone who became known as the “welfare queen.” As the journalist Josh Levin has written, this referred to a real individual, a woman in Chicago named Linda Taylor. Reagan never talked about her race, yet “welfare queen” became entrenched as a trope of lazy Black women grifting off the welfare system.

This may explain why people such as Posobiec and Fishback are so agitated about the Arkansas ad: They understand how powerful a political weapon racial associations can be in shaping policy. But they don’t have Atwater’s finesse or plausible deniability. The right has gone from insisting that its rhetoric about entitlements was color-blind to demanding that it include racial signifiers. And though the number of Americans willing to tolerate or embrace gutter racism in politics appears to be growing, it remains a marginal group. The hardest-core activists, hyping one another up in group chats and on Discord servers, may have lost sight of that, failing to recognize how bizarre they look freaking out over a forgettable advertisement.

If these identitarians looked around, they could see cautionary tales about the wholesale embrace of identity politics, such as the impression among many voters that the Democratic Party is more interested in niche groups than in ordinary people. They could learn from the first wave of the MAGA movement, which was willing to accommodate racism and bigotry for electoral gain but apparently believed that they could be contained and channeled. The commentator Erick Erickson, for example, has spent years tolerating Trumpism but now reacts with horror at neo-Nazis in the movement. Or they could consider the plight of Sarah Huckabee Sanders, who defended Trump’s every move from the White House lectern and now finds herself accused of treachery by his zealous followers.

The Kennedy Center Honors was once one of the biggest nights in Washington, which may not be saying much in a city that’s more blue blazer than red carpet. Still, it was where D.C.’s buttoned-up power class got to mingle with Hollywood stars at an awards show that was broadcast to millions. Today the main building is closed and the organization has largely stopped presenting the arts. But once again this December, the Kennedy Center will put on a show.

Officials announced today that the 49th Kennedy Center Honors will take place December 7 at Capital One Arena, the downtown stadium where Washington’s NBA and NHL teams play. At that point, the Kennedy Center will likely still be mired in a dispute in which its self-appointed chairman—Donald Trump—has said it might be demolished unless his name is added to the facade. Because of that drama, the Honors are poised to serve as something of a barometer of the institution.

Under Trump, donors, artists, and audiences have fled from the center. And the most prominent tenants are discovering that they may function better away from it. Unable to perform at the Kennedy Center, the National Symphony Orchestra opened its new season to a grateful crowd in the Maryland suburbs last weekend and will spend the season seeking new audiences at six venues in the region. The Washington National Opera reported record fundraising hauls after ending its affiliation with the Kennedy Center earlier this year. But the Honors may prove harder to disentangle, because the show’s identity is inseparable from the Kennedy Center—and the Kennedy Center, in turn, has become inseparable from Trump.

Trump’s issues with the ceremony go back to 2017, when several of the honorees spurned him. But last year, Trump made himself central to the event by selecting the awards recipients and becoming the first president to host the ceremony—at which he celebrated both the artists and himself. How involved he will be this time remains unclear; the White House did not respond to my questions about his plans. Also unclear is whether the Kennedy Center still has the cultural cachet to attract the level of talent it historically has drawn for the Honors. I posed that question to five people—former officials, staffers, and producers—who helped plan the Honors over the years, all of whom spoke with me on the condition of anonymity out of fear of professional repercussions. No matter what, they told me, it’s Trump’s show now, for better or worse.

“It has gone from being the Kennedy Center to being about Donald Trump,” one person told me. “So in order to accept the Honor today, you have to be aligned with what he is doing.”

Ordinarily, most of the details for the Kennedy Center Honors would have been firmed up by now—especially the recipients. Past organizers told me the ceremony required preparation year-round, starting even before the current year’s event raised its curtains. The selection process would begin in winter, and honorees were announced by the summer. But as October begins, the Kennedy Center has only now disclosed where and when the ceremony will occur.

Last year, Trump said he handpicked the 2025 recipients: Sylvester Stallone, Gloria Gaynor, George Strait, Michael Crawford, and the rock band Kiss. Although the ceremony drew some Nashville heavyweights to honor Strait, the rest of the celebrity crowd was lighter on star power, and the biggest group of red-carpet walkers seemed to be Cabinet officials and their spouses. And this was before the biggest shock wave hit the institution: 12 days after the Honors, Trump’s name was installed on the building’s facade, which led to a new round of artists canceling appearances; later, Trump announced that he planned to close the institution for a renovation. A legal fight over the future of the center is still playing out in court.

Speculating about who might be a realistic choice to accept a Kennedy Center Honor this year comes down to a relatively narrow set of criteria: someone accomplished, available, acceptable to Trump, and willing to receive the award from him. Maybe Jon Voight, whom the president honored with the National Medal of Arts in 2019 and recently named a “special ambassador” to Hollywood? Another such ambassador, and another plausible candidate, is Mel Gibson. Would the Honors turn to hip-hop stars such as Lil Wayne, whom Trump pardoned in 2021, or Nicki Minaj, who’s made a MAGA pivot in the past year? Either would be younger than the typical honoree, though their participation might boost ratings, which former producers tell me has always been a consideration in the selection process. Last year’s Honors, the first planned under Trump, saw ratings fall by about 25 percent from the previous year.

Trump’s celebrity orbit offers plenty more options for a shortlist (last year he said he made his final picks from a list of about 50 names): The tenor Andrea Bocelli, who met with Trump in the Oval Office last year, wouldn’t feel out of place on an Honors roster. Kid Rock might, but he’s a longtime friend and supporter of the president. (Ditto Vanilla Ice?) Or maybe the Village People, whose “YMCA” became such a standard of Trump rallies that the group performed at several events for the president’s second inauguration.

The Honors have also lost much of the infrastructure behind the event. CBS’s deal to broadcast the show expired after the 2025 ceremony, and the network did not respond to my request for comment about this year. A Kennedy Center spokesperson told me that it would announce its broadcast partner later. Still, producing the Honors requires more than what viewers eventually see on television. Former officials described it to me as an all-campus effort, relying on staffers across multiple departments, including fundraising and special events. Many of those employees have left or been laid off ahead of the closure.

“Uncertainty is the enemy of what’s going on here,” Senator Shelley Moore Capito, an ex officio member of the Kennedy Center board, told me during an unrelated event this week hosted by Americans for the Arts. The West Virginia Republican wants to see the Honors return to the Kennedy Center eventually, but suggested that temporarily moving the ceremony elsewhere doesn’t need to be fatal. “Take one year and do it off campus and come back,” she said.

At the same event, Kara Young, a two-time Tony Award–winning actress, told me the turmoil at the Kennedy Center has made celebrating art more necessary. “The truth is being silenced, and to me, art is truth,” she said. She added that if administration is “taking over places that are historically apolitical and historically for the arts, then people will find a way to seek what they need, which is the truth.”

The Honors have proved adaptable before. During the pandemic, producers abandoned the typically packed Opera House to film segments across the Kennedy Center campus. One former producer believes the show could still work if organizers found worthy honorees, great performances, and an event free of political influence. But moving it to a cavernous sports arena is a whole other proposition—suggesting that even if the organizers have a spectacle in mind, they risk replacing the Opera House’s refined setting of velvet seats and ornate chandeliers with something discordant.  

[Alexandra Petri: Trump’s very weird night at the Kennedy Center Honors]

That change could also scramble the economics of an event that the institution regards as its biggest annual fundraising operation. It is unclear if the center will pay to rent the venue. One advantage, if the center can pull off a fundraising feat, is the arena’s 20,000 seats—compared with the Opera House’s roughly 2,300.

Last year, the center claimed that it raised $23 million from the Honors, doubling the previous year’s number under the pre-Trump leadership. Whatever happened, it wasn’t enough. By spring, donations to the center had plunged, and officials recently said the center was running out of money.

The Kennedy Center began hosting its annual lifetime-achievement ceremony in 1978. The evening lauds five figures, groups, or organizations that have made a significant contribution to American culture, spanning disciplines and tastes. In 2006, for example, the orchestra conductor Zubin Mehta was celebrated alongside Dolly Parton, Smokey Robinson, Steven Spielberg, and Andrew Lloyd Webber. A former producer described the event to me as something like an American Nobel Prize, but just for the arts: a rare recognition bestowed by not just an arts organization but, in a sense, the country itself.

The Honors certainly packed in lots of Washington pageantry. The weekend traditionally included a White House reception with the president and a medallion ceremony at the State Department before culminating on the first Sunday of December with an evening at the Kennedy Center, where honorees watched from the presidential box as some of the world’s biggest acts paid tribute to them. Every president since Jimmy Carter attended at least once during their term.

“I think it was a cool weekend, no matter who you are,” another former producer told me. “(A) to tell everyone you’re getting the Kennedy Center Honor, (b) to bring your family to the White House and take a picture with the president, and sit there up in the box and have everyone perform in your honor.”

Trump’s first year in office represented a rupture. In 2017, Kennedy Center officials hoped to have an Honors weekend much like any other. But following the violence at a white-supremicist rally in Charlottesville, Virginia—and Trump’s infamous remark that there were “some very fine people on both sides” of the protest, despite his attempt to clarify that he “condemned totally” the white nationalists and Nazis who were involved—honorees drew a line. The dancer Carmen de Lavallade said she would skip the White House reception. “I was in Richmond setting a ballet, and that’s when all that stuff was going on in Charlottesville,” she told Essence. “I saw the burning cross and I said, ‘I’m not going.’” The television producer Norman Lear told The New York Times that he would skip the reception, and Lionel Richie soon expressed his own uncertainty, telling The Washington Post that he would “play it by ear.”

Eventually, the White House announced that Trump and the first lady would not participate in Honors activities so that they could proceed “without any political distraction.” The Kennedy Center publicly welcomed the decision. “In choosing not to participate in this year’s Honors activities, the Administration has graciously signaled its respect for the Kennedy Center and ensures the Honors gala remains a deservingly special moment for the Honorees,” then-Chairman David M. Rubenstein and President Deborah Rutter said in a statement at the time. “We are grateful for this gesture.”

Trump skipped every Honors ceremony during his first term—the only sitting president to do so. Removing himself showed that the Honors could do just fine without the president’s participation. But second-term Trump produced the opposite effect: Inserting himself seemed only to diminish the ceremony.

After remaking the Kennedy Center’s board and leadership when he returned to office last year, Trump became more involved in the Honors than any president before him—which is to say, no other president considered it part of his job to plan and host an awards show. Just as it’s become part of presidential lore that Trump may have run for president in 2016 because Barack Obama roasted him at the White House Correspondents’ Dinner in 2011, close observers of the Kennedy Center wonder if Trump’s spurning in 2017 helped inspire his takeover of the center. Now it’s his awards show, his arts complex, his renovation, and—if the center is never able to recover—his mess.

Manipulator vs. Manipulator

For a few minutes on Wednesday night, the New York Film Festival crowd watched Elizabeth Holmes, the disgraced ex-CEO and founder of the medical start-up Theranos, and her partner, Billy Evans, as they seemingly went about their lives at a San Diego beach house. Evans was on the phone, insisting that nobody really understood Holmes; she sat next to him, her expression vacant, and patted her baby on the back. Moments later, she was talking with someone on her computer, her AirPods in and eyes unblinking. Then, suddenly, the camera cut to a third person—the comedian and director Nathan Fielder, who was observing from the side—prompting huge laughter from the crowd.

Honestly, the reveal wasn’t that funny. Fielder was just standing there—not saying anything, not making a face, just existing in the room. But his appearance acknowledged something obvious: We were not watching a sober, straightforward accounting of Holmes’s domestic life in 2023, days before she was set to report to a federal prison on counts of defrauding investors in her company. To his fans, Fielder has cultivated a reputation as modern culture’s foremost stenographer of strange situations. Somehow, he had scored extensive access to a notorious fabulist whose moon-faced staring and affected baritone had made her a national punch line. And now, in You Can See Everything, the documentary that emerged out of the time he spent with Holmes, we were about to see just how weird this woman really was; the anticipation was unbearable, hence the laughter.

You Can See Everything, made in collaboration with the director Lance Oppenheim, has gathered intense interest since its surprise reveal last month at the Telluride Film Festival. The standby line for tickets Wednesday stretched long and wide outside Lincoln Center’s Alice Tully Hall, where the film would be playing; a publicist told me she’d heard that someone had been out there since 2:30 a.m. Inside, the director Noah Baumbach and the novelist Rachel Kushner took seats. The screening was the only one of the festival, and the last time the movie would be shown publicly ahead of its theatrical release, on October 16. Although some detailed reports about the movie had popped up after its screening at Telluride, Fielder and Oppenheim had apparently asked that crowd to not give away too much, and they did the same in a post-screening Q&A at NYFF.

I’ll do my best. (You’ll have to find out for yourself why the movie earned an R rating for “some graphic nudity.”) In some ways, it’s easier to talk about what the movie isn’t. You Can See Everything lacks the architecture of Fielder’s best-known shows, where he establishes and solves an elaborate problem of his own making. Think of the second season of HBO’s The Rehearsal, during which Fielder wonders why airline pilots have a hard time communicating directly with one another—and, in his exploratory process, ends up becoming a certified pilot himself. In projects such as that show, and Comedy Central’s Nathan for You, he inhabits a quasi-fictional “Nathan Fielder” persona, who is droll and almost uncomfortably literal—possibly neurodivergent, as explored in an episode on The Rehearsal.

What he’s doing in You Can See Everything is closer to journalism. The filming constituted Holmes’s first exposure to the media in seven years, and Fielder—amazingly, preposterously—was one of the few people on the planet (she also did an interview with The New York Times) in a position to chip away at some of Holmes’s self-evident fictions, a privilege he took seriously. He does not appear to be inhabiting a persona; initially, he told the New York audience, he wasn’t even supposed to appear on-screen. The documentary is filled with scenes in which Fielder deliberates with his collaborators about a potential line of inquiry, or aggressively presses Holmes or Evans in the hope of catching them fibbing about one of their many boisterous claims.

[Read: Elizabeth Holmes isn’t fooling anyone]

Frustratingly for him, Holmes is a world-class prevaricator. She is a convicted felon, a newish mother, in a happy relationship—all positions that can transform how a person moves through the world, yet the facade never drops. Up close, the public stereotypes around her persona are largely confirmed. At one point she insists, with total sincerity, that not only is she always being honest, but she has never told a lie in her life. Hearing this, Fielder’s stoicism cracks; he’s as openly baffled as viewers may be. Faced with such willful obstinacy, he attempts a more indirect fact-finding mission, working his way backwards from some of Holmes’s contentions to understand how she distinguishes between a truth and a lie. For example, the documentary spends some time unpacking Theranos’s well-publicized claim that it had created a machine that could perform more than 1,000 diagnostic medical tests. To be clear: This was not true, at all, and was extensively debunked. But Fielder keeps nudging Holmes to clarify how she came to believe she was telling the truth.

In her eyes, Holmes slowly explains, the machine itself was theoretically capable of performing these tests, only the company hadn’t developed the actual tests. Again, to be clear: This is sophistry, like claiming you’ve invented a car that’s theoretically capable of driving at 10,000 miles an hour … only the necessary engine doesn’t exist, and you know it doesn’t exist. Fielder continues pushing, leading Holmes to quietly withdraw; she seems genuinely shell-shocked by the growing impossibility of justifying her position out loud. Inquisitive moments like these are seemingly the closest she comes to grasping that her reasoning doesn’t stack up. Meanwhile, Evans simmers with badly concealed irritation over Fielder’s skepticism. “I know what you’re feeling,” he interjects at the end of this long explanation, only for Fielder to respond, quickly and forcefully, “No, you don’t.”

This has been one of the recurring themes of Fielder’s work: the difficulty of really seeing inside someone else’s head, of authentically connecting with another person. His shows are filled with people who appear to have never thought through their worldview using their words—who, when they try, do such a bad job of it that Fielder can typically respond only with his trademark deadpan “Oh, okay” (a phrase that pops up in this film). In letting his subjects speak for themselves, he elicits the easy laugh at the obviously ridiculous, like a man on The Rehearsal who’s obsessed with numerology and won’t stop talking about how he crashed his Scion tC. But his works remind viewers that not all things under the sun abide by rationale and common sense. People are strange, generally. Some of them squirrel away in a cave of their own making for years and years, never exposing their beliefs to sunlight. Others are convinced they’ve got something special to share with the world—and, when they try to do so, slowly erect the circumstances of their own downfall.

Because it’s preposterous, really, that Holmes would attempt to convince the world of her fundamental authenticity by inviting Nathan Fielder, of all people, into her home. It’s like announcing a special guest at your local swimming class who turns out to be the shark from Jaws. But the director gives her a fair shake. It is possible to come away from this documentary with empathy for Elizabeth Holmes. By withholding his judgment, and making an earnest attempt to untangle her tortured justification for what easily scans as garden-variety chicanery, Fielder digs at something more interesting than whether she was knowingly lying about what Theranos could medically accomplish. He makes space for the possibility that someone can be so wrapped up in their bespoke logic that their actions, however destructive, essentially come from a place of innocence. In attempting to understand this, one’s empathy may easily be exploited; there are moments when the viewer may wonder whether Holmes is getting one over on him. (At some points, she appears to be openly flirting with him, but the thread is dropped.) Yet how can we possibly abandon the responsibility to make sense of one another?

[Read: You’ve never seen anything quite like The Rehearsal]

This is an emotional argument, not a legal one. Holmes’s actions had more consequences than just some failed investments; her chief scientist, Ian Gibbons, committed suicide over the stress of being deposed in a lawsuit. You Can See Everything is certainly not an apology tour. Yet to some people, the fact that Holmes has even the tiniest chance of rehabilitating her image, through her association with such a critically acclaimed humorist, will seem like a flagrant travesty. In modern America, incarceration is no longer the last word on the criminally liable; our culture is filled with plenty of scandalous figures who went to prison, said enough of the right things, and got right back to it once released. If you don’t think it’s possible for Holmes to leverage some of the goodwill this movie will incur into marketing Theranos 2.0—an oft-discussed project within You Can See Everything—then, as they say down South, bless your heart. (Evans, over the course of the film, openly monetizes his partner’s image, betting that having any exposure, even if negative, is better than disappearing entirely.)

In parts of the film, Fielder incorporates some of his familiar staging techniques, recreating some off-camera interactions with Holmes with the help of a famous actor whose first appearance prompted knowing applause. These sequences are less compelling than what we get from watching Holmes herself; the actor, though certainly talented, can’t re-create her intensity. In conversations, Fielder and the actor—who by now have both spent plenty of time studying Holmes—still can’t quite crack what is going on here. Is Holmes, somehow, a victim of conspiracy? Is she just the most manipulative woman alive? Could both be true, at the same time?

Viewers may decide for themselves; I found it plausible that Holmes still doesn’t comprehend what she did wrong. But though parts of the movie are unavoidably funny, the project is less explicitly comedic than you might expect, given Fielder’s previous work. At least that’s how I took it: Personally, I find nothing really amusing about the idea that people remain unknowable and morally unaccountable to anyone but themselves. During the screening’s Q&A, Fielder said he and Oppenheim had structured the movie so audiences will go on a discovery journey like theirs. In that sense, once the lights went up, I felt the way he did: at a loss for words to explain what I’d really learned from all of this, beyond a great, nervous confusion at the ongoing mystery of other people.

Zack Korman, the CEO of Embroidery, thinks so. He works in cybersecurity and argues that “rogue” AI agents are less frightening and complicated than they may seem. Galaxy Brain host Charlie Warzel talks with Korman about how companies and institutions should up their cyberdefenses and why the so-called AI doomers are frustrating him.

The following is a transcript of the episode:

Some publishing trends so clearly reflect cultural changes that they are easy to explain: Few readers in 2026 are surprised by, say, a sudden crop of books about artificial intelligence, or a best-selling series of novels catering to young gamers. Other literary patterns are more mysterious. This week in The Atlantic, Julia M. Klein wrote about Robert Kolker’s The Vanishing Family, which adds to a growing bookshelf of new works focusing on dementia. Klein’s essay led me to wonder why several notable recent releases, both nonfiction and novels, have taken on this condition—and how each explores the subject differently.

The Vanishing Family follows a clan dealing with an inherited form of dementia that drastically changes the sufferer’s personality. Klein is most interested in Kolker’s emphasis on the “slipperiness of identity” and the “malleability of the self”; are you still the same person without your usual behaviors or your formative memories? As some family members succumb to the condition, the author also focuses on their healthy caregivers, for whom “the sense of loss is ongoing—like witnessing a living death.”

The mysteries of dementia—both medical and personal, for both patient and loved ones—provide rich terrain for writers, as Marion Renault pointed out last year in a different Atlantic essay about the book Valley of Forgetting, by Jennie Erin Smith. We know very little about the condition, but at the same time, it comes with a “grim certainty”—it is largely untreatable, and always terminal. “This tension makes the illness ripe for figurative language,” Renault wrote.

Both Kolker and Smith conveyed this tension in fine writing and painstaking detail, but figurative language is where fiction excels. This year, two novels managed to approach the illness in new ways—one from the perspective of a caregiver and another from a patient’s point of view. Kenan Orhan’s The Renovation is a somewhat surrealist fable about many things—exile, autocracy, the nuclear family, the carceral state. But the greatest stressor in the life of its protagonist, Dilara, is her father’s rapidly progressing Alzheimer’s. Orhan portrays the hard realities of caring for someone you no longer recognize, but also explores the metaphorical possibilities: Dilara and her father were forced to flee Turkey as political refugees, and they both yearn for a past that has become inaccessible, whether or not they remember it.

In her stark new novel, Switzy, Emma Cline gives us the perspective of the sufferer. “Alzheimer’s novels are rare,” Hillary Kelly wrote in an Atlantic essay about the book, “and those told from the patient’s point of view are rarer.” Cline portrays her subject, David, “in a wandering, dreamlike style,” Kelly writes. His mental absence “becomes a startling and eerie phenomenon—like the moment you realize you’ve been driving for miles but don’t recall anything about the journey.”

David is flying to Zurich in a private jet so that he can die by assisted suicide before Alzheimer’s robs him of his agency. As he rifles through half memories and smiles through painful interactions, Cline expertly dramatizes the tension Renault defined between the unknowableness of dementia and its unavoidable end. David is becoming a different person, but for now he is still himself, and he holds on desperately to the single choice he has left. Alzheimer’s novels are not so rare anymore, but like any great novelist, Cline has found a wholly original way to distinguish her work.
