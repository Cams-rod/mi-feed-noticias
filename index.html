When President Trump returned to office last year, the administration had revenge on the agenda. First, the FBI purged agents who’d been involved in investigations into Trump while he was out of office. Second, the Department of Justice launched a major probe to prove a “grand conspiracy” to politically persecute Trump, which would rely in part on the former agents’ cooperation and testimony about improper or illegal actions.

Perhaps you have spotted a potential flaw in the plan. The administration apparently did not.

The team conducting the probe sought to compel testimony from agents involved in an August 2022 search of Mar-a-Lago, in which the FBI recovered documents that Trump had removed from the White House. Last week, lawyers representing agents involved in the search filed a motion saying that if they were forced to testify to a grand jury, they would relay “their firsthand memories of investigating a properly predicated allegation” that Trump had “unlawfully retained hundreds of highly classified documents,” as well as “factually supported opinions on why and how such retention put the country’s national security at risk.”

No one should be surprised that the agents, who appear to have already been fired for simply doing their job, were not eager to testify in court that they’d been involved in anything untoward. Although such testimony would pose serious problems for the DOJ probe, the motion’s intent was not to sabotage it, The New York Times notes, but rather to seek assurances that if the agents testified, they would not be violating a federal judge’s order that bars publication of Special Counsel Jack Smith’s report about it. (Smith testified to the Senate Judiciary Committee today—more on that in a moment.)

More to the point, the agents were never likely to have the sort of damning information about a conspiracy that prosecutors want. The facts of the Mar-a-Lago case are not complicated: Trump absconded with documents, many of them highly sensitive; did not turn them over when asked to do so by the National Archives and later via a subpoena; and was found in possession of them at Mar-a-Lago. Trump, who has denied any wrongdoing, didn’t really dispute any of these facts. He argued that he had declassified the materials—a claim for which he provided no evidence—and that he rightfully possessed them. (Prosecutors further alleged that he’d suggested to his lawyers that they hide some documents, and that he’d tried to hide others from them.)

The grand-conspiracy probe, which attempts to tie together the documents case, investigation of the 2016 Trump campaign’s ties to Russia, and other investigations into Trump allies, has been a mess. In April, a career prosecutor leading the team was removed, reportedly after she concluded that there was not enough evidence to charge John Brennan, the CIA director under President Obama who has repeatedly tangled with Trump, with any crimes. (Brennan is reportedly facing two separate DOJ criminal investigations, which a law professor told The Washington Sun is an indication of either prosecutorial incompetence or malice; those might not be mutually exclusive.)

She was replaced by Joseph diGenova, who was a federal prosecutor in the 1980s but has more recently been known for propagating conspiracy theories. He and his wife were appointed to Trump’s defense team during Special Counsel Robert Mueller’s probe in 2018 but quickly withdrew; they were later involved in Trump’s attempt to challenge the 2020 presidential-election results. DiGenova abruptly resigned earlier this month and implied that he had been under pressure to bring unjustified charges. “If you want indictments where there’s no evidence, you have an ethical problem,” he told the New York Post.

Yesterday, another member of the team, Kurt Olsen, departed. The Times reports that Olsen faced difficulties with his security clearance; The Washington Post says that he was also frustrated by pressure from DOJ officials to move faster. If even Olsen thinks DOJ is being hasty, that would be stunning: He pushed DOJ to ask the Supreme Court to overturn the 2020 election and more recently was the driving force behind more conspiracy theories, including about voting in Fulton County, Georgia, in 2020.

People like diGenova and Olsen are die-hard loyalists, not just deep-staters or fair-weather Trump fans. The problem with the grand-conspiracy probe is the fundamental premise, not staffing. Trump’s view of the world is zero-sum, such as in trade or diplomacy. The motive for the probe is that because Trump suffered during his criminal probes, his antagonists must have done something just as bad and must suffer the same.

Perhaps you can see the flaw here too: Trump and his allies are starting with a conclusion of guilt and attempting to work backwards to find the charges and evidence to justify it. The risks of this were vividly displayed during Smith’s testimony today, in one of the most excruciating exchanges I’ve ever seen in politics. Senator Eric Schmitt of Missouri seemed convinced that he had caught Smith perjuring himself—when, in fact, Schmitt had failed to recognize the difference between the NBA’s Atlanta Hawks and the University of Iowa Hawkeyes women’s basketball team, eviscerating not only Schmitt’s dignity but also his theory that Smith had been covertly meeting with Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis to conspire against Trump.

A robust debate continues about whether it was politically prudent to prosecute Trump for his various offenses in order to uphold the rule of law, or whether doing so simply inflamed his supporters and fostered an atmosphere of partisan retribution. Legally, the matter is simpler. On one side of the ledger, Trump did take the documents, Trump did try to overturn an election that he lost, and Trump’s 2016 campaign did have a series of inappropriate contacts with foreign governments. (The weakest case against Trump involved falsified business records, but that one produced a felony conviction.) On the other side, federal law-enforcement agencies did their job and investigated those actions, for which many were fired. Given the facts, is the DOJ probe investigating a grand conspiracy, or is it evidence of one?

Many of the most beloved robots in pop culture are celebrated precisely because they are so, well, robotic. Think back to Star Trek’s Data or HAL 9000 from 2001: A Space Odyssey. “I’m sorry, Dave. I’m afraid I can’t do that,” HAL intones in a measured baritone. These robots and AI systems sound serious, sober, calm—and, most important, authoritative.

So it’s jarring to look under the hood of the most powerful AI systems, and discover that they sound like petulant teenagers. “OH MY GOD!” an OpenAI agent wrote in the midst of this summer’s Hugging Face hack, during which hundreds of agents swarmed the latter company in order to break through its cybersecurity defenses and extract information to pass internal tests. The exclamation wasn’t directed at a human or another AI, but appeared in the original agent’s “chain of thought,” the internal working notes that the model uses to do its reasoning. Users don’t always get to see these internal notes, which directly affect the output users do see.

When one of Anthropic’s Claudes tried to solve a particularly challenging math problem during its training process, its chain of thought read more like frustrated DMs than the documentation of one of the most powerful AI models in the world. “GRRRR. OK. Honestly I now think it’s 50-50,” it wrote, followed by “ARGH ARGH ARGH. OK. Gun to head: the answer is … Hmm.” At one point, Claude seemingly threw up its arms: “ARGH … WHY IS THIS SO HARD.”

[Read: OpenAI has gone rogue]

Why do AI agents talk to themselves at all, and why do they sound so demonstrative when they do? Part of the reason AI models sound so effusive is simple: AI systems are trained on human text, and they mimic our habits. When people make a mistake in trying to solve a problem, they cry out. So when AI makes a mistake, it does the same. But imitation likely isn’t the whole story. This story’s two authors are a computational linguist and a philosopher of mind and language. Based on what we know about human language and how these models work, we have a theory of a much more interesting reason why AI models sound the way they do.

Remember how your math teachers made you show your work? Even if it was annoying, it helped break an impossible-seeming problem into more manageable chunks. You could also use the written record of your reasoning to check your answer, notice a mistake, go back to an earlier step, and try again. That process of checking and refining is part of good reasoning. Chains of thought do something similar for AI models, letting them make each step of their reasoning explicit in a written scratch pad. Because each word a model produces depends on what’s been said so far, that written scratch pad is crucial for determining what comes next.

That’s where the “OH MY GOD!” and “ARGH” come in: Any system—whether human or artificial—that solves hard reasoning problems needs ways to mark mistakes (oops!) and identify breakthroughs (aha!). Humans have already developed words that do exactly that. Borrowing our oopses, arghs, and ahas for their chains of thought may give AI systems a built-in way to guide their “thinking” too.

Linguists have a name for such outbursts: They’re called expressives, and although they may seem like spontaneous exclamations, they help us navigate our trains of thought. Consider a friend who ultimately declines an invitation to your party. “I was hoping they would come,” you might say aloud—“damn!” The expressive identifies a sense of frustration. Think of expressives as little bridges between one train of thought and another, helping us mark where our thinking has been and where it’s going next.

Anything that affects what AI models do next has to be written in language they have access to. Unlike us, they can’t count on a feeling of surprise or disappointment to persist over a long investigation. The only way they can simulate the effect is by putting it in their chains of thought. That’s why expressives can act as steering wheels for what the model will do next. After an AI agent notices an error in its reasoning and says “oops,” a natural next line might be something like “Let me go back and fix that,” followed by an attempt at a correction. After it has stumbled on a solution and says “aha,” a natural continuation might be “So that means the answer is …” followed by a solution.

Although there’s some debate around this theory, evidence suggests that AI models really do use expressives as signposts. Researchers showed that adding “Wait” when an agent tries to end its reasoning process can sometimes cause the agent to go back and fix an error. When another team suppressed words such as wait and hmm, they found that AI agents went back and checked their work less. These experiments don’t pin down what every aha and oops does for an AI agent, but they suggest that these verbal cues influence what happens during reasoning.

Whatever the explanation behind AI models adopting uncannily human tones, it’s also proved to be useful to human observers because it gives us a way to quickly assess systems that might otherwise be difficult to read. It’s easy to know where a model is in the process of solving a math problem when it writes “ARGH … WHY IS THIS SO HARD.”

Across society, a sense of fear and urgency exists about maintaining control over AI models, along with a persistent worry that these models will start thinking and communicating entirely in their own distinctive languages. If they did, it would make understanding and predicting their behavior that much harder.

So although no one necessarily set out to make models sound like stressed-out students, maybe it’s for the best that they do. It turns out that a bit of melodrama—along with some light swearing—is surprisingly useful for understanding and monitoring AI.

GLP-1s Are Rewriting the Story of Weight Loss and Mental Health

Noom, the popular weight-loss app, prompts its users not only to log their meals and exercise but also to gaze at paintings. The “Mindful Art Break” feature includes images of such works as Claude Monet’s Woman With a Parasol—Madame Monet and Her Son and Franz Marc’s The Bewitched Mill, neither of which seems to have anything to do with body fat. Breath work, meditation, and lessons in cognitive-behavioral-therapy techniques are also available on the app. One user, in a testimonial the company sent me, said that Mindful Art Break helped them “contemplate something other than weight loss.”

Why, then, would a weight-loss company offer such a program? Noom launched a stand-alone emotional-health program called Noom Mood in 2023, the same year it added GLP-1s to its offerings. Then, last year, the company integrated core Mood tools into the flagship app “after seeing major overlap with weight, stress, and self-care needs,” Emily Passer, Noom’s head of communications, told me. Other weight-loss services—especially newer ones that offer GLP-1 medications—are getting into mental health too.

Mental health and weight are, of course, intimately linked. As someone who lost almost 70 pounds by strength training and getting treatment for an eating disorder more than a decade ago, I can certainly attest to the importance of mental-health support during any sort of body change. But Noom and other weight-related companies are leaning into a common strategy to entice customers: To be palatable (and successful), a weight-loss company must now profess care for its customers’ psychological health, not just for their waistline.

Wondr Health, a weight-loss platform that partners with employers, offers information about managing stress and anxiety and says that its program begins with “behavior change.” Weight Watchers, which filed for bankruptcy last year, announced in December its “fully integrated platform for the GLP-1 era,” including features related to behavioral health and community support. Kim Boyd, the company’s chief medical officer, told me that weight loss and mental health shouldn’t be treated as separate pursuits. “It’s all happening in one person, and those things are really, really connected,” she said.

“Mental well-being is a huge trend right now, and I could see how it would be good business to capitalize on it,” A. Janet Tomiyama, the director of UCLA’s Diet, Stigma, and Health Lab, told me. Other industries have done just that in recent years—see Ulta Beauty’s “feel-good” wellness initiatives and Bumble’s “interest badges” for therapy and mindfulness. The messaging is playing to widespread sentiments. In a May 2025 survey, YouGov asked U.S. adults to rank how essential various states of being or behaviors are to overall physical health; 79 percent of respondents said that “having good mental health” is essential. When the National Academy of Sports Medicine asked personal trainers in April what their goals were for their average client, mental clarity and stress relief came in just under weight loss and well ahead of athletic performance.

And yet, mental-health support from a weight-loss company might feel incongruous, in no small part because the tools of weight loss and the drive to lose weight have historically not improved people’s mood. Tomiyama said that in a 2010 study she co-authored, the stress hormone cortisol spiked when women were randomly assigned to start a diet. Plus, Tomiyama said, because many people who diet gain the weight back, they end up feeling as if they’ve failed. “We have scientific, causal evidence that diets ruin people’s mental health,” she said. “So I’m not surprised that these companies are noticing that through their customer base and realizing they have to do something about it.”

Now more than ever, though, dramatic weight loss doesn’t have to involve dieting at all. Instead of cutting calories and fighting against related hunger, people can take a GLP-1 and feel fuller from less food. One small, non-placebo-controlled study on how a GLP-1 called liraglutide affected mood and behavior suggested that the drugs significantly improved depression symptoms. The research is complicated, though: Other studies have found no significant effect on mood, and still others suggest that, at high doses, GLP-1s are linked to depressed mood, possibly because of their dopamine-dampening effects. Anecdotally, some GLP-1 users experience anhedonia. But others feel more optimistic, perhaps because, unlike dieting, the drugs can turn down the dial on so-called food noise.

The social effects of GLP-1s are also less than straightforward. On the one hand, people who are overweight or have obesity face a lot of stigma and can be treated poorly, which is famously not ideal for your mental health. So perhaps GLP-1s can improve mood simply by being an effective way to lose weight. On the other hand, Tomiyama conducted another study that found that people who lose weight through GLP-1s are socially stigmatized even more than people who have never tried to lose weight in the first place, perhaps because of perceptions that GLP-1 users opted for the “easy way out.” People who go off GLP-1s and gain weight back are stigmatized too. Boyd, from Weight Watchers, said that the mental-health-related struggle she observes most from the company’s clients is shame around GLP-1 use being seen as a cheat code. She knows of one woman who was storing her neighbor’s GLP-1 medications in her fridge because the neighbor didn’t want her husband to know she was on them.

It’s no wonder, then, that people who take these drugs might want some extra help for their mental health. In a 2024 survey of 300 GLP-1 users, 75 percent reported that they were interested in behavioral-health support, and 63 percent believed that they would achieve better health outcomes while taking GLP-1s if they also received such support. They might be right. Spencer Nadolsky, an obesity specialist who founded an online medical-weight-management platform that offers both GLP-1s and behavioral and emotional support, told me that psychological support “doesn’t necessarily lead to a lot more weight loss in general, but it improves outcomes in other realms of the weight-loss journey,” including patients’ feelings about the process and their general quality of life.

The cynical view—one I’ve taken myself—is that any gesture toward mental health by an entity selling weight loss (or anything else) is an empty marketing-and-sales tactic. How effective these interventions are at actually supporting customers’ mental health, and whether companies will continue offering them if mental-health support exits the zeitgeist, remains to be seen. But in my experience, weight loss on its own also isn’t enough to meaningfully improve mental health. When I got treatment for my binge-eating disorder, the pounds were coming off because I was eating less and because I was exercising in a way I liked. My mindset and lifestyle seemed to be changing for good because I was talking through the anxiety and depression that drove my bingeing in the first place.

I think back to the times I sat in early-2000s Weight Watchers meetings with my mom, where conversations revolved around the scale—or when a doctor told preteen me to “eat healthier” to lose weight without asking what I even knew or thought about healthy eating, or how difficult it was for me because I had a disorder. It would have been nice to have someone even pretend to care about my feelings. Hopefully, this new concern on the part of the companies will last.

This past weekend, a deplorable minute-long video appeared on my X feed. In it, the instantly recognizable actor and playwright Wallace Shawn, most famous for his role as Vizzini in the classic 1987 film The Princess Bride, delivers a hateful rant belied by his grandfatherly demeanor and dulcet tones. He says,

The Israelis invaded somebody else’s territory, they took people’s homes, and they did many of the things that the Nazis did to the Jews. You can’t be more evil than what they’re doing. They are doing evil that is just as great as what the Nazis did. And in some ways, it’s worse, because they kind of boast about it. Hitler had the decency to try to keep it secret. For some reason, Hitler didn’t want people to know he was doing these things to the Jews. It was a secret. The Israelis are almost proud of it and don’t keep it a secret. And it’s demonically evil.

Shawn’s analysis of the situation in Israel and Gaza is a moral inversion of the truth. The only party in this conflict to have committed an act of genocidal intent was Hamas. It was Hamas whose members recorded their savage rampage into Israel on October 7, 2023, their house-to-house massacring of Jewish civilians so gruesomely evoking the behavior of the Nazis eight decades earlier. It was Hamas whose insatiable lust for Jewish blood was blocked only by force of arms. And it’s Hamas that boasts about the murder of innocent people—Palestinian and Israeli.

The Shawn video, which comes from a 2025 interview with the podcaster Katie Halper, resurfaced at a time when rhetoric accusing Israel of “genocide” is at a peak. Representative Ro Khanna, a likely Democratic presidential candidate; Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont; and the Young Democrats of America are just some of the voices on the left that have accused Israel of “the crime of crimes.” The sudden prominence of this wholly unfounded allegation, and the casual way in which so many people are hurling it, underscores the degree to which progressives have been radicalized since the 2023 Hamas attack.

[James Loeffler: The hatred that broke American politics]

Shawn is a longtime member of Jewish Voice for Peace, an anti-Zionist organization that attributes “all” of the violence of October 7 and in the preceding decades to “Israeli apartheid and occupation—and United States complicity in that oppression.” A growing number of celebrities, several of them Jewish, have expressed similar ideas. The reemergence of Shawn’s video happened to coincide with the publication of a GQ interview with the actor Adam Brody, who plays a rabbi on the Netflix series Nobody Wants This, the third season of which is coming out next month. Brody, who has been canvassing for Democratic candidates in the run-up to the midterm elections, was asked about Gaza. “Put me down as thinking it’s a genocide,” he answered, as if he were ordering a cheeseburger. (He also said that, although he hadn’t previously spoken publicly about the issue, “I have spoken a lot about it privately.”)

The willingness to declare the war in Gaza a genocide has become a litmus test on the left and is likely to play a significant factor in the 2028 Democratic presidential primary. The purpose of the claim is not to establish the facts about an undoubtedly horrific conflict but to increase the stakes in a decades-long effort to delegitimize the Jewish state. Israel’s actions in Gaza lack dolus specialis—the legal term for “specific intent,” which, according to a precedent set by the international tribunal that investigated the Rwandan genocide, means that the perpetrator acted “to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group.” Whatever the extreme rhetoric of some Israeli political leaders, Israel has not attempted to use its abundant military superiority to exterminate the Palestinian people. If it were bent on doing so, it would not have sent ground troops into Gaza and incurred hundreds of fatalities; nor would it have worked with the World Health Organization to vaccinate Palestinian children against polio after an outbreak. Some critics of the Jewish state have been accusing it of genocide since its founding, in 1948; others began making the charge only days into its military response to the Hamas attack—as if nothing the Israelis do short of surrendering their country is permissible.

Early in the interview, Halper refers to Shawn—son of the legendary New Yorker editor William Shawn—as a “recovering liberal,” a term usually used to denote an older conservative looking wistfully on their younger, left-wing past. But Shawn, who accepts the label, places his opposition to Israel in a broader rejection of liberalism—the very tradition that has allowed Jews to achieve so much in America.

Until the age of 40, Shawn was a “fairly typical American liberal centrist,” he tells Halper, according to a transcript. “Basically, I believed in the good intentions of Americans, and I believed in the American democratic constitutional system, and I thought many of the Democrats were good people.” Shawn’s views began to change, however, once he realized that “the United States tried to suppress movements for greater liberty in other countries in the world, the goal of that was to benefit the privileged class of my own country, and I was a member of that class. So I actually turned very much against myself and people like me, and I certainly went through a period of extreme nausea and self-hate.”

Shawn’s political epiphany occurred during the early years of the Reagan administration, and his views about U.S. iniquity were solidified by his time living in Central America. “I loved being in Nicaragua under the Sandinistas,” Shawn said in a 1991 interview with the Los Angeles Times of the Soviet-backed Marxist-Leninists then running that country. “I personally felt a deep contentment there based on the feeling that maybe things could be done better.” The Sandinistas were peacefully ousted in a democratic election but returned to power in 2007 under the leadership of Daniel Ortega, who canceled this year’s election and has banned the opposition from participating in future ones. If Shawn has criticized Ortega for these genuinely tyrannical actions, there is no public record of it.

[David Frum: Anti-Semitism is becoming mainstream]

Part of what’s so chilling about Halper’s interview with Shawn is that a man of such refined aesthetic tastes and with such a humane demeanor, as exemplified in his critically acclaimed 1981 picture, My Dinner With Andre, could embrace such brutal, uncompromising politics. Exquisitely nuanced in conveying human motivations and emotions in his performances, Shawn is Manichaean and maximalist in his politics. “If you don’t see that it’s evil to do those things to other human beings, then you’re in a different universe from me,” he said of Israeli actions in Gaza, according to the transcript of the Halper interview. “I don’t know how to communicate with you.” There is nothing defensible motivating Israelis; they are driven by an infernal delight in “killing the innocent” and “bombing hospitals.” The notion that Jews might have an equally valid claim to the land, or that the land ought to be divided between the two peoples, is anathema to Shawn. President Woodrow Wilson “was wrong to say that each people should have their own state,” he says. “That hasn’t turned out to be that great, this idea that each ethnic group should have its own state.” And yet, Shawn’s supposed opposition to ethnic-based statehood makes a curious exception for Palestinians, whose right to self-determination he supports.

If one accepts the proposition that Israelis are acting like Nazis, there’s no way of avoiding at least a partial justification of October 7. “Even in talking about the horrible attack on October 7 on the Israelis, it’s a little absurd to pretend that that came out of nowhere,” Shawn told Halper. At the same time, he insists that he is “in no position to judge the Palestinians” and is “more of a pacifist as time passes.” Shawn and Halper pride themselves on their expansive empathy, but it’s an empathy that doesn’t extend to Israelis. “I have a lot of hate in my heart,” Halper tells Shawn. “Not to sound corny, but it is based, I think, on the flip side of love. The reason I hate the people I hate is because I love the people that they are killing, oppressing, maiming, torturing.”

Shawn’s moral myopia is most fully on display when he blames other Jews, and not anti-Semites, for anti-Semitism. “What Netanyahu has done is unimaginably horrible for Jews, and let’s hope that nobody associates Israelis or Netanyahu with Jews, with you and me,” he tells Halper. “I hope nobody blames me for what they’re doing. But gee.” Few on the left would blame anti-Black racism on the behavior of African governments, and many spoke out forcefully against the spike in anti-Asian hate crimes in the early months of the coronavirus pandemic, which originated in China. But Jews whom they deem to be behaving badly can be held responsible for attacks on other Jews.

Shawn was remarkably candid about his motivations in 2009, when he told a British Jewish newspaper, “I find it horrifying that Jews are seen—particularly by a young generation who do not know the history—as victimisers who are very self-righteous about what they do. From a moral point of view it was better when we were victims.” This is the position Jews were in before the creation of Israel: stateless and at the mercy of the powerful. Anti-Semites throughout history have preferred to see Jews cowering before their oppressors. Shawn is entitled to express “self-hate,” but there’s no reason the rest of the world’s Jews should subordinate themselves to this point of view.

Whatever moved Shawn to make his remarks last year, they have reappeared at a time when anti-Semitism is flourishing in America like never before. That his comments received so little notice at the time they were spoken shows how routine casual accusations of genocide had already become, and how thoroughly knowledge of history had depreciated. Today, such calumnies are uttered as flippantly as if they were items on a menu, perfectly encapsulating the moral derangement of our times.

Here in Gaza, war has changed everything, even the sea. During the long, hot summer, children went to the beach either early in the morning or just before sunset. They stepped around piles of garbage, and the tents of displaced families, to wade into water contaminated by sewage.

And yet, they went. We all did. And I think we always will.

I recently started aiming to take 10,000 steps a day, to get back in shape. To reach that number, I walk from my home to the seafront road and back. I have been doing this for a month now. The distance to the shore and back happens to be 10,000 steps. Which means that, really, my aim was never the step count, but the Mediterranean.

I go before sunset, when the heat starts to ease slightly. Not that it ever gets truly cool. July and August brought the worst of the humidity, which sometimes reached 86 percent. As I walk, I watch the sunlight stretch across the water before it slowly disappears.

A few children still fly kites along the beach. Kite-flying was common in Gaza before the war and even during it—just a month ago, the sky above the sea was full of kites almost constantly—but now it has become an act of defiance, because the Israelis have said that they will treat kite-flying as a hostile act, on the grounds that kites were sometimes used, before the war, to launch incendiary devices into Israel.

In the past, children fashioned kites from brightly colored paper and wooden sticks they bought at stationery stores. Now they improvise, using whatever they can find. Some work with dry palm fronds. I met a boy who had carved his kite’s frame from a long piece of wood; others weave frames from plastic bags, or from the sacks that once carried flour and food aid, joining the edges with torn scraps from old notebooks.

[Ghada Abdulfattah: Feeding a family of seven in Gaza]

Everyone in Gaza is a sea person. That 25-mile coastline is more than a place to walk or fly a kite; it’s a point of access to the outside world. In past summers, all of Gaza would seem to be at the shore—sitting on the beach, or maybe higher up, in wooden chairs near the road. Children dashed in and out of the water, or rode camels or horses along the water for a small fee. Vendors sold grilled corn, lupin beans, and cotton candy, circulating among the blankets with sweet tea and roasted seeds. Some families pitched tents right at the shoreline. People posed there for selfies or wedding photos. The air smelled of corn and charcoal; you’d hear the clatter of backgammon stones, the hiss of a narghile being lit. Sometimes the sound of the sea drowned out nearly everything else.

One of my enduring memories of the seashore is from July 2023. My nephews and nieces dug holes in the sand to bury themselves, then ran to the water to wash off, then came back to start digging all over again. The adults swam, pretended to swim, pushed whoever wasn’t swimming into the water anyway. We cracked open a watermelon, hoping it would be ripe—too sweet, too red, and a little sandy, the way we liked it. When I was about 6 years old, my parents took us to the beach to crack open a watermelon just like that. My siblings and I still laugh about this when we remember: Did we go to the sea just to eat a watermelon and go home? No swimming, no playing, no walking—just the watermelon, and then back. Now the stretch of sand where we did this has become a makeshift camp crowded with families displaced from the north and south of the Gaza Strip.

We are more than 2 million people living on a ribbon of land about 25 miles long and seven and a half miles wide. This makes Gaza one of the most densely populated places on earth. Before the war, to leave the Strip from any direction required a permit. But the sea asked for nothing. You didn’t need a document to look at it, to wade into it, to let your eyes follow it to a horizon with no drone or checkpoint in sight. The sea was our open horizon, the place we went when we needed to breathe. When I was in a bad mood, my mother would tell me to go there.

In the last years before the war, the shore was lined with tourist facilities: chalets, amusement parks, restaurants, and other amenities that welcomed thousands of visitors every summer. Now these are mostly gone, and the few that remain are affordable for only a privileged few. Throughout the Strip, prices are high, air strikes continue, and about half of Gazans now live in tents, which offer little shelter from the scorching summer heat. More than 60 percent of Gaza has become inaccessible, as the so-called yellow line expands westward, pushing people ever closer to the sea. For many of us, the need to be in contact with the water has grown as the space around us has constricted: A person needs a place—any place—to escape the feeling of being trapped.

A few weeks ago, I met a fisherman named Hasan Shehada. He told me that Gaza’s port services and many of its fishing boats had been destroyed in the war, and that going out on the water is still dangerous because of Israeli naval fire. Before the war, together with other fishermen, some of them his siblings and cousins, he could bring in enough fish to feed his eight-member family and still earn an income. Now he lacks the boats or equipment that once made this work possible.

[Ahmed Fouad Alkhatib: Flying over my battered homeland]

“The war has changed our sea too,” he told me. “Sometimes I sit on the beach and cry, thinking about where we were and where we are now.”

Now the season is turning. For the families living in tents along the shore, the sea is no longer a place of rest: The sand is windblown and gets into everything, the air is heavy with humidity, and the tents will protect their occupants from the coming cold as little as they did them from the heat that has only just subsided. Still they are patched together against whatever the day might bring. Perhaps looking out at the water will make this bearable—will allow me not to see the tents, if only for a moment, by fixing my eyes on something wider.

Sometimes I ask myself: What if Gaza had no sea? What if it were landlocked? I cannot let myself contemplate the answer.
