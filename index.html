This is an edition of The Wonder Reader, a newsletter in which our editors recommend a set of stories to spark your curiosity and fill you with delight. Sign up here to get it every Saturday morning.

A strange thing happens when you see something every day: Eventually, you stop seeing it. A building you once admired becomes part of your commute. The trees on your block become background. Your favorite coffee mug becomes something you reach for without thinking.

This tendency has been compounded by a culture that encourages us to move through life as efficiently as possible: We follow directions instead of wandering, put in headphones instead of listening to the world around us, and fill spare moments by looking at our phone. As Ian Bogost writes in a new article, we’ve become so focused on making life more productive that we risk treating the present as mere preparation for whatever comes next.

Wonder can interrupt that habit, Bogost argues—not just the kind inspired by mountains or sunsets, but the kind available in ordinary things. He describes noticing the warmth of a coffee mug, the crunch of gravel underfoot, and the clever engineering of a toaster. Wonder, in this sense, is less about seeking out extraordinary experiences than it is about paying close attention to the ones already around you.

Wendell Berry was not an easy man to reach. When I wanted to interview him for this magazine in the spring of 2015, his publicist warned me that a press request would take some doing. Berry didn’t have a cellphone. He didn’t use email. He didn’t even have a computer. He wrote most of his drafts by hand during the day, without electric light. And he definitely didn’t make himself available to any old reporter who wanted to talk.

I knew this already, of course. Berry, who died on Monday at the age of 92, lived a life defined by acts of radical abstention. He said no emphatically and often; principled refusal was a central theme of his writing and the lore that surrounded him.

The publicist spent the better part of a week trying to reach Berry on my behalf. This involved multiple phone calls to his farmhouse, in Henry County, Kentucky, where Berry and his wife, Tanya, had lived since the mid-1960s. Wendell spent most of his time outdoors, the publicist explained, so he often simply wasn’t around to hear the landline ring. After a while, though, she told me that she and Berry had managed to connect, and that he would consider my request. He asked that I write him a letter about the article I had in mind, which I emailed to the publicist so she could print it out and send it to him by post.

[From the February 1991 issue: Out of your car, off your horse]

A few weeks later, I received a voicemail from a number I didn’t recognize. “Hello, Mr. Fassler,” said a voice that was deep and hoarse with age. “This is Wendell Berry.” A giant of Luddism was speaking out of my iPhone. He asked me to call him back.

When we spoke, he told me in a warm, genial way that I was out of luck. “I’m just so covered up with work,” he said, “and I can’t tell if it’s because I’m just busy or because I’m old.” But he’d thought a bit about my letter. I’d asked him to take part in “By Heart,” a series in which authors discussed the passages from literature that had most shaped their writing and outlook. He said that his forthcoming book, Our Only World, contained a section that the magazine might adapt in some way. It hinged on some verses from Jesus’s Sermon on the Mount, as described in the Gospel of Matthew: “Take no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof.”

He asked me to read that section of his book, and to call him back once I had so that we could talk. So I did. And I’ve been thinking about its message ever since.

I was surprised, at first, that Berry felt so drawn to this specific moment from the Bible. Jesus’s instruction to not worry about the future seemed at odds with much of what Berry had written. Many of the social and environmental problems that Berry railed against were really problems of short-term thinking, including the pursuit of quick profit, the extractive poisoning of the land, and the love of cheap thrills over deeper, more hard-won pleasures. “Think not of tomorrow” seemed to better describe the ethos of Berry’s longtime nemeses: coal-industry strip miners, big agribusiness, the global financial system.

It was also an odd choice for someone who seemed so concerned about the excesses of modern life. Berry struck me as someone who absolutely would fixate on the general worsening direction of things. Fretting about the future is a favorite preoccupation of the conscientious. Berry occasionally admitted to his own worried state of mind on the page, including in one of his best-known poems: “When despair for the world grows in me,” he wrote, “I wake in the night at the least sound / in fear of what my life and my children’s lives may be.”

Nevertheless, in the part of his book that he asked me to read, he wrote, “Taking thought for the morrow is, fairly predictably, a waste of time.” He insisted that what we call “the future” is a flimsy and insubstantial thing at best—a phantom we obsess over that never really arrives, or never arrives in the way we imagine. “Most of the bad possibilities I have worried about have never happened,” Berry wrote. “By taking thought for the morrow we have invested, and wasted, a lot of effort in preparing for morrows that never came.”

Obsessing about the future, he argued, also has devastating moral consequences. It allows people to avoid engaging with the urgent realities of the present.

I don’t know whether Berry, given his extremely offline lifestyle, ever learned about doomscrolling—the way social media can serve as a collective orgy of dread. But he’s right that, when people become too obsessed with a future that may or may not befall them, they tend to turn their back on the moment they’re in. “The time is coming when it will be too late to save the world. Oh, oh, oh,” he wrote, lampooning the handwringer in so many of us. “If that is the way our minds are afflicted, we and our world are dead already. The present is going by and we are not in it.” As I see it, this was the core purpose of Berry’s work: He dared his readers to live in a way that honors the situation at hand, at this very second.

That mandate suffused everything he wrote about. He exhorted readers to enjoy the food on their plates, to bear witness to the places that surround them, and to revel in shared experience with the people in their orbit. Honoring the present was a joyful thing for Wendell Berry. It was also a form of rewarding but very difficult work. It meant slow, careful deliberation about the things he was willing to let into his life, whether that was a new piece of technology or a request from a journalist.

And it meant searching for ways to disentangle himself, by turns and sometimes painfully, from destructive systems. The moral goal of a human being, he often argued, should be to do less harm right now. When one of his most famous essays, 1987’s “Why I Am Not Going to Buy a Computer,” resulted in astonished and outraged letters from readers, Berry replied by writing, “I did not say that I proposed to end forthwith all my involvement in harmful technology, for I do not know how to do that. I said merely that I want to limit such involvement, and to a certain extent I do know how to do that.” This response boils down to a simple, radical worldview: We must seek to understand the harms caused by the systems we’re implicated in, then strive to become less reliant on them.

[From the February 1992 issue: A jonquil for Mary Penn]

Berry was no purist. He understood that no 20th- or 21st-century American can live a wholly blameless life. He lamented needing to drive a truck, the unavoidable, occasional use of gas-powered equipment on his farm. He knew that his ancestors had enslaved their fellow human beings and that he, in direct and indirect ways, had benefited from that obscene bondage. “I am owned by the blood of all of them,” he wrote in an early poem, “My Great-Grandfather’s Slaves.” “We cannot be free of each other.”

Moral blamelessness isn’t possible. But Berry’s larger point was that people should strive for something better, here and now. As he put it in Our Only World, with his trademark aphoristic brilliance: “Maybe we could give up saving the world and start to live savingly in it.” This reframes Jesus’s “sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof” as a simple, clarifying injunction: Forget the future, and work toward what is good today.

I did speak with Berry a second time. We talked a little while about the Sermon on the Mount, but I didn’t get him to agree to a formal interview. I could tell he wanted to help me, to be a sport. Still, I sensed something else nagging at him: the irresistible presence of the now. He was here, living on this Earth. He had land to tend to, animals to care for, a wife to love, books to write—more than 50 of them, by the end. Maybe someday, in some dimly glimpsed future, he would find some more time to talk with me. But right then, at that moment, there was just too much to do.

When J. D. Vance was asked at a press conference on Thursday about his relationship with Tucker Carlson, the media personality and ultra-right-wing conspiracy theorist, he displayed a surprising side of himself: human, vulnerable, almost touchingly naive.

“Tucker is a friend, despite the fact that we disagree on a number of things,” the vice president said. “And I’m not going to play the game where I throw friends under the bus because I have political disagreements with them. I think that is a terrible place for the United States to go. And if you have a disagreement with somebody politically, even over a deeply held issue, you should still be friends with that person, because the whole republic depends on people of different perspectives being able to talk to one another.”

This was a matter not of politics, Vance was at pains to emphasize, but of friendship. If Vance were to renounce Carlson over a matter as trivial as, say, Carlson’s oddly positive assessment of Adolf Hitler, what hope is there for society? The bonds of affection between Vance and Carlson are the very glue holding together the American experiment.

And yet, tempting though it might be to imagine Vance clasping hands with the children of the world and teaching them to sing in perfect harmony, some notes of skepticism are warranted. Perhaps Vance’s affection for Carlson has more to do with holding the loyalty of his friend’s enormous audience.

[Peter Wehner: What J. D. Vance once knew]

For one, Vance is not known for cordially disagreeing with people. He has accumulated an unusual number of bitter enemies by flinging insults at people—including Senate Democrats (“You’re full of shit, and everyone knows it”), reporters (“scumbags”), and “childless cat ladies.” Last month, Vance hurled himself into an obscure debate over burrito prices to make a gratuitous swipe at a conservative Washington Post columnist’s weight.

At the very same press conference where he waxed so earnestly about open dialogue, Vance mocked the idea that he would talk to former President Biden at the 9/11 ceremony (“I’m really looking forward to hanging out with Joe Biden. I’m sure that we’ll have many, many intense intellectual conversations, and it’ll be great”) and dismissed the Michigan Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed as “a crazy person.”

Vance’s dig at El-Sayed, and the context in which he made it, revealed a lot more about his reasons for maintaining the alliance with Carlson than did his encomium to friendship. Vance was trying to illustrate that he and Carlson do not agree on everything. Carlson had recently praised El-Sayed as “America First” and “literally Trumpian,” apparently calculating that the candidate’s anti-Zionism outweighs his many left-wing positions.

That view was the one Vance chose to highlight as his point of disagreement, rather than several other controversial remarks Carlson has made. Examples include but are not limited to his endorsement of “Great Replacement” and other conspiracy theories, his expressions of sympathy for Russia and its war against Ukraine, and his sympathetic interviews with figures such as Nick Fuentes, Alex Jones, and the World War II–revisionist historian Darryl Cooper, who called Winston Churchill “the chief villain of the Second World War” and described much of the Holocaust as mistaken deaths resulting from poor planning.

All of these statements have left Carlson teetering at the edge of respectability within the Republican Party. He has become one of the most prominent exponents of a strain of right-wing populism that flirts with and in some cases embraces isolationism, anti-Semitism, and general crackpottery. Vance has courted this faction of the right. He and Carlson have a partnership that has lasted nearly a decade and has yielded dozens of interviews.

In the course of this partnership, they may well have formed a genuine friendship. But Vance has discarded plenty of friends as he has cycled through various political identities. The difference between Vance’s ex-friends and Carlson is that Carlson is still a useful ally in a Republican factional conflict. When Carlson upset Fox News in 2021 by endorsing the Great Replacement theory, which holds that Democrats are using illegal immigration to replace the current electorate and cement power, Vance wrote on social media, “Tucker Carlson is the only powerful figure who consistently challenges elite dogma—on both cultural and economic questions. That is why they try to destroy him. Don’t fall for it this time, or any other.”

Vance went on to endorse the same theory in advance of the next midterm elections. “Democrat politicians who have decided that they can’t win reelection in 2022 unless they bring in a large number of new voters to replace the voters that are already here” are orchestrating lax border enforcement, he said on Carlson’s show that year.

The Carlson-Vance partnership has generated more conservative blowback over the past few years as Carlson has progressed from skepticism of Israel into anti-Semitism, which Carlson denies. Traditional Republicans who have supported, or at least gone along with, the party’s attacks on Muslims and immigrants and its rejection of democratic norms have spoken out against cracking the door open for anti-Semites. Building an authoritarian, ultranationalist party that marinates in populist conspiracy theories but that abhors anti-Semitism turns out to be tricky.

[Helen Lewis: Carlson and Vance—two smart guys who play dumb for power]

Vance, heading into his expected 2028 presidential campaign, has a strong incentive to try to harness this right-wing energy. His political interests would be served if Republicans were to stop attacking Carlson and if Carlson were to tone down his criticism of the GOP. Hence, he seemed less concerned that Carlson had crossed a moral line than that, in the Michigan Senate race, he had crossed party lines. Carlson praising a member of the Democratic Party is more threatening to Vance than Carlson praising an apologist for the National Socialist German Workers’ Party.

At this week’s press conference, the reporter asked Vance, “What do you say to those who are uneasy with your close relationship with Carlson and claim that you’re avoiding addressing a lot of these issues, which may be causing divisions within the GOP?”

“What a travesty it is that in the United States of America, we let political disagreements define who our friends are going to be and not going to be,” Vance said. “So, yeah, Tucker is a friend of mine.”

Suppose Carlson decided to support someone other than Vance for president in 2028. Does anybody think their friendship would survive?

Construction is notoriously slow-going work. But in Donald Trump’s Washington, cranes and bulldozers appear like DoorDash deliveries: fast, frictionless, and on demand.

To build the president’s White House ballroom, roughly 250 workers have been pouring concrete and installing steel framework, keeping the site humming for 20 hours a day, seven days a week. The National Park Service has proposed a similarly intensive schedule for Trump’s planned triumphal arch: Construction will begin, Interior Secretary Doug Burgum announced on Thursday, in the next two weeks. And officials are trying to shut down the Kennedy Center for two years to carry out renovations that consultants say will take twice as long if the building remains open during the work. These are very different and ambitious projects, but they share the same presumed deadline: January 20, 2029.

They also share a risk: The compressed schedules could lead to shoddy work that will require more repairs later.

Trump has less than three years to complete his transformation of Washington, D.C., before his projects fall into—and perhaps die in—the hands of a successor. “Nobody will do it once I’m gone. When I leave here, nobody will,” he told New York magazine. The next president, whomever they are, likely won’t share Trump’s enthusiasm for gold finishes and Italian marble. To get the work done, his administration has found ways to circumvent the procedural obstacles that tend to slow federal projects.

Construction—one of the world’s least productive industries—may present its own paradox. Research has shown that long hours, additional shifts, and overlapping work tasks can shorten a project’s timeline—but only to a point. In many cases, the effort to build faster can invite new delays, higher costs, and revisions. Trump’s botched repairs to the Reflecting Pool ahead of the nation’s 250th-birthday celebrations, in July, offer a classic example of the problem.

The ballroom project, a hulking party venue that overshadows the White House’s main building and its modest neoclassical design, could be another. The Supreme Court ruled 5–4 this week that its construction could continue, saying that the preservationists trying to halt the project did not have standing to sue. The three liberal dissenting justices—along with, notably, Chief Justice John G. Roberts—argued that the construction was “likely unlawful.” But government filings claim that it’s already 65 percent complete—even as project leaders have continually revised design plans. By the time a new lawsuit from a challenger with stronger standing advances through the courts, how far along will the ballroom be?

[Read: The Court’s ballroom decision is a sign of deeper problems]

“We have hundreds of happy construction workers in D.C. busily doing their job building the finest Ballroom/Military complex anywhere in the World,” Trump wrote on Truth Social this week after the Supreme Court decision.

With his lawyers saying that the project will be “substantially” finished by November, the clock is ticking fast.

Trump’s building spree presents a tricky calculation: The returns he wants are about time rather than cost. But speed in construction can get expensive fast, especially for a project whose anticipated costs have been hard to nail down. When Trump announced the ballroom plans in July 2025, he put the price at roughly $200 million, to be paid for by private donors. By October, he’d knocked down the East Wing of the White House without seeking any approvals. As the work has started, stopped (because of the preservationists’ lawsuit), and started again, the original estimate has tripled to $600 million, and taxpayers are expected to cover half of that amount, according to internal documents from the ballroom’s general contractor, Clark Construction, that were obtained by The Washington Post.

Those projections could rise again. “Accelerated construction is always at higher cost, no matter what you do,” Awad Hanna, the chair of the construction-engineering and -management program at the University of Wisconsin at Madison, told me. There are essentially three ways to make construction move faster, according to Hanna: Workers can put in overtime, contractors can add second or third shifts, or the project can resort to overmanning—that is, adding an unusually large number of workers to a site. Each method may buy some time but sacrifices some productivity, Hanna said.

Researchers have documented that trade-off for decades. A 1997 study published by the American Society of Civil Engineers examined 121 weeks of labor-productivity data from four industrial projects and found efficiency losses of roughly 10 to 15 percent for 50-to-60-hour workweeks.

Hanna’s own research has found similar patterns. With overmanning, the problem is that there’s only so much room for specific tasks to be performed. As the site fills up with workers, they can find themselves competing for tools, materials, and information—or simply getting in one another’s way. Meanwhile, overtime can lead to fatigue for workers who are spending 10 to 12 hours on their feet, carrying heavy objects while also needing to remain diligent about their own safety.

There is another way to speed up construction without adding hours or workers: starting construction before a design is complete. The industry calls this fast-tracking. But a 2023 study found that construction professionals perceived the fast-track strategy as posing a different set of risks, including construction errors and productivity declines, when better productivity is supposed to be the whole point.

A certain amount of secrecy surrounds the White House worksite, in part because of a large military structure that is being built underground, beneath where the ballroom will stand. The methods to speed up the work are unclear, although the teams behind the project are reputable: Clark Construction is a seasoned federal contractor, and the architectural firm Shalom Baranes Associates repaired the Pentagon after 9/11. A White House spokesperson told me that the project is proceeding with the 20-hour schedule for now.

In April, Trump turned his focus to the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, a century-old landmark that has long struggled with leaks and algae blooms. Trump first claimed that he could complete a repair project in a week for less than $2 million. “I have a guy who’s unbelievable at doing swimming pools,” he told reporters at the time. The costs climbed to more than $16 million, which included the installation of a waterproof liner tinted in a color that he called American-flag blue. But shortly after the pool reopened, in June, algae returned in force and the sealant peeled, leading crews to repeatedly drain and scrub the basin over the summer. Now it sits completely empty and fenced off. Trump accused vandals of damaging the pool, but his own U.S. attorney for D.C. refuted those claims, pointing to the contractor’s failures and the Interior Department providing “less than fulsome information at the outset of the case.” Federal officials recently hired a new contractor to deal with the algae.

Trump has already cleared one obstacle blocking the ballroom. But the legal case is not over. Following the emergency ruling, the government still has to formally ask the Supreme Court to hear its appeal. And the National Trust for Historic Preservation, which brought the suit against Trump, can continue a fight in the lower courts.

For each of Trump’s Washington projects, officials have beefed up their justifications in one way or another in the face of legal challenges. In the administration’s view: The Kennedy Center may have to be “taken down” if its planned two-year shutdown for renovations is blocked. (Critics have pushed back on officials’ claims that the building’s conditions are existentially dire.) The Reflecting Pool’s needed repairs were urgent enough to exempt them from the typical competitive-bidding process. (The urgency was the July 4 celebrations.) And now? The White House ballroom is needed for security reasons, because it’s connected to a military bunker.

[Read: The Kennedy Center has a problem that courts can’t fix]

The administration has also pointed to the ballroom’s progress in arguing against an injunction, raising a larger question about whether speed itself has become a form of power in Trump’s building spree. If a president can build faster than Congress or regulators can review, legal constraints may matter less in practice—not because they have disappeared but because enforcing them comes too late. This week’s Supreme Court ruling has already prompted at least one federal judge to consider its implications for another Trump project. Yesterday, U.S. District Judge Carl J. Nichols ordered lawyers representing the Interior Department to address how the Court’s ballroom decision affects its pending motion to dismiss a lawsuit challenging work on the Reflecting Pool.

When asked about the start of excavations for the triumphal arch, the White House spokesperson said in an email, “Work will begin that does not require approval, but rather helps in the process. We will of course continue on our path of seeking final approval from all applicable entities.” The arch still needs final approval from the National Capital Planning Commission, to which Trump has added loyalists. But the administration has also been working to water down historic-preservation rules governing federal projects just as the arch has become subject to that process. Meanwhile, the Kennedy Center—which Trump also controls—cannot start its renovation until a judge signs off on the latest plan. But this week, the center removed a prominent artwork near the Potomac River, a sign that top-down change has begun.

All three projects—the ballroom, the arch, and the Kennedy Center—may soon be moving forward simultaneously, red tape cleared (or ignored). But sped-up construction can impose its own obstacles. When unexpected delays crop up, even presidents have to wait.
