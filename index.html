Perhaps Connecting Fridges to the Internet Was a Bad Idea

Bags of frozen blueberries and Swedish meatballs, packets of waffles and pizzas, and tub after tub of ice cream: all melted, floppy, spoiled, and stinking. This has been the scene at grocery stores across Europe after their fridges and freezers have been hacked.

Last month, employees at hundreds of supermarkets in France came into work to find they had to trash millions of dollars’ worth of food. The machines were not simply deactivated; they had been remotely hijacked and reprogrammed to hit temperatures as high as 122 degrees Fahrenheit. Last Monday, grocery outlets in at least seven more countries, including Estonia, Belgium, and Sweden, experienced a near-identical incident. The same day, freezers at the French national blood bank were also hacked (though staffers were able to intervene before any samples were corrupted). Depending on who you talk with, America is either next or has already been hit: In August, the Pentagon quietly revealed that freezers at commissaries on several U.S. military bases had malfunctioned in a suspected attack.

No one has claimed responsibility for any of these exploits, and it’s not even clear whether they are connected. The hackers seemingly didn’t have a goal besides sowing chaos and wasting an enormous amount of food. Perhaps the most surprising aspect of these widespread fridge attacks is that they haven’t happened sooner. For hackers, these appliances make perfect targets.

The basic problem is that many fridges and freezers are now connected to the internet. “The attacks are very simple,” Nadiya Kostyuk, a cybersecurity researcher at Carnegie Mellon University, told me. Each unit has a microcontroller that is connected to the local network, so even someone sitting at a computer thousands of miles away can theoretically access it. These devices are typically password protected, but they still tend to be pretty easy to crack, Kostyuk explained. “Usually it’s done by brute force,” which is to say the devices are spammed with password after password until one works. Some of the recent attacks were made simpler because usernames and passwords had never been changed from the manufacturer’s defaults. Once the hackers remotely broke into a system, they were able to take control of a heating element that can be periodically switched on to prevent frost from building up on the evaporator coils, which stops the freezer from working as effectively. Instructed to run repeatedly, that heating element has proved capable of cooking perishable food.

For a home fridge, the benefits—a touchscreen that can show family photos, an interior camera so you can see what you already have while you’re at the grocery store—may not outweigh the security risks. In the aftermath of the recent attacks, people have assumed the same is true for industrial fridges: Why on earth does a supermarket fridge need to be online? But for the millions of supermarket chill cabinets, refrigerated trucks and shipping containers, and gigantic cold-storage warehouses that our food sits in on its way from farm to table, the calculus is quite different.

Logistics companies are often required to demonstrate that food has stayed within a narrow temperature range while in their custody; the ability for a freezer to broadcast an alert if its temperature deviates from a set point at 2 a.m. is an extremely useful feature. Refrigeration units are always on—they have to run 24/7 by design—and a lot of today’s smartest devices provide the diagnostics that allow companies to perform predictive maintenance to preempt any potential issues. “The risk of loss is so high if the unit goes down,” Chris Gaffney, a supply-chain expert at Georgia Tech who previously ran logistics for Coca-Cola, told me.

The food that our fridges are protecting is extremely fragile; the machines themselves are quite complex; and qualified technicians are in short supply. A refrigerated warehouse, let alone a supermarket, won’t have a person with that kind of specialist skill set on-site, Gaffney said. But when connected to the internet, a scattered portfolio of freezers can be managed and sometimes even fixed remotely. And, as politicians push legislation to make food traceable, the entire supply chain is being digitized. “There are a lot of positives that come with that—ways to increase our food security and understanding where food is coming from,” Sara Stickler, the president of the Global Cold Chain Alliance, an industry trade group, told me. “But with digitization, there also comes risks.”

The food business isn’t like banking, where online security is central to a company’s value proposition. In perishable logistics, where the mission is keeping food cold, warehouse operators and grocery stores have not typically seen the value in spending extra on cybersecurity. “Margins are tough,” Gaffney said. When it comes to adopting sophisticated new technology, he told me, “logistics is a laggard, and within logistics, cold is a laggard.”

The genius and horror of hacking refrigeration is that although the cold chain is essential infrastructure, it’s not treated as such. As I quickly learned while writing Frostbite, my recent book on the topic, almost no one has a complete picture of refrigeration’s scope and scale. Nearly three-quarters of everything on the American plate passes through the cold chain on its way to our tables. Thousands of chilled warehouses, refrigerated containers and trucks, meat lockers, and banana-ripening rooms are located out of sight, underground, at ports, near freeway intersections or cattle ranches: Added together, these pockets of artificial winter form the invisible backbone of our food system.

Meanwhile, refrigeration is somehow different from other crucial infrastructure—water-treatment plants, say, or power stations—where the public may be ignorant, but at least industry members and government officials are on top of how many facilities we have and where they are. As supermarket shelves emptied during the early days of COVID, one industry expert told me that he received a frantic call from a senior official in the government asking how many refrigerated trucks and warehouses the country’s food supply depended on.

If there is a silver lining to these attacks, it is that they could serve as a wake-up call. “With these cyberattacks, it totally redefines what ‘critical infrastructure’ is,” Kostyuk said. After all, cyberattacks are not the only threat facing our food-supply chain: Dozens of supermarket refrigerators across the U.K. failed this summer during a heatwave that pushed them beyond their limits, and severe-weather-induced blackouts are a constant threat. Whether the culprit is a hacker or the weather, the outcome remains the same. Get used to seeing more empty supermarket shelves.

On SNL, the Struggle (For Power) Was Real

This season of Saturday Night Live has gotten a lot of mileage from inaction. Week after week, sketch after sketch—as James Austin Johnson’s version of Donald Trump interrupts whatever comedy might be at play—the show’s performers have done more than merely step aside as the president storms the stage. Instead, his presence leaves them comically petrified: frozen, at pretty much the moment of his entrance, unable to do anything else.

Like much of what plays out on live TV, the gag works because, so often, it doesn’t quite; half the fun is watching stars such as Kenan Thompson, temporarily transformed into audience members, trying to stifle their laughter while Johnson Trumps it up. The freeze-frame device also works, though, because of its upstager in chief. Few jokes capture the president’s scene-stealing capabilities better than ones that find Trump like a modern-day Medusa, turning everyone in his path to stone. There’s a fine line, however, between running joke and crutch—and it’s one that SNL has frequently crossed. Only five episodes into the current season, the freeze-frame bits have become notably tepid.

But on last night’s episode, the freeze frames were not just the point; they made a point. The cold open was an inevitable recap of the midterm elections: the blue wave that, on Tuesday, swept the country. The sketch’s action began in expected fashion, with the Senate Democrats Hakeem Jeffries (played by Thompson) and Chuck Schumer (Sarah Sherman) celebrating, and analyzing, their party’s landslide victory. How did they do it? What was the source of their historic rebuke to Trump, the MAGA coalition, and the political status quo?

“Me!” said Trump, storming the stage. Johnson’s version of the president blathered (about Millennial cringe, about the long-gone sitcom New Girl, about men’s backsides); Colin Jost, as Pete Hegseth, at one point smarmed onto the scene. All along, Jeffries and Schumer were relegated to the background, present but vacant: two power players rendered awkwardly inert.

The staging made sense—because the upstaging made sense. The sketch, dominated by Trump even in his moment of loss, hinted at Democrats’ famed ability to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory. It also visualized the fickle workings of American party politics. Power, particularly in this moment, is largely a function of attention: a matter of which politician can occupy the forefront of voters’ screens and minds. The sketch, as each character jockeyed for eyeballs, managed to turn that reality into comedy. American politics is a battle of attrition; last night, on SNL, the (power) struggle was real.

The episode’s host made that opening salvo especially apt. The comedian Druski has made a specialty out of skewering the politics of race (which he brought with him to the show, including to a sketch co-starring Andrew Dismukes as a fictional Atlantic reporter) and rose to fame posting comedy videos to Instagram. He is the first digital creator to host SNL. His episode’s cold open fittingly, and elegantly, mimicked the workings of the phone screen and the brute dynamics of the digital economy. Images, vying for primacy: That’s how information works now. It’s how culture works now. It’s how politics work now. Candidates battle not just to convince voters that their way is the right way but also to simply be there, crowding out the other contenders—undeniable, unavoidable, and ever in the foreground.  

The sketch, in recognition of that, anointed a new scene stealer: Senator-Elect James Talarico, whose victory this week helped turn Texas blue. Operating in another long-standing SNL tradition, Talarico made a surprise cameo, closing out the sketch by upstaging everyone else, literally and symbolically. The lame-duck executive, the leaders of the legislative establishment—for the first time in a long time, they were relegated to the background as the young politician talked about a new day in American politics.

“It’s clear people want something new,” Talarico announced, grinning, as SNL’s live audience whooped in approval. But true change—real transformation—is more easily scripted than done. And before Talarico shouted the customary “Live from New York” tagline, he ended his appearance with a joke that, ironically but perhaps appropriately, upstaged his own message. Americans want something different, he said, before getting to the punch line: “I’m talking, of course, about SNL’s cold opens.”

The Democrats’ Fight Over Israel Is Just Beginning

During a swing through Detroit in the final days of the midterm campaign, former President Obama invoked the kind of gauzy pluralism that Democrats were so proud of during his presidency. He pointed out that Michigan had the opportunity to pull off an improbable feat: By electing Abdul El-Sayed to join Elissa Slotkin in the Senate, the state could turn its delegation into a walking symbol of buried enmities and transcendent harmony. “You’ve got a Muslim man, you’ve got a Jewish woman fighting for you in the United States Senate,” Obama said. “That’s something, Michigan, you can be proud of.” 

It took exactly a week for that optimism to run aground on the rougher shoals of modern Democratic politics. The following Sunday, Representative Haley Stevens withdrew her backing of El-Sayed and threw it to his opponent, the Republican Mike Rogers, citing, in part, El-Sayed’s stance on the issue that has divided Democrats more than any other this year: Israel. The abrupt reversal didn’t prevent El-Sayed from winning decisively. But that outcome masked the apprehensions that I heard again and again this week from Michigan Democrats about the potential for tensions over the Middle East to rupture the broad coalition that the party assembled to win in 2026. 

By the time I sat down with a group of Democratic activists at a red-sauce joint in Ann Arbor, on Election Night, the first bottle of wine was already empty. Nearly everyone at the table had voted for El-Sayed, who, hours later, would go on to make history as the first Muslim elected to the U.S. Senate. But no one was gloating. If anything, the Jewish Democrats sharing bruschetta and pasta seemed a little shell-shocked, groggily emerging from a bitter Senate campaign and reckoning with what they described as one of the most difficult votes they had ever cast. 

Rubini Naidu for The Atlantic
Annie Wolock, Cindy Bank, and Beth Bashert gathered on Election Night in Ann Arbor, Michigan.

Everyone at the table wanted the same thing: to see Democrats retake control of the Senate and repudiate President Trump. None of them, however, was particularly excited to elect a senator who seeks to make Israel a pariah, who speaks about Zionism in a way that they said makes Michigan Jews unsafe, and who campaigned with Hasan Piker, the far-left streamer who has said that “America deserved 9/11.” In other words: They did not want to elect a senator like the candidate whom all but two of the people around the table had just voted for. “It’s hard to express the depth of heartache that’s happening here on the ground,” Decky Alexander, who organized the dinner of 11 people, told me. A theater professor, a vice chair of the state Democratic Party, and the leader of its Jewish caucus, Alexander voted for El-Sayed with certitude but not excitement. She views the far right as more of a problem to the country—and to American Jews—and her husband, Bruce Kutinsky, believes that the far left is a bigger threat. He reluctantly voted for Rogers, the Republican. 

[Read: The Hasan Piker question]

Even as the group eagerly discussed the anticipated Democratic gains nationwide, there was a plaintive quality to their conversation about El-Sayed’s impending victory. Earlier that evening, the Ann Arbor–school–board member Rima Mohammad had none of that apprehension as she reflected on the Senate race outside a polling place across the street from Michigan Stadium. The 46-year-old pharmacist spoke softly, her voice scratchy after a long weekend of get-out-the-vote efforts. Mohammad, a Palestinian American whose grandparents were forcibly displaced from their homes in what is now Israel, in 1948, came to Ann Arbor for a residency at the University of Michigan, where she now teaches. Mohammad grew up poor in Cincinnati, part of the only family of color in a very conservative neighborhood. She told me that a victory by El-Sayed would be “life-changing,” especially for her four kids to see. “It’s not even about being Muslim,” Mohammad explained. A win would show that “elections are not gonna be bought” by corporations and groups such as AIPAC, she said, and “also that hate is not going to win.” 

The Israeli-Palestinian conflict has been perhaps the biggest and ugliest point of division within the Democratic Party in the three years since the October 7 Hamas attacks on Israel and the resulting war in Gaza. President Biden, a self-proclaimed Zionist who viewed Israel as both an essential security partner to the U.S. and a country with similar values, pledged American support for the war against Hamas—prompting a backlash by progressive activists. A protest movement led to more than 100,000 Democrats voting “uncommitted” in Michigan’s 2024 presidential primary to register their discontent. President Trump received more votes than Kamala Harris in Dearborn, the heart of Michigan’s Arab American community, which historically votes for Democrats.

This year, the Democratic Senate primary in Michigan became the main battleground for the party’s internal fight over the U.S. relationship with Israel. Stevens defended Israel as an “imperfect” ally, one that is America’s “truest friend” in the Middle East. El-Sayed has made clear that he does not view Israel as an American partner. He called the country “bloodthirsty” and said that, like Iran, it is a threat to the United States—rhetoric that some Jewish Michiganders worry might open the door to anti-Semitic elements on the left. After El-Sayed won the primary and Stevens backed him, the issue faded and the party appeared to be united, particularly amid Republicans’ overtly Islamophobic attacks on El-Sayed. Stevens’s last-second pivot to Rogers resurfaced an issue that Democrats will not be able to ignore for much longer.

Democrats who are about to be in control of Congress now need to figure out how to handle divisions over Israel and Gaza that show no signs of being mended. There will be a “real battle for the soul of the party” as a number of Israel critics arrive in Washington, David Kramer, an insurance executive who served as chair of Stevens’s primary campaign, told me. El-Sayed is already talking about the issue. In his victory speech late Tuesday, he spoke about protecting Palestinian children from “being murdered by our tax dollars” and said that Michiganders “don’t want our money sent to do apartheid or genocide or war.” He took a moment to taunt AIPAC: “Thank you for giving me the opportunity to beat you twice.” 

Jewish and Muslim voters alike told me this week that they see El-Sayed’s election as a pivotal moment in Democrats’ evolution on the once-marginal issue of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict. Democrats have indisputably moved left over the course of the Gaza war; all but seven Democratic senators voted earlier this year to block certain weapons transfers to Israel, a tally that would have once been unthinkable. (Republicans, though still more reliably pro-Israel than Democrats, have also shifted away.) The question is whether El-Sayed will succeed in moving Democrats even further to the left, or whether the party, with a slew of new lawmakers and a surprisingly large Senate majority, will be able to find room for a wide spectrum of beliefs.

[Read: The anti-Democrat]

Many Muslim officials and activists see El-Sayed’s election as a banner moment for pro-Palestine activism. On Tuesday morning, outside a 100-year-old redbrick elementary school in Dearborn that served as a polling place for many in the city’s large Arab American community, Alabas Farhat sprinkled his good mornings with Arabic phrases as he greeted voters. The 26-year-old state representative from Dearborn said he cared less about El-Sayed being the first Muslim senator than about him being an “‘America First,’ ‘Michigan First’ U.S. senator.” El-Sayed maintains that Washington’s financial support for Israel’s war in Gaza—which left tens of thousands dead after Hamas militants attacked Israel in 2023 and killed approximately 1,200 people—means less tax money is available for health care and education back home in America. The message resonates for Farhat, whose family is from southern Lebanon. “He’s going to speak out when something is wrong in a way that the status quo will not know how to handle,” Farhat told me.

State Representative Alabas Farhat

El-Sayed understands that Democrats’ attitudes toward Israel have shifted. In a June AP/NORC poll, 58 percent said that the U.S. was too supportive of Israel, up from 45 percent in early 2024; 62 percent of Democrats said that the U.S. was not supportive enough of Palestinians. Michigan Jews understand these trends too. They’re just divided about whether to cheer them—as a mostly young, progressive contingent has done—or try to reverse them. Hannan Lis, a businessman who splits his time between the Detroit metro area and Israel, has supported Democratic politicians for years. This year, he raised money for the Democrat Jocelyn Benson, the new governor-elect—and also held a fundraiser for Rogers. Lis believes that Israel faces “an existential threat,” and he worries that Democrats don’t care. “I’m not optimistic about how the party is going to deal with this,” he told me in a phone call from Israel this week. 

Josh Whinston also does not feel hopeful. The Reform rabbi told me on Election Night in Ann Arbor that he worries that El-Sayed’s rhetoric—particularly when he calls Israel “bloodthirsty” and seems to pin the American cost-of-living crisis on U.S. support for the country—could lead people to take out their frustration on Jews, many of whom have deep personal and religious connections to Israel. “For me, this has only been about Jewish safety in Michigan,” Whinston, who declined to share whom he voted for, said. Several Jewish Democrats told me that they were alarmed by an anti-Rogers ad during the final days of the election that showed an American couple at a grocery store saying they can’t buy eggs, because that money has to be used for “killing Palestinians.” El-Sayed has said he does “not agree” with the ad, which was made by an outside group.

El-Sayed has made other attempts to reassure his Jewish critics. Perhaps the lowest point for his relationship with skeptical Jewish Democrats came in March, when he condemned a terrorist attack at a synagogue near Detroit but also said that “hurt people hurt people,” noting that the perpetrator’s family had been killed by Israeli air strikes. To many Jewish voters here, the comment read as justification, or at least undue sympathy. Several months later, after the primary, he told Jewish Democrats that he regretted using those words. He also hired a Jewish outreach director toward the end of the campaign and met personally with many prominent Jewish Democrats, including some—such as Whinston and Decky Alexander—who were skeptical of him. The meetings didn’t necessarily help. Alexander said that El-Sayed told her that he could win without the Jewish vote, and that he would make up the votes from other minority communities. (A spokesperson for El-Sayed did not respond to a request for comment.) 

[Read: What El-Sayed does next matters]

National polling consistently shows that more than two-thirds of Jews vote for Democrats. For many, their faith underscores their support for liberal values. And until recently, support for Israel was generally a no-drama, consensus issue. Jewish Democrats must now contend with the reality that something many of them consider a core belief—maintaining strong ties with the Jewish state—is no longer a given for the politicians in their party. 

Jordan Acker, a Democratic attorney, called me on Tuesday morning as he drove to cast his ballot near his home, in Huntington Woods. Earlier this year, he lost the party’s nomination for a second term on the University of Michigan’s Board of Regents. Acker—whose home and law office were vandalized in 2024 by pro-Palestinian activists who were unhappy that he opposed divesting from Israel—would not tell me whom he planned to vote for. “Either way, tomorrow morning, we’re gonna wake up with a senator-elect who in some way is opposed to our interests and may actually be opposed to listening to our community about its interests,” he told me.

Palestinian American voters say they can relate to the feeling of their opinions not being especially valued by their party. Amer Zahr, an adjunct law professor and comedian who lives in Dearborn, has been a Democrat his whole life. But he wasn’t particularly enthusiastic until Bernie Sanders’s presidential campaign a decade ago marked “the first opportunity we had to have Palestine be visible in a national campaign,” Zahr told me. He pulled up to the Dearborn elementary school in an Audi with a license plate that said PLSTN48. His father was one month old in 1948, the year of Israel’s founding, when Zahr’s relatives were forced out—to Jordan, where Zahr was born. His parents brought him to America as a toddler. 

In El-Sayed, Zahr sees a politician who can emulate Sanders’s ability to be the left-most voice on any particular issue, even if it’s lonely. “He’s going to have to be further than anyone else currently there. He’s going to have to be the standard bearer,” Zahr said. He wants to see an unconditional recognition of Palestinian self-determination from Washington. If El-Sayed needs to filibuster to stop funding weapons for Israel, then Zahr expects him to. 

El-Sayed will join at least 10 freshman Democrats in the Senate in January, including several moderates from traditionally deep-red states such as Kansas and South Carolina. Conventional wisdom says El-Sayed’s new colleagues are more likely to follow the lead of more senior senators than that of a freshman from the Rust Belt when figuring out how to vote on foreign-spending packages. But in this new political reality, where wins for Democrats keep pouring in from the most unexpected places, it wouldn’t be a surprise to see El-Sayed push hard for his vision of the world—and for his new colleagues to listen.

The mittens were almost 80 years old when Ethel Dougan painted this watercolor, one of more than 18,000 works by some 1,000 financially strapped artists commissioned by the Index of American Design. Part of the Works Progress Administration’s Federal Art Project, the Index aimed to document historical American arts and crafts and provide inspiration for modern designers.

The mittens had been knitted by the fiancée of Private Franklin Martin Spaulding, an 18-year-old who had enlisted in the 6th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry Regiment in August 1862. He had seen active service through that winter, as had his mittens. The black-and-white photograph archived with Dougan’s drawing shows little more than a gray pile of moth-eaten wool. Her watercolor—now in the National Gallery of Art’s collection with the rest of the Index—brings the mittens back to life. Worn and faded, certainly, they are nonetheless spirited, with a patriotic color scheme, embroidered stars, and rib-stitched stripes. Arranged on the page with fingertips touching, they seem to reach toward each other like children holding hands.

Dougan depicted the seams and rows with enough precision that an experienced knitter could re-create the design, but even a casual observer might note the mittens’ odd silhouette. Nestled between the usual two pouches—a big one for the fingers and a small one for the thumb—is a third. It’s there for the teenage soldier’s index finger, so he could tear the end off a paper cartridge, load his rifle, and pull the trigger in the cold.
