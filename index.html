President Trump gathered his top political advisers in the Oval Office early this month to solve a problem of riches: how to spend his super PAC’s $400 million political war chest with fewer than 100 days to go before the midterm elections.

Republican strategists are concerned about Trump’s low approval ratings and voter anxiety about the economy. One person familiar with the Oval Office strategy meeting put it this way: “When the issues that you typically campaign on don’t move voters—or voters are pissed off at you about those issues—the question becomes: What do you campaign on?” Some GOP strategists favor focusing entirely on specific races in an effort to convince voters of the merits of individual candidates and local issues.

But Trump himself has rejected that approach. House and Senate Democrats, the president noted during the meeting, will make their fall campaigns a referendum on him. His advisers told him that the poor polling reflects the fact that the voting public still does not know about many of his accomplishments—the dozens of new stock-market highs, a decline in fentanyl deaths, the “sealed” southern border, the increase in domestic-manufacturing production.

The solution Trump settled on, according to three people familiar with the meeting, was to lean into the Democratic strategy of focusing on himself. The president ordered a series of television advertisements about his own record that will serve as a backdrop to the hundreds of individual House and Senate contests. Trump wanted what one person described to us as “environmental lift” ads—commercials that would focus on nationalizing the midterms broadly around his victories—and he suggested that the ad makers mine his own on-camera statements as building blocks. “Go look at the clips,” the president told his team, according to this person. “I cite accomplishments all the time.”

That decision has resulted in a Trump midterm-election ad blitz that is set to be rolled out soon from MAGA Inc., the main repository of Trump’s political fundraising, according to people familiar with the discussions, all of whom insisted on anonymity to describe internal conversations. Most of MAGA Inc.’s spending is still expected to go to targeted House and Senate races, for which the group has been establishing teams to review polling, voter modeling, and message testing. “We are going to unload the guns on both chambers real soon,” one of the people said. But another person told us that because Trump will inevitably be part of voters’ considerations, highlighting his achievements makes sense. “I think everybody does understand that the president and his agenda are on the ballot to some degree,” another person told us.

A spokesperson for MAGA Inc. told us that the group does not comment on internal deliberations or plans. Olivia Wales, a White House spokesperson, told us that Trump is committed to maintaining the Republican majorities in Congress. “President Trump is cleaning up Joe Biden’s mess,” she said in a statement. “There is a great story to tell because the United States of America has never been stronger than under his leadership.”

Trump’s decision to focus a portion of his spending on himself echoes President Biden’s decision in the fall of 2023 to launch a series of ads trumpeting his own legislative record, as he sought to increase his approval rating in advance of his reelection bid. But Trump is not on the ballot this fall, which presents new risks. A new strategy of touting Trump’s achievements could well backfire by aiding Democratic efforts to make each race about national issues at a time when Trump’s approval rating is at record lows. Yet given the midterm headwinds, Republicans may have little choice but to present their most effective communicator to voters.

The political climate—an economic election in which voters don’t feel that their life has gotten better, despite some positive indicators—is in many ways similar to what Democrats faced in 2010 and 2022, when the electorate blamed their party for the economic pain of the 2008 financial crisis and the coronavirus pandemic, respectively. But in other ways, the situation Trump faces is different. Polls show that voters aren’t just anxious about inflation; they are frustrated with Trump’s war of choice with Iran and concerned about government corruption. Before Trump’s first midterm election as president, in 2018, the Pew Research Center found that 51 percent of the country believed that the economy was doing well, including 13 percent who described the economy as excellent. By July of this year, only 22 percent felt that the economy was doing well, and only 3 percent described it as excellent. Trump’s economic approval rating hovered at around 50 percent in 2018 public polls. Most polls this year show that his standing has fallen into the 30s.

Republican political consultants have voiced concerns both publicly and privately in recent weeks about the triumphant tone of some of Trump’s economic messaging. Democrats see a familiar dilemma. “You’ve got to take voters from where they are to where you want to go, and the only way to do that is to not dismiss in any way what they know—and what I mean by that is, you can’t go in playing ‘It’s Christmas Time’ when that’s the last holiday they can afford,” Rahm Emanuel, who worked for the Obama and Biden administrations and is a potential contender in the 2028 presidential election, told us. “They’re going to put you on mute.”

In 2010, Barack Obama wrestled with this problem by alighting on a “car in the ditch” metaphor to explain why, after the 2008 financial crisis, the promised recovery still felt slow in coming. Yet even that formulation proved ineffective, Obama’s senior strategist David Axelrod told us. “We sort of abandoned it because what we found was, even that was offensive to people, because it felt like we were trying to blame others,” he said. “What we learned very quickly is, you cannot jawbone people into feeling what they do not feel in their own lives, and Biden defied that to his own detriment.”

Trump has shown much more interest in hyperbole than in complicated metaphors, branding his second term in office as a new “Golden Age.” While stumping last Friday for Senator Darline Graham of South Carolina, Trump spoke glowingly about his second-term. “You have record manufacturing and auto plants being built right now in the United States and in South Carolina,” he said. “We have the greatest economy in our history. Now, some people haven’t seen it yet, but they will.” Axelrod noted that Trump argued at the same rally that his own polling “should be at 100 percent on the economy.” “If you’re the average person out there, you’re seeing that and thinking, What planet is he living on?” Axelrod said. “You have to acknowledge that people are struggling and this is a work in progress.”

David Winston, a Republican strategist who works with congressional leadership, told us that key to the midterms is whether voters, when they walk into a grocery store, feel that they can manage at least a little bit better than before Trump returned to office. “So Republicans need to be talking about what actions they took in terms of the economy to at least get things moving in the right direction, and what the outcome has been,” he said. “This is one where it can’t be a laundry list of 35 talking points. This has to be one or two key things.”

Trump’s advisers are hoping to drive home a discrete set of facts about his accomplishments over the past 19 months. The list is likely to include new tax breaks for certain workers and seniors, the stock market’s rise over the past two years, the military’s boat strikes purportedly targeting fentanyl, and his efforts to “seal” the U.S.-Mexico border, to name a few. Rather than distance himself from congressional elections—the preference of some Republican candidates in swing districts—Trump has argued that he can drive turnout in those races by making himself the center of the conversation. Next month, he will host the Republican Party for a two-day “midterm convention” rally in Dallas aimed at nationalizing the November races as a choice between extreme Democrats and Republican accomplishments. Trump has asked his supporters to treat November as though his name is on the ballot. “What I really want you to do is pretend, please, that I’m on the ballot. Just come and vote. It’s so important,” Trump said on Friday in South Carolina. “Just say that I’m on the ballot one more time.”

In other, important ways, Trump is better positioned than either Obama or Biden were at this point in their midterm races. He has a massive cash advantage, thanks to his aggressive fundraising over the first year of his term that collected tens of millions from crypto companies, AI entrepreneurs, and traditional Republican donors. He also enjoys a sharply gerrymandered political map in the House that has created a new cushion of Republican-leaning seats after mid-cycle redistricting. And the Senate majority will be decided by races in Republican-leaning states such as Texas, Alaska, Ohio, and Iowa.

Trump’s advisers have not yet indicated to Republican consultants how much money the president will spend on the midterms, according to people familiar with the matter. But the merits of Trump inserting himself into the election may be less important to some Republicans than the fact that MAGA Inc. is starting to spend. Some in the party have worried for months that Trump might hoard his fundraising for the 2028 presidential-election cycle—or for something else entirely.

MAGA Inc. has begun work on a number of campaigns about specific House and Senate races for the fall. Much of that advertising is expected to feature negative attacks on Democratic candidates, the traditional role of independent super PACs. MAGA Inc. has commissioned polling and research, along with modeling in the targeted House and Senate races. It has also shared information with other independent groups, including the Senate Leadership Fund and the Congressional Leadership Fund, which are affiliated with Senate Majority Leader John Thune of South Dakota and Speaker Mike Johnson of Louisiana.

In the early spring, Trump’s aides hoped to launch a disciplined sales pitch to voters focused on Trump’s accomplishments, only to have that effort largely derailed by the open-ended Iran conflict. Trump’s approval ratings have eroded further since then. Now is the president’s last chance for a do-over, and his team is projecting confidence that it can get it right. “Anybody that is saying we are headed for a shellacking absolutely can’t just read the numbers,” one of the people familiar with the Oval Office meeting told us. With early voting starting in the first weeks of October, Republicans are once again betting that more Trump is their best way to block Democratic hopes of a wave election.

Dolly Parton’s death at age 80 comes at a time when her influence seems at its peak. Country music is surging, female singer-songwriters are on top, and audiences seem hungry for honest idiosyncracy—for figures who, like Dolly, transmit their inner truth to mass audiences in a voice that’s clear yet scraggly. But Parton’s greatest legacy is existential in nature. Piling on wigs and feathers, she showed that you can be yourself while playing a character.

Parton’s self-construction project began in her childhood. She was born the fourth of 12 kids in a tiny cabin in Pittman Center, Tennessee. Her parents were so poor that they paid the doctor who delivered her with a sack of cornmeal. As a girl, she sang songs on her porch into a fake mic fashioned from a tin can and a tobacco stick, envisioning herself at the Grand Ole Opry. Her talent drew attention from local radio and TV personalities, and by age 13, she was singing on the real Opry stage.

The craftiness and self-consciousness that came from growing up impoverished shaped her outlook for life. As she’d sing in her 1971 song “Coat of Many Colors”—her signature (and favorite) hit—her mother used to sew rags into garments that the young Parton would flaunt at school. Mockery from other kids taught her the lesson in self-determination that she’d turn into one of her defining lyrics: “One is only poor only if they choose to be.” As a young woman, she modeled her fashion off a “town tramp”—as she later put it—whom she’d grown up around. Her preacher grandfather whipped her for dressing that way, but to her, women with heels and push-up bras weren’t trashy; they were glamorous and confident.

A fascination with stereotypes—with fulfilling them and subverting them—gave her an edge when she moved to Nashville. In 1966, her first solo hit, “Dumb Blonde” (written by Curly Putman), saw her singing that despite her looks, she “ain’t nobody’s fool.” On the TV variety series The Porter Wagoner Show, she played the aw-shucks second fiddle to the titular star even as she wrote songs that outcompeted Wagoner’s music. Her 1970 hit “Joshua” spun a tale in which the narrator approaches a mean-looking backwoods hermit, someone whom the rest of the town fears, only to realize that he has a heart of gold. It was an example of how her penchant for social criticism and her interest in flipping expectations made her songwriting stand out.

Dolly Parton at her theme park, Dollywood, in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee (Ron Davis / Getty)

She left Wagoner behind in 1974 to court an audience that transcended country music. Her songs sparkled with fun production touches, clever lyrical twists, and melodies that seemed at once as winding as a lazy river and as smartly constructed as good Levi’s. For 1980’s “9 to 5,” the sound of her fingernail clicks did as much work as the lyrics—and the movie the song accompanied—in celebrating the hustle of women at work. “Jolene,” her enduring ballad of jealousy, built itself around a chorus so plaintive that it seemed surely scraped from a harrowing chapter of Parton’s own life—but she was quick, in interviews, to say that the song’s story was more or less fiction.

As she settled into icon status, she seemed to revel in the franchisability of her image: Dolly Parton was an idea that everyone could benefit from. She founded a theme park in her home state, selling a blend of rustic realness and fun kitsch. She only ever seemed tickled that Whitney Houston’s cover of “I Will Always Love You” eclipsed Parton’s original in eminence. She made childhood literacy the central focus of her philanthropic efforts—another subversion of the “dumb blonde” image (and one that channeled her upbringing by a father who couldn’t read).

In recent years, Parton’s persona has come to take on political significance. She was, as my colleague David Graham has written, the rare public figure who seemed beloved by both red and blue America. She maintained that status through careful circumspection regarding partisan politics. But when it came to questions about sexuality and identity, she took a stand by repeatedly supporting gay, trans, and gender-nonconforming people. After all, she once told RuPaul, she herself was a drag queen.

Many of the most important stories in music this decade owe a lot to Parton. Sabrina Carpenter has brought Dolly-style humor (and hair) back to the charts; Beyoncé enlisted Parton to play emcee on her 2024 album, Cowboy Carter; Ella Langley is blending backwoods scenery with wry, shrugging wisdom that recalls Parton’s catalog. But Parton set an example bigger than music—by showing how to take the scraps you were given and stitch them into something bright and new.

ICE quietly enacted new rules this month that will keep officers involved in deadly-force incidents—such as firing a gun and using a choke hold—off the streets until the completion of an investigation. Previously, these officers were able to return to their regular job in as few as three days. Now they must remain on desk duty until they are cleared by investigators, according to two senior ICE officials with knowledge of the change, who spoke with me on the condition of anonymity because they are not authorized to discuss agency matters with reporters.

Under the old rules, ICE supervisors could lengthen an officer’s period of leave, but such an extension was often viewed internally as signaling a lack of confidence in the officer and their version of events. At ICE and other law-enforcement agencies, officers are typically eager to return to full patrol duties as quickly as possible, and not remain in limbo as an investigation plays out. The change, which has not been publicly announced, means that officers who fire their guns, choke someone, or use some other form of deadly force—even if no one is killed—will potentially be limited to performing administrative duties for much longer periods. Acting ICE Director David Venturella and other senior ICE leaders ordered the change late last month, the two senior officials said.

ICE and the Department of Homeland Security did not respond to my questions about why the policy change was made or how many such incidents have occurred since President Trump returned to office. ICE officers have shot at least 16 people since last January, four of them fatally, according to public records. Last month, there were two fatal shootings during traffic stops: On July 7, ICE officers in Texas killed Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old contractor from Mexico, and six days later, an officer in Maine fatally shot Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero, a 25-year-old Colombian immigrant. DHS said the officers fired in self-defense, but the incidents are under federal investigation.

Veteran ICE officials have worried for months about the competence of the thousands of new recruits who were added during a hiring binge last fall, many of whom were rushed onto the streets after taking a fast-track training course. The ICE officer in Maine, David Brouillette, was a new hire, and his relatives told reporters he had a history of mental-health issues and violent behavior that should have disqualified him from getting a badge and gun.

Violent confrontations during traffic stops particularly concern ICE leaders; the agency has not typically had so many officers pulling over vehicles and attempting to arrest people who are in cars. After the shootings last month, ICE and DHS pledged to expedite the deployment of body-worn cameras for ICE officers. Although most of the cameras have been delivered, the officials told me that they don’t know when all of ICE’s roughly 15,000 deportation officers will be wearing them. After the killing in Maine, Venturella and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin halted the use of nonemergency traffic stops—but Trump reversed the directive when immigration hard-liners said it made the White House look weak. The president has said that ICE officers “make mistakes sometimes” and can be “too rough,” but they’re doing a “GREAT job.”

Arrests by ICE totaled nearly 50,000 in July, the highest level in a single month since Trump returned to office, the officials told me. The agency is no longer using a city-by-city approach to surge arrests, as it did last year under then–Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, and this has led to fewer clashes with protesters. But officers remain under pressure to maximize arrests and deportations, and new videos continue to emerge showing officers tackling immigrants and pointing guns at people who are filming them or are allegedly disobeying their orders.

ICE leaders say they’re looking for ways to reduce the use of deadly force by giving officers more nonlethal tools. The agency is planning to spend up to $20 million on electrified gloves that allow officers to deliver painful shocks. The gloves, which have been used by some police departments and public-school security officers, will be assigned to ICE teams involved in traffic stops and other potential confrontations, according to the officials I spoke with. They told me the shocks delivered through the gloves are painful but less intense and more localized than those from a Taser, and will be easier to direct than chemical sprays.

Prior to the current Trump administration, the use of deadly force by ICE deportation officers in U.S. cities was more rare than shootings by U.S. agents along the Mexico border were, Scott Shuchart, an attorney and a former ICE official who worked at the agency’s Office for Civil Rights and Civil Liberties from 2010 to 2018, told me. The U.S. Border Patrol reduced its annual number of shooting incidents more than a decade ago by hiring independent consultants who recommended giving agents more nonlethal tools and better training. The number of Border Patrol’s firearm-use incidents, measured across fiscal years, fell from 55 in 2012 to eight last year. Shuchart said the administrative-leave extension “seemed like a healthy signal” that ICE leaders knew “they might have a real problem” after the shooting in Maine. “They don’t want to be creating more risk by putting people back out on the street,” he said.

Deborah Fleischaker, a senior ICE official during part of President Biden’s term, told me that the change to ICE’s rules will matter little if internal oversight mechanisms don’t hold officers accountable for wrongdoing. Trump officials gutted offices at DHS that had reviewed complaints against ICE personnel and about poor detention-center conditions. “If the goal is to ensure that people are on administrative leave through the entirety of an investigation, that’s a good goal, but it doesn’t speak to the quality of the investigation,” Fleischaker said.

Fleischaker, now an immigration-policy adviser at the Hispanic-advocacy group UnidosUS, said Trump officials have conveyed a clear message to the federal workforce that “mass deportation is a high-profile goal of this administration and people need to be real careful of getting in the way of anything that slows that down.”

In October, Stephen Miller said on Fox News that ICE officers had “federal immunity” to perform their duties while carrying out Trump’s immigration crackdown. But since then, Democratic leaders in several states have vowed to bring charges against ICE officers for use of excessive force. In Minnesota, state prosecutors charged the ICE officer Christian Castro with four counts of second-degree assault and one count of falsely reporting a crime for his role in the nonfatal shooting of a Venezuelan immigrant on January 14—a day after DHS published a clip of Miller’s “immunity” remarks. Minnesota has been seeking Castro’s arrest and extradition from Texas, but Governor Greg Abbott, a Republican, is challenging the request in federal court. CNN reported today that the Department of Justice is considering filing federal charges against Castro.

Dolly Parton transcended.

It almost didn’t matter what: class, gender, region, musical genre, partisanship. She transcended boundaries in all of them. During an era when politicians act like pop stars, pop stars are expected to engage in politics, and politics divide fiercely, Parton was perhaps the only person in the United States who commanded nearly universal affection. Her death today, at 80, leaves an absence that no contemporary cultural figure could dream of filling.

Parton’s origin story was, in the most literal sense, a rags-to-riches tale. She was born in a one-room cabin in the Appalachian Mountains of East Tennessee, one of 12 children of an illiterate sharecropper father. She memorialized her impoverished childhood in “Coat of Many Colors,” which tells of how her mother sewed a jacket from rags. When she wears it to school, “with patches on my britches / and holes in both my shoes,” her classmates mock and laugh at her. Rather than a hard-luck story, this is a triumph. Parton gets the last laugh: “But they didn’t understand it and I tried to make them see / That one is only poor only if they choose to be.” The song showcases one of the keys to Parton’s success: the ability to bare her soul while keeping a sense of humor and a smile.

Her rise to fame began when she was hired by the rhinestone-adorned country singer Porter Wagoner as a “girl singer” in his act. Parton quickly demonstrated that she had more than an attractive face and voice, writing a string of successful songs, such as “Jolene” and “My Tennessee Mountain Home.” One indication of her songwriting talent was that her most famous song was a bigger hit for another singer. Parton had initially hoped that Elvis Presley would record “I Will Always Love You,” but she refused his manager’s demand to give Presley songwriting credit, and she cut the song herself. It topped the country charts twice, but after Whitney Houston rerecorded the song for The Bodyguard’s soundtrack in 1992, it was a No. 1 pop hit for weeks.

The country singers and collaborators Porter Wagoner and Dolly Parton pose for a portrait with their backup band, the Wagonmasters, circa 1968. Wagoner is wearing a Nudie Suit designed by Nudie Cohn of Nudie’s Rodeo Tailors. (Michael Ochs Archives / Getty)

Today, little separation exists between country music and the mainstream, but the genre garnered much less respect when Parton’s career began. She helped tear down old prejudices in an era when many music listeners might have professed to like “anything other than country.” Her success helped pave a path for later crossover stars such as Shania Twain and Taylor Swift. She pursued her own genre experimentation, too, collaborating with a range of artists. When she was nominated for the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2022, she protested that she wasn’t a rock musician and tried to withdraw from consideration. When the hall refused, she announced with appealing humility that she would earn the honor by making a rock album—her 49th and final—with guests including Joan Jett, Ringo Starr, and Paul McCartney.

Parton always maintained her connection to rural southern music. Few country stars could claim a more authentic origin. She kept her down-home accent and platinum-bouffant hairstyle, and she spoke openly about her abiding Christian faith. These traits may have allowed her to take stands that might otherwise have been controversial. (She also avoided electoral politics, and declined Presidential Medals of Freedom from both Donald Trump and Joe Biden.)

Like her predecessor Loretta Lynn, she cultivated a no-nonsense strain of feminism in her lyrics. In 1980, she starred alongside the outspoken liberals Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin in the movie 9 to 5, a workplace comedy in which three women conspire to get even with their sexist-pig boss. Parton agreed to take the role if she could write the theme song, and the track has proved even more enduring than the film. “It’s a rich man’s game no matter what they call it / And you spend your life puttin’ money in his wallet,” she sang. Was this working-class grievance or progressive economics? Radical feminism or mass-market entertainment? Why not both?

From left: the American actors Jane Fonda, Dolly Parton, and Lily Tomlin on the set of the film 9 to 5, directed by Colin Higgins, in Los Angeles in 1979 (Steve Schapiro / Corbis / Getty)

Parton was beloved by the gay community, and she reciprocated that affection. She also quietly fostered change using her fortune. In 1986, she opened Dollywood in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, and the theme park became the largest job provider in the area. In 1995, she launched Dolly’s Imagination Library. Starting first in her native Sevier County, and then expanding around the United States as well as in a few other countries, the program mails a book each month to children from birth to age 5, to encourage literacy and a love of reading. In 2020, she donated $1 million to help fund the development of a COVID-19 vaccine, then proudly received the shot.

In her middle age, during a perhaps less enlightened time in American society, Parton was often a punch line. Her voluptuous chest and many plastic surgeries were frequent fodder for jokes, and Parton perfected the trick of playing along with the gags while also subverting them. One of her earliest singles is titled “Dumb Blonde,” but it warns, “This dumb blonde ain’t nobody’s fool.” In a 2002 New York Times interview, she said, “Hopefully people can see beneath the hair to know there’s a brain, beneath the boobs to know there’s a heart, and behind all the other stuff to know there’s some talent.”

If that wasn’t always the case, Parton got her wish in the end. Admiration for her became an unshakable conviction across cultural lines. One sign of Parton’s status is that even Trump is on his best behavior, treating her death with a reverence he usually reserves only for his biggest public admirers, of whom she was not one. The president ordered flags flown at half-staff for a week to mark her death. It is a fitting farewell for an artist who united Americans in a way that few people ever have or will.

It was not the three boisterous tweens in the back seat who were clamoring to be set loose in the basketball gym. My first guess drew a warning message in red letters: “Invalid PIN. PIN cannot contain 4 sequential numbers, 2 repeated digits, or the last 4 digits of your phone number.” I searched my trusty password manager, which was supposed to spare me these predicaments, but it came up empty. By that point, the kids had run ahead and piled themselves against the gym door, waiting for me to let them in.
