Last night, in the streets of Austin, Democrats honked their horns and hung out their windows, like it was V-E Day. In the nation’s capital, they blasted “Party in the U.S.A.” near the White House. But in southeast Iowa, the blue revolution came quietly.

The Democrats of Des Moines County—a place that does not actually contain the state capital, but a small, industrial river city and rural areas along the Mississippi—have not come to associate Election Night with joy. The half dozen white-haired Iowans at the Burlington office’s “watch party” were blinking in disbelief at the TV as MS NOW election alerts flashed across the screen. Their party had retaken the governorship of Ohio—and somehow won a Senate seat in South Carolina? Texas was within reach. So, it seemed, was Kansas? “This might be a very big night,” Tom Courtney, a 79-year-old former state senator and former leader of the county party, told me, in what was already sounding like a colossal understatement.

Iowa’s results had yet to come in. He and the other Democrats in the room seemed to be experiencing happiness only distantly, like birdsong from deep within a dark wood. This county, which had elected Barack Obama by about 23 points in 2008, was one of 31 Iowa counties that swung to Donald Trump in 2016. Two years ago, Trump won Des Moines County by 15 points, and Democrats began speaking of the “river counties” in the past tense—pieces of Iowa’s blue wall, lost forever.

Courtney was on the phone. He paced the office, passing lawn signs, a cooling pot of chili, and the final slices of a Casey’s taco pizza. Then, suddenly, gasps. The countywide results were in, and a map of election results showed Des Moines County faintly—yet undeniably—blue. The county’s voters had opted to send a Democrat to the state house and another to the U.S. House; they’d also voted to make Rob Sand the state’s first Democratic governor elected in 20 years. “Did you hear that?” Courtney asked me, from across the room.

By the end of the night, Iowa would also send Josh Turek to the Senate, plus three Democrats to the House. Kansas would send a Democrat to the Senate for the first time since the invention of beer in a can. In Ohio, the party would send Sherrod Brown back to Congress, and Amy Acton to the governor’s mansion. At the office in Des Moines County, Courtney’s wife, Nancy, emerged from the office kitchen with several plastic cups full of room-temperature peppermint schnapps. “We did good,” she told the small group of people, which, despite the cavalcade of thrilling news, was maintaining a measured, midwestern composure. A fortunate combination of variables had put Iowa, and the rest of the Midwest, back on the map for Democrats. The next big challenge for the party will be keeping it there.

War. Tariffs. Gas prices. It’s all I heard at the polls yesterday morning in Iowa, where, like pretty much everywhere else, the midterm elections were a referendum on choices made by President Trump. Even loyal Republicans I spoke with at the polls seemed bummed to be voting for the GOP. “Somebody’s gotta do it,” one man told me outside Burlington’s First Church of Nazarene when I asked how he felt about Republican leadership. Later, a woman on the other side of town told me she was eating mostly toast and tacos to save money. She’d still submitted an all-Republican ticket, she said. “But I didn’t want to vote for any of them.”

Still, to have a night like last night, Democrats needed more than bad atmospheric conditions for Republicans: They needed good candidates. And where it mattered most, they had them. In Iowa, Democrats ran Sand, a centrist who has emphasized frugality and promised to take on corruption. “Right person, right message, right place, right time,” Sandy Dockendorff, a longtime Democratic activist in Des Moines County, told me. Her party also had Turek, a four-time U.S. Paralympian whose compelling backstory and commitment to moderation proved enough to defeat Ashley Hinson, widely considered to be the star Republican recruit of the cycle. In Ohio, Sherrod Brown defeated Jon Husted, despite the latter’s allegedly fantastic posterior. And in Kansas, Democrats managed to get Adam Hamilton, a megachurch pastor who until recently was a Republican, at the top of the slate. It helped that Hamilton’s opponent, the incumbent Senator Roger Marshall, was impressively weak. “In a shitty environment, one way you don’t win races is to combine shitty candidates running shitty campaigns,” a senior—and very bitter—GOP strategist involved in multiple Senate races, said.

The lesson, according to national Democrats, is clear: The winning candidates were “comfortable being a different type of Democrat,” the party strategist Lis Smith told me in a text. Those candidates had worked hard to persuade independents and Republicans, even if “it didn’t always earn them fans online.” Perhaps Smith is referring, in part, to the way that Sand and Turek handled some of the most contentious issues facing the party. Both candidates, for example, ditched the party line to say that transgender girls should not play in girls sports. Last week, Turek released an ad about it that had sent many Democrats sputtering. He won anyway. “Democrats need to get better at winning rural voters,” Adam Jentleson, president of the center-left think tank Searchlight Institute, told me. “Iowa is the testing ground, and Sand and Turek are the models.”

[Read: The simple message that produced a Democratic landslide]

Last night, every county in both Kansas and Iowa moved left. Dockendorff, the longtime Des Moines County activist, had told me over coffee yesterday morning that something like this would happen. (I trusted her because, in 2020, she had been the Cassandra of the caucuses, warning me—correctly—that Iowa’s vaunted nominating contest was likely to devolve into chaos.) “Iowa is turning back to purple,” she said. A year ago, or maybe less, Democratic activists had started filling chairs at county party meetings again. Some young people, too. Energy wise, she said, “there is no comparison to 2018.” This was much, much bigger. River towns, like Burlington, where the decline of unions had hollowed out the party, were showing some potential for Democrats once more. “I hear dissatisfaction from voters here,” she said. “People trusted Trump to do. He hasn’t done.”

This assessment inspired another warning from Dockendorff—for Democrats both in and outside of Iowa. “This is not a coronation of the Democratic Party,” she said. It is an expression of frustration from voters looking for something different. “Our problem will be creating that something different.” Last night, for example, Iowans passed a constitutional amendment that will make it more difficult to raise income taxes, which might make it harder for Iowa’s new leaders to address the state’s budget shortfall. And voters in Des Moines County and others that flipped are not suddenly committed Democrats.

National Democrats have been warning for months that a significant win in November would be overinterpreted by party activists and leaders, who would assume that voters were in love with their message, rather than simply sick of the president’s antics. The Democratic Party remains thoroughly unpopular. With Trump repudiated, albeit not totally in the rearview mirror, Democrats now have the challenge of presenting an affirmative vision for the country. How will they lower gas prices, or the price of milk? How will they talk about transgender issues as a party? Or immigration? Now, Dockendorff said, “the internecine war begins.”

Signs of the coming conflict were on display last night at the watch party in Des Moines County. Sometime after the announcement that Florida Democrats had broken the state’s Republican supermajority but before the schnapps, Ben Diewold, a co-leader of the group, said he hoped that the Democrats wouldn’t try to impeach Trump again. “All that’s gonna do is invigorate MAGA,” he said. “Let Trump fade away.” Nancy didn’t agree. “He needs to be accountable to all that he’s done.”

Soon, though, their conversation was interrupted. MS NOW was projecting that James Talarico had won in Texas. (“TalaFREAKO!” Diewold shouted.) And Rob Sand would lead Iowa.

“I’m glad I’m alive to see it,” Courtney said. He dictated a congratulations text and sent it to Sand. A few minutes later, a couple of the younger staffers, who’d come to work in Iowa from California and New Jersey, left to celebrate at a bar downtown. They asked Courtney to join them, but he declined. He wanted to keep watching.

Over the past few years, Google’s low-priced laptops have become an AI Trojan horse in America’s schools. Students can use a school-issued Chromebook to automate much of their homework. Fire up the Gemini app, and Google’s chatbot will spit out a full essay about the meaning of The Giver. Type an algebra question into the Google search box, and an “AI Overview” will solve it for you. Ask a follow-up question, and the search box might give way to “AI Mode,” which turns the search engine into a full-on chatbot.

Districts across the country give each student their own device, which typically comes equipped with a Google account and a suite of the company’s software. Over the past year, some of the country’s largest public-school districts, including Los Angeles and New York City, have passed policies limiting students’ screen time and banning generative-AI tools. But they hit an obstacle: Google didn’t offer schools a way to fully opt out of AI.

Yesterday, the tech giant’s attempt to address the problem only made people madder. Google’s stand-alone Gemini chatbot will now be disabled by default for school accounts, the company announced. But Google quietly left out any option to turn off its most popular and widely used generative-AI features: those built into its search engine. In other words, the only way for schools to go AI-free is to cut off students’ access to Google Search, the world’s most popular website.

Tech companies threatening to withdraw products rather than modify them has become part of Silicon Valley’s playbook. (Meta once responded to a Canadian law requiring the company to share revenue with news publishers by banning news articles from its platform altogether.) Online, the move drew outrage, including from congressional Democrats who will soon hold newfound legislative and subpoena powers. “Google is once again prioritizing profits over people,” Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut posted. “Clearly, the Tech Giant is more concerned with forcing Gemini into schools than enabling students to succeed.”

Google contended that the anger was based on a misunderstanding. “Today’s announcements give schools more choice, not less,” the company posted on X, evincing a faith in the power of boldface font to get its message across. That much is true. Google has never offered schools an AI-free version of its search engine (though there is a “Web” tab that limits results to links). What was new in Google’s announcement was that both Gemini and Search will now be turned off by default, and schools will have the option to enable one or both. For schools that enable Search, the company is touting a new “guided learning” mode in its AI features that will prompt students to work through questions one step at a time, rather than simply giving them the answer. That could help address one of the biggest objections to Google’s AI search tools in the classroom, which is that they short-circuit the learning process. (A spokesperson for the company declined to comment beyond pointing me to its public statements.)

[Read: My son’s math homework is essentially just Pokémon]

Yet Google’s response to the blowback failed to address the aspect of its announcement that had upset people in the first place. The company wasn’t taking away any options that had existed before, but it also wasn’t providing the one option schools seemed to want most: a way to let students use Google Search without also enabling the AI bells and whistles.

The unspoken logic behind the company’s stance is that, in some sense, all Search is AI search. AI Overviews and AI Mode launched in 2024 and 2025, respectively, but the company has been using large language models to help rank its search results since well before the arrival of ChatGPT. Google also used AI for years to help power the precursors to AI Overviews, including “featured snippets”—those bolded excerpts from web results that Google surfaced atop its list of links. So when Google says that opting out of AI entails opting out of Search, that’s technically true.

Even so, there’s something obtuse about Google’s refusal to acknowledge that consumers—and schools especially—might reasonably draw a distinction between LLMs that help power search results and LLMs that generate answers themselves. Parents and teachers mostly don’t object to Google using AI to decide which webpages to link to when students ask a question (though there is a case to be made that the company’s opaque ranking process has always deserved more scrutiny than it’s received). They object to turning Google into an instant-answer machine—and an error-prone one, at that. They object to students being conditioned to delegate their critical-thinking and source-evaluation skills to a machine. And they object to infusing the same site that indexes the world’s online information with a chatbot that can function as an ersatz friend or dispense personal advice to impressionable children.

Which brings us to the business logic underlying Google’s stubborn stance. The company built its empire around a search tool that offered the shortest path between a user’s question and the information they were seeking. For years, that mostly meant refining its algorithm to downgrade spammy sites in its results. Then came ChatGPT, which promised to answer just about anything a user could ask, dispensing with the list of links altogether. OpenAI claimed this week that ChatGPT now handles about one-fifth as many search queries as Google. Chatbots are the most significant threat to Google’s core business that the search giant has ever faced.

Its response has been to morph Search into an AI-answer tool in its own right. A few discontented users have dropped Google for niche alternatives such as DuckDuckGo, the privacy-focused search engine that now touts AI-free search as a selling point. Kamyl Bazbaz, a DuckDuckGo spokesperson, told me that usage of the company’s “No-AI” search option is up 30 percent since September. The company is now looking for ways to make it easier for schools that opt out of Google Search to switch their default search engine, he added. But Google seems to be betting that convenience will win out over qualms about generative AI. That bet could easily pay off, given how accustomed students already are to the tools Google has provided them with.

While researching this story, I asked Google for data on how many Chromebooks are in U.S. schools. The site responded with an AI Overview confidently asserting that 93 percent of U.S. school districts plan to purchase Chromebooks. In small print, the overview cited one source for that figure: an article from a website called commandlinux.com. Skeptical, I did what fewer and fewer Google users are doing these days: I clicked on the link. The article, which appeared to be AI-written, offers no methodology or original source for any of the figures it provides.

I took those extra steps because I’m a professional journalist who covers AI and its limitations for a living, and because my own schooling trained me to seek out original sources and evaluate their credibility. Had I grown up with AI search, as today’s kids do, I doubt I would have thought to question the number that Google handed me.

Heading into the midterms, everyone who follows American politics had the same question: Were the polls correct, or would they underestimate Republicans once again? The answer turned out to be neither. This time around, the polls undercounted a different kind of voter: Democrats.

According to the Silver Bulletin polling averages, Democrats were on track to win the U.S. House popular vote by nine points and narrowly flip a few Senate seats in states that Donald Trump had won by double digits. Democratic voters across the country saw these numbers and braced for disappointment. They remembered the polling misses of 2016 (which underestimated Trump by about three points on average) and 2024 (which did too) and believed that they could be watching another blue mirage.  

In fact, even this year’s seemingly incredible poll numbers turned out to significantly understate Democrats’ strength. Democrats are now expected to win the House popular vote by 13 points, the largest such victory in 50 years. And that four-point national polling error was small compared with the miss in certain states. In South Carolina, polls underestimated the Democratic candidate by five points on average; in Mississippi, by seven; in Texas, Georgia, and North Carolina, by nine. As a result, what was already expected to be a great night for Democrats turned into a rout of historic proportions.

Polling is hard and getting harder. Americans used to reliably answer surveys. Now simply finding people willing to pick up the phone is an uphill battle. As response rates have declined, so has pollsters’ ability to reach a representative sample of the electorate. In 2016, pollsters underestimated Trump’s support among white voters without a college degree because they couldn’t poll enough of them.

In 2020, the industry made a concerted effort to reach more of those voters. And yet the polls underestimated Trump by even more. Pollsters reasoned that even if they were now reaching the right educational groups, they must be missing certain personality types that were disproportionately likely to prefer Trump. More judgment calls needed to be made. Ahead of 2024, pollsters made a point of ensuring that their samples included enough 2020 Trump voters. That didn’t do the trick either—they managed to underestimate Trump yet again.

This cycle, pollsters introduced even more bespoke solutions. In their jointly run polls, The New York Times and Siena College devised ways to discount respondents they deemed unrepresentative—rural white college-town residents, for example, might not be like other rural white voters—and started weighting respondents based on how Republican their name sounds or what car they drive. YouGov put resources into making sure that it had enough voters from not just purple neighborhoods but deep-red ones too. It also introduced new ways to model turnout, differentiating between a “strict” definition of likely voters and a “loose” one, because “strict” likely voters might include only the most engaged ones, and not the hard-to-reach ones who disproportionately support Trump and might still vote.

[Idrees Kahloon: Why Republicans lost so big]

Pollsters are scientists, but they are also humans. They do their best to avoid biases, and yet they have them anyway. The adjustments they made this cycle were in service of maximizing the accuracy of their surveys, yes, but they also had another goal: not getting burned again. The changes were unsubtly aimed at not overrating the Democrats’ chances. If pollsters had used these new techniques in 2024, they would have more closely predicted the final result. Nate Cohn, the chief political analyst for The New York Times, wrote over the weekend that the paper’s post-2024 weighting techniques made its polls look better for Republicans.

In the past two midterms, in 2018 and 2022, pollsters nailed the final result with little systematic error. That’s probably because the voters that are hardest for pollsters to reach tend not to show up to vote in midterm elections, when the president isn’t on the ballot. By working hard to correct what went wrong in 2024, pollsters were solving for challenges that were less likely to come up this year anyway.

Even so, in one sense, pollsters accomplished their goal: They were not burned by hidden Trump voters. Instead, the polls moved in the opposite direction, especially when it came to some minority groups. Tracking how different groups voted so soon after an election is difficult, but according to a sophisticated new model deployed this year by the Times, Hispanic voters in Texas swung toward Democrats by 42 points—10 more points than the polls predicted. Black voters’ nationwide support for Democrats was underrated by a similar amount. According to the polls, Democrats were set to win young men in battleground states by nine points after losing them badly in 2024. In the end, Democrats seem to have won that demographic nationwide by 24 points.

In the long history of electoral polling, errors have not tended to favor one party over the other. Then, from 2016 to 2024, they did. That they no longer do is perhaps one more sign that the Trump era is ending.

Democrats were jubilant last night, and for good reason: Few midterms in modern American history have produced such a thorough repudiation of a party as the Republicans experienced in this election. But celebrations have a pesky way of being followed by hangovers. For Democrats, the cause of their morning-after headache is affordability.

Across the country, Democratic candidates campaigned and won by railing against Donald Trump’s handling of the economy. When they take control of Congress in January, however, they’ll find themselves with few direct levers to bring down prices. We don’t need to guess what happens to politicians who campaign on affordability and don’t deliver. We saw it last night.

Democrats can hardly be blamed for placing affordability concerns at the center of their campaign, alongside discussion of rampant corruption. This was good electoral politics—you want to run against your opponents’ weakness—and also good republican politics: It’s healthy for candidates to respond to voters’ concerns. The price of orange juice is up nearly 25 percent since Trump returned to office. The price of the candy that trick-or-treaters received on Halloween has risen 71 percent in five years. The average national price of a gallon of gas was more than $4 on Election Day for the first time in history.

These price increases are a drag on many Americans’ budgets. Real wages—the difference between nominal wage growth and inflation—have declined over the past year, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics numbers released this week. For the Democrats, this has been a perverse political godsend. Long the undisputed party of workers, the Democratic Party has recently developed a reputation among voters for caring more about elites and cultural issues than basic economics.

Trump demonstrated the power of focusing on inflation in 2024, when he assailed President Biden and Vice President Harris over rising prices. Trump never demonstrated any sort of plan for how he’d bring prices down, but his emphasis on the matter—along with Democrats’ muddled messaging about it—helped him overcome voters’ deep hesitations about his character.

Since taking office, though, his lack of any vision for whipping inflation has been obvious. He has not pursued policies to fight high costs. He has demanded, in vain, that the Federal Reserve lower interest rates, which would be inflationary. He has mocked and dismissed the concept of affordability as a “hoax.” And although presidents typically have very limited short-term power to affect an economy, he found a way to make inflation worse by launching an ill-advised war in Iran, which he now has no idea how to end. This made Republicans very vulnerable in the midterms.

But even with impressive wins in both the Senate and the House, Democrats will have few ways to bring prices down. On corruption, by some measures the top issue in the election, Democrats have room to hold Trump and his administration accountable using investigative powers. But on affordability, rhetoric and doomed bills designed to send a message are almost all they can offer.

If reliable methods of bringing prices down in the short term existed, Republicans in Congress would have tried them this year. With unified Democratic control, Congress will be able to pass legislation, but Trump has already vowed to become “Mr. Veto.” Democrats can and will attempt to pressure Trump to end the conflict with Iran, but as the United States has seen repeatedly this century, starting a war is a lot easier than getting out of one.

“People are impatient. They want answers now. They’re economically insecure,” Gina Raimondo told me in an interview last week. Raimondo, the commerce secretary under Biden, is often mentioned as a potential 2028 Democratic presidential candidate. “If we’re sitting here in a year and a half and we haven’t done a damn thing about cost and prices, people might say, Well, forget about you; I’m going to try the other guy.”

Any parallel with voter rejection of Trump is imperfect. A president tends to shoulder more blame from voters than Congress does, a dynamic that is likely to be exacerbated with Trump, who is compelled to dominate attention, although it is sometimes to his disadvantage. But even if voters don’t blame Democrats specifically, they may still feel disillusioned with all politicians’ inability to bring quick change. That points to another risk for Democrats in 2028: not that people who voted blue in the midterms will turn against them, but that they will tune out entirely, just as the many disaffected Trump voters who opted not to vote in 2026, rather than flipping to Democrats, did.

The fact that party leaders are aware of these challenges doesn’t give them any easier answers to the affordability conundrum. Democrats had no choice but to campaign on high costs, but they may still pay a price for it.

Last month, in Minneapolis, I visited the homegrown memorials that still stand in the spots where Alex Pretti and Renee Good were killed by federal agents. To me, they looked like living organisms fed by pain and hopefulness—handfuls of teddy bears with matted fur, crayon drawings of the victims, fresh daisies, tall red candles, cloth banners bearing expressions such as Only Love. Yesterday, on Election Day, these shrines were refreshed—this time by I voted stickers.

The symbolism of these new offerings spoke succinctly to what the election seems to have meant to many voters. This was a chance for vindication, a response not just to Donald Trump’s policies but to the harsh and forbidding worldview he has come to represent. Pretti and Good were killed while monitoring an aggressive, armed, and often indiscriminate crackdown on their immigrant neighbors. Last night, more than 1,000 miles away from those memorials, in Austin, Texas, most of the people gathered for a victory speech by Senator-Elect James Talarico held signs that read Love Thy Neighbor. You might chalk these placards up to Talarico’s lefty Christian point of view, but they also felt like a modest plea, not the kind of thing you’d hold on a triumphant night: the expression of a desire for politics that spoke of simple values or, as Talarico himself put it, “love.”

The election was about many things, and the polling clearly showed that economic pain topped the list, intertwined with concern about corruption. But alongside it ran a mushier motivation for the blue wave (or tsunami or earthquake or volcano; the metaphors in last night’s newscasts were as tired as they were plentiful) that impressed itself even on David Axelrod, the famously brass-tacks Democratic strategist. “We’ve been in a season of such cynicism and such anger,” he said as midnight neared and the results became definitive. Sounding more like a preacher than a pundit, he said the strong Democratic showing was evidence of our “common humanity.” People want, Axelrod said, “a season of decency.” Even some on the right could recognize, as Ben Shapiro did, a change in the “gestalt,” as he put it. To channel James Carville, it all felt like the genesis of a new truism about politics: It’s the morality, stupid.

You don’t have to believe that Democrats’ hearts are somehow purer for making arguments premised on civility, or for promoting candidates who campaigned on restoring decency. Whether it was a premeditated effort or simply an instinctive collective response to 10 years of Trump, this kinder, more humane approach to political messaging was also a smart strategy.

In fairness, Democrats had some pretty good moral foils. Talarico’s rival, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, was impeached by Republicans over bribery and corruption, including pocketing a Montblanc pen. Ohio’s GOP candidate for governor, Vivek Ramaswamy, released an ad implying that his opponent was lying about being sexually abused as a child (and using her long-estranged mother, who had sided with the abuser, as a character witness). Some of Ramaswamy’s party colleagues, including the outgoing governor, Mike DeWine, and former South Carolina Governor Nikki Haley, condemned the ad for having crossed a line (Haley went moral, calling Ramaswamy a “bad guy”). But the most senior Republicans had no time for parsing the rightness of such an approach. “Stop whining,” J. D. Vance said to his fellow Republicans. “Whether the ad was appropriate or not is not really my concern. What is my concern is who’s going to govern the next generation of American government.” (His boss backed him up on this.) Judging from the astonishingly decisive results in Ohio and other red states, Americans of all kinds did have an opinion on its appropriateness, and voted accordingly.

Chris Christie, the former New Jersey governor and onetime Trump ally, called the ad “an X-ray” of Ramaswamy’s “moral center.” He had harsh words, too, for Vance: “A man who wrote a book on Catholicism stood behind that ad. It’s a disgrace.” What Christie understood is something that Republican leaders, taking a cue from Trump, appeared to have forgotten. If politics seems too nakedly transactional, too much a game of power for power’s sake, it loses people.

There might be a tribal aspect to how many Americans vote, but this identity has to be built around a story that feels righteous on some level. For a long time, Trump’s supporters could hear in his appeal an argument about justice, a case for the “forgotten man” to be remembered and rewarded. If he acted like a bully, he was their bully, for this virtuous purpose. But over the past two years, he has started a war that raised prices and put soldiers in danger, made Americans pay for tariffs, and spent much of his time gilding his immediate surroundings amid an affordability crisis. When the virtuous purpose vanishes, all that’s left is a bully.

The wild shift in the Latino vote from 2024 might have resulted from the perception of this abandoned purpose. Trump lost nearly 30 points in this demographic compared with the last election; according to exit polls, 70 percent of Latino voters chose Democrats yesterday. The moral aspect of this shift could be seen in places such as Texas’s Rio Grande Valley. The region went Republican for the first time in modern history in the last election, largely because Latinos living near the border favored his promise to restore order there. Trump vowed to stop the flow of migrants into their neighborhoods and deport the criminals among them who were living in America.

The crackdown, however, was far wider and crueler than many had anticipated, spreading fear throughout the entire area as ICE agents deported people who had been living in the country for decades. The local economy suffered, and those Latino voters felt not just betrayed by Trump but suddenly aware of an aspect of his approach that felt inhumane to them. Jessica Cantu Vuittenot, a former Trump supporter whose brother was detained just after he had checked in with an immigration official, described her regret to The New York Times: “Your world does go upside down because now it’s not just hurting you—it’s hurting your parents, it’s hurting his children, his grandchildren.”

Voters aren’t usually motivated by only one issue. Democrats’ focus on affordability, and on corruption in the Trump administration, surely helped turn so many districts blue, especially given the notable lack of focus from Trump himself in the closing weeks of the election. Surely in the mix, however, was a desire to recapture that sense of decency—a word many pundits tossed around after the results became clear. The move to run candidates such as Talarico—who might say, as he did last night with a straight face, that “every single person is holy”—helped provide a stark contrast, one that went beyond (or maybe, more accurately, underneath) differences in policy or ideology. This was not just the old question of a candidate’s character—of whether, for example, Adam Hamilton, the Kansan megachurch pastor, was a nicer person than his opponent, Roger Marshall, who had sued his patients over their medical debt. What Democrats offered was a way of speaking about American society that seems to have disappeared under Trump. You don’t need to think that these politicians believe what they’re saying, or that they don’t want to win at all costs, to appreciate this shift in rhetoric as a shrewd way of reflecting the kind of change voters want.

The idea of oneness, of neighborliness, of selflessness was what struck so many people about the anti-ICE protests in Minneapolis early this year. People I spoke with there told me about their relief and pride that their fellow citizens were still capable of seeing beyond their narrow self-interest. I saw it in the signs at the shrine to Pretti. Don’t let ICE overcome us, read one in a child’s scrawl. Do not let our neighbors fall! Don’t give up!!! The enormous success of Democrats in this election has many causes; among them was the party’s ability to listen and respond to exactly these sentiments.

Whatever else can be said about Trump, his superpower is, or has been, his ability to read a room. Are you laughing when he comments on the impressive behinds of Republican candidates? Then you’re going to get a lot more of that. So maybe the biggest sign that yesterday was, at least in part, an election about acting gracefully was that Trump himself seemed to pick up the message. Along with his usual blame-shifting, name-calling, and minimizing was an uncharacteristically simple post: “The Democrats did a good job, they fought hard.” Even if it was only a glimmer, a throwaway line, the tiniest concession, it seemed suddenly possible that even for Trump, kindness had prevailed.
