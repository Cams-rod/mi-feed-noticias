For decades, Texas has been the foundation of the Republican electoral college map, holding up the red bloc of midwestern and southern states like a pedestal. Democrats have long dreamed of winning it back, only for their hopes to be crushed in election after election. And now, suddenly, James Talarico and Gina Hinojosa have accomplished what no Democrat could for over a generation: winning statewide elections in Texas. 

Whether or not Texas is truly a battleground state now, a few things have become clear. Republicans can no longer take Texas voters for granted. Donald Trump harmed his party’s chances by propping up a weak Senate candidate in Attorney General Ken Paxton. And even Elon Musk, the world’s first trillionaire, couldn’t buy the result he wanted. 

Heading into election day, polls showed Talarico with a modest lead over Paxton, while the incumbent Republican governor, Greg Abbott, was favored to fend off Hinojosa. But not long after the polls closed, it became clear that both Talarico and Hinojosa would win comfortably. 

The Talarico election-night party was jubilant, with cheers erupting every few minutes as new races were called for Democrats and updated results continued showing him with a sizable lead. James Edge, a Republican mayor pro tem from Bryan, Texas, stood eating a cookie and enjoying what he was seeing. He had endorsed Talarico just after Labor Day. “My party, the Republican Party, needed a kick in the pants,” he said. “This is uncharted waters. If it holds, it’s an earthquake like we’ve never seen.” 

[Read: Trump has pushed some Texas Republicans too far]

At Paxton’s election night event in Dallas, party activists groaned as Fox News updated its ballot totals and the magnitude of the loss began to sink in. The Democrats even won the race for Texas Railroad Commissioner—a job held by Republicans for the past 36 years—as Jon Rosenthal easily defeated Bo French, who ran a race-baiting campaign that outraged the state’s growing Indian-American population. Jarrod Atkinson, a Dallas-area Republican activist, walked out of the ballroom where Paxton was scheduled to speak, looking stunned. He laid the blame squarely on Trump. “The fact that he keeps telling everyone to vote like he’s on the ballot is absolutely insane,” he said. “He has said some of the dumbest things that have ever been uttered.”

Before tonight, Texas had not elected a Democrat to statewide office since 1994, and had not chosen a Democrat for the U.S. Senate since Lloyd Bentsen, in 1988. But the state’s shifting demographics have nurtured Democratic hopes of a comeback for years. Texas, which Trump won by nearly 14 points in 2024, has grown significantly wealthier, more urban, and more racially diverse even as Republicans have dominated its politics. In fact, the shift traces in part to some of the very pro-business policies that have made Texas so appealing to large corporations. During the past three decades of GOP rule, the state has offered itself as a foil to New York and California, with cheaper housing, more job opportunities, lower taxes, and less red tape. The state’s population has grown over 8 percent since 2020, absorbing more new residents than any other. More than 300 companies have relocated their corporate headquarters to Texas since 2015, including Tesla, Oracle, and Charles Schwab. Tech employment has been growing faster in Austin than in Silicon Valley. 

Republicans like to think of the influx as a political endorsement of GOP rule. But the newcomers are more racially diverse and more likely to be college-educated than the average Texan. Over the past decade, the percentage of adults in the state with a bachelor’s degree or higher—a group that has shifted decisively to the Democratic coalition—rose from 28 to 35 percent. Hispanics, who made up 40.9 percent of the population in 2025, are now a plurality of the state’s residents.

Demographic change alone was not enough to deliver a statewide election to Democrats. But Republicans entered this Senate race with at least two additional disadvantages. The first is broad discontent about the rising cost of living, which voters seem to have directed not just at Donald Trump but at Texas’s ruling class. Republicans of yore had campaigned on the Texas Miracle, touting broad economic growth. That case became harder to make amid rising prices for groceries, gas, health care, and housing. “It felt like everything was on the upswing for a long time,” Nancy Zdunkewicz, an Austin-based Democratic pollster, told us. “The bill is now due.”

[Read: The GOP’s culture-war Hail Mary]

Mike Madrid, a political consultant who studies Hispanic voting trends, told us that the percentage of early votes cast in the state included more ballots cast by Hispanic voters than any other group, including white voters. “That is something I have not seen in 35 years,” Madrid said. “If the economy were doing really well right now, we would not be seeing this.” In 2024, Hispanic Texans swung heavily toward Trump. This year, they appear to have shifted right back. Madrid argues that Hispanic voters are an emerging swing demographic, more populist than ideological, and that this year they’re mad about inflation. The lesson for Democrats, he said, is that the party needs a pro-growth message and a “new southern strategy” tailored to working-class Hispanic voters across the Sun Belt states, with Texas at the center.

In the Senate race, Republicans compounded their disadvantages by nominating a deeply flawed candidate, while Democrats nominated one who positioned himself as the morally upstanding alternative. Paxton, whom Trump endorsed in the primary over the incumbent, John Cornyn, was known for a list of legal, ethical, and personal scandals far too extensive to summarize here. Dan Jackson, a truck driver and three-time Trump voter in Plano, said he “had some trouble” sorting out all the negative ads he’d seen this year. Ultimately, he voted for Abbott for governor—a safe choice, he reasoned—but went for Talarico for Senate because he doesn’t trust Paxton. 

“You’ve got, in Paxton, the worst statewide candidate to top the Republican ticket in the entire history of Republican hegemony in the state,” James Henson, the director of the Texas Politics Project at UT Austin, told us. “And then an unusually skilled Democratic candidate in Talarico, who came along at the right moment and has been able to execute better than any Democrat before him.”

When Gina Hinojosa was considering whether to run for governor, she met with Beto O’Rourke, whose campaigns for Senate and governor had electrified a hopeful Democratic base only to end in disappointment on Election Day. Someone needed to run against Abbott, the entrenched and powerful incumbent, Hinojosa told him. Should she do it? One comment stood out: “Not one person can win Texas,” he told her, according to someone familiar with their conversation. “It has to be two.” O’Rourke told her that both times he was running statewide, he felt that his campaign would have benefited from a stronger ticket.

The dynamic that emerged in 2026 was one in which Hinojosa and Talarico could work in tandem, sharing a similar message while energizing different pockets of the state. Talarico spoke openly about his faith, and had a sunny disposition that played well in rural areas. Hinojosa, a Latina woman, targeted the conservative suburban women known as MAGA moms who shared her concern about privatizing public schools. 

Talarico, in particular, ran as that rare political breed: a Democrat who seems genuinely comfortable talking about his Christian faith. He was willing to mix it up with Joe Rogan and Fox News hosts. He was careful not to mention Trump, lest he alienate the many Texans who voted for the president but whose votes he would need.

Talarico’s final rally, held in an outdoor amphitheater filled with nearly 2,000 people, was an anxiety-filled dose of optimism. “I’m a worrywart,” Pauline Bryson, an Austin resident attending the rally, said. “But a very hopeful worrywart.” Some recalled bawling when Beto O’Rourke lost in 2018; they questioned whether it was ok to be hopeful. But this time felt different—right? Right? Through a hoarse voice, Talarico asked the crowd to be the “slingshot” to help his David beat the Paxton Goliath.

Some 225 miles away, Paxton’s campaign sputtered to a close. Paxton’s team had planned to end his campaign with a rally at the Alamo Monday evening. They cancelled the event with no explanation, and Paxton’s finale took place instead at Matt’s Rancho Martinez, a Tex-Mex restaurant next to a Quality Inn on the side of the expressway north of Dallas. About 150 supporters showed up, helping themselves to free nachos and margaritas, and the NRA handed out trucker caps and stickers. Paxton had spent the final stretch traveling the state on what he called the “Come and Take It” tour, speaking at restaurants and other relatively small venues. His slogan, and the cannon imagery on the side of his tour bus, were meant to channel the defenders of the Alamo, and the breakaway defiance that produced the state’s independence. But it sounded like he was inviting Democrats to take the Senate seat.

Local Republican officials warmed up the crowd with warnings that the state was on the cusp of disaster. One praised Paxton for helping defeat “the Bush cartel,” a reference to the state’s pre-Trump Republican establishment. A lot of the red-meat rhetoric was over-the-top, with communism, Sharia law, and forced vegetarianism looming over the taco bar. “If Texas turns blue, it’s our children and grandchildren who will be enslaved with it,” state senator Bob Hall told the crowd. The Turkish former NBA player and political stuntman Enes Kanter, who has changed his name to Enes Kanter Freedom and more recently declared himself a woman who wants to play in the WNBA, came out to introduce Paxton. He told the audience “close your eyes and think about your daughter being in the same locker room with biological males.” There were gasps.

Frank DeSimone, a software developer and local Republican precinct official who attended the Paxton rally, told us the booming suburbs north of Dallas where he lived were facing “a paradox.” Rapid growth brings rapid change, he said, jeopardizing the same things that attracted so many transplants—safe neighborhoods, good schools—in the first place. It was up to Republicans to stand for a set of “Texas values” that would win suburban voters over. DeSimone said he wanted to help grow the party by defining that in a big-tent way. Did he think Texas will be an up-for-grabs purple state in ’28? “It already is,” he said.

For all his weaknesses, Paxton entered the general election with a not-so-secret weapon. Elon Musk poured almost $200 million into the state, the bulk of it to boost Paxton’s Senate bid. Musk’s home base is in the state, and he has located many of his businesses in Texas, with all the tax breaks and political connections that flow from that. On Monday morning, we paid a visit to a corner of Bastrop, Texas, that has become so identified with Musk that locals call it “Elon Land.” His tunnel-digging business, the Boring Company, is here, along with SpaceX and Starlink. Those companies have made some residents rich. But, even in Elon Land, we found signs that his political reach is limited.

Sebastyon Champion, a 38-year-old senior project manager working on the SpaceX facility, wore a Carhartt hat and a silver cross as he finished a pulled pork sandwich at the Boring Bodega, a combination of a market, a bar, and a hair salon. He is well paid, living in an apartment complex so new that most units haven’t yet been sold, but he also noted that he paid $100 to put three-quarters of a tank into his truck that morning. He’s a conservative voter—“We have a saying here: Don’t California my Texas”—but was turned off by Paxton and his history of corruption. “Ken Paxton should be in jail,” he said. “I wouldn’t piss on him if he was on fire.” He voted for Abbott, but when it came to the Senate race, he went for a third party candidate. In fact, over the course of several hours, in conversations with more than a dozen Texans whose livelihood depends on Musk—and in a setting directly across from a mammoth manufacturing plant the tech mogul is constructing—we could find no one who was supporting the candidate he had spent so much money to prop up.

The lingering question, which will be litigated in election cycles to come, is whether a generational political realignment is taking place in one of the nation’s largest states—or whether Democrats just got lucky this time. “People who win think it’s about them. It’s always about their opponent,” Bill Miller, a lobbyist in Austin with connections to both parties, told us. “People vote against people. That’s our culture now. If Democrats think it’s because they got all the issues, they don’t. It’s the issues that Republicans are carrying are turning people off.”

That might be true—but in two years, the GOP will still be defined by an incumbent president who is currently the most unpopular in the history of polling. Texas will be worth 40 electoral votes in the next presidential election, and there are few plausible Republican roads to victory without it. The last time a Democratic presidential candidate won the state was Jimmy Carter in 1976. Heading into 2028, Texas Democrats won’t be able to stop themselves from dreaming big.

The president’s party is always expected to do poorly in midterm elections, just not this poorly. The results arriving tonight are a disaster for Donald Trump—the kind of complete repudiation that the president’s opponents have spent a decade pining for, but never quite achieving. On current trends, Democrats are not just poised to claim a large majority in the House of Representatives. They are also on track to capture the Senate by winning seats they hoped for, as in Texas, and others that they had no business hoping for, as in South Carolina.

The rebuke looks too ubiquitous to be confined to the gripes of any particular subset of voters. In Miami-Dade County, in Florida, for example, the impressive Latino support that Trump built up in his 2024 win appears to have totally evaporated. Kansas is on the verge of electing a Democratic senator—for the first time in a century. Democrats in Ohio seem likely to flip both the governor’s mansion and a U.S. Senate seat. Earlier today, a (Never Trump) Republican operative in the Midwest texted me, saying, “Our party badly needs an acid lava barbed-wire enema and I’m hoping we get it tonight.” Voters heard this wish.

How did the GOP’s situation get so bad? A moralizing account might say that Trump is getting just deserts for his precedent-breaking behavior in office—the great personal enrichment, the weaponization of the justice system against his enemies, the dismantling of agencies without congressional consent. But this assumes too much about the propriety of American voters—who, after all, returned the president to office after he had tried to overturn his election loss in 2020.

The more obvious explanation for Republicans’ poor performance is economic. Having come to office by exploiting discontent with inflation under Joe Biden, Trump seemed to think that the same rules would not apply to him while in office. The president has launched trade wars against the rest of the world, increasing the costs of imported goods, in addition to passing a massive tax cut and encouraging the AI-spending boom, which have pushed up interest rates. Most disastrously, he launched a war of choice against Iran that has raised the cost of gas and diesel.

[Read: The beginning of the end for Trump]

Only 38 percent of Americans now say that they approve of the president’s job performance. Trump and his allies attempted to juice their odds of holding the House by having Republican-led states aggressively gerrymander their districts. This strategy did not work.

Unlike the president, Democrats learned the lessons of the last election: both that incumbents were vulnerable to attacks over the cost of living and that they had veered too far to the left on immigration and social-justice issues such as trans rights. The party has many sects—urban socialists, rural populists, “abundance”-pilled technocrats—that converged on a common message: the issue of “affordability,” on which Republicans were weak.

All of these factions can be expected to jockey for credit in the party’s election victory, but Democrats’ all-terrain overperformance suggests that it was Republican actions in leadership that lost the election. The president’s predilection for underwhelming candidates, including the scandal-ridden Ken Paxton as Senate nominee in Texas and the underqualified Darline Graham in South Carolina, was a factor. But most of the problem seems to have been the president’s own policy making—a drag so serious that it imperiled experienced and established Republicans such as Senator Susan Collins of Maine and Governor Greg Abbott of Texas.

The imminent demise of Trumpism has been predicted many times in the past decade. Even now, it cannot be guaranteed. The repudiation of Trumpism at the ballot box does not spell its demise even in the Republican Party. Such is the president’s hold among his loyal base. Democrats will be able to stymie the White House with budget battles and constant investigations, and will likely be in a position to block any Supreme Court nominees.

Tonight’s outcome does not so much foretell a powerless president as it does an angry and perhaps even more disinhibited one. All of the president’s signature policy decisions of this term—the Iran war, the trade wars, special military operations in Venezuela and the Caribbean Sea—were launched without any congressional input whatsoever. Although Congress will more jealously try to guard its power under Democratic control, the imperial presidency is well entrenched. Trump’s power will eventually end—but only after one more election.

Repudiation

President Trump has been the dominant political figure of this century. He pulled off one of the most unlikely campaign victories in American history, and then staged the greatest political comeback. He has altered the United States’ relationship with the world, rewritten the rules and expectations of the presidency, inflamed the nation’s divisions, and changed how—and how often—Americans think about his office. He is omnipresent, and draws his power from that ubiquity.

But tonight, Trump has been forced to face his political mortality. He asked voters to make these midterm elections a referendum on him, to imagine that his name was on the ballot. They listened—and then rejected him. It is a stunning, widespread rebuke. Democrats appear poised to win the House and, if trends hold, could also capture the Senate after Americans registered their strong disapproval of the direction in which the president has taken the country over the past two years. Trump will now likely have to deal with congressional Democrats who could block his agenda and wield the power of the subpoena. He also must face the possibility that the political party he remade in his image may soon start looking beyond him. Donald J. Trump is now officially, thoroughly a lame duck.

But we haven’t heard the last of him. He remains the commander in chief, dominates global affairs, wields the expansive power of executive orders, and remains unequaled at capturing attention. He has escaped the political graveyard more than once before, and emerged stronger. Yet for all his power, for all his volume, he cannot browbeat the unrelenting calendar: The next national election will be the one to replace him. Trump will before long begin to slip in the headlines, pushed off the front page by the presidential campaign. He’ll face the possibility of being succeeded by a Democratic president who could employ the Department of Justice to plumb the previous four years and look into Trump’s allies, friends, family, and businesses. And he’ll confront arguably his greatest fear: irrelevance.

Trump will not go quietly. It is not clear whether he and fellow Republicans will even accept the midterm results—and he has defied expectations so often that it would be a foolish exercise to set any. Tonight’s expected losses will not humble him. But they mark the beginning of the end.

Trump’s midterm campaign ended in the most ridiculous, yet predictable, way possible. For months, the president had been accused of being out of touch, of losing his once-unmatched feel for what animated his supporters. He has mocked affordability concerns while his own fortune has swelled, started a war that Americans did not want, remodeled the White House, and planned various monuments to himself. And then yesterday, he decided that the best way to push back against the accusation that he’d been consumed with vanity projects and corrupt bargains would be to deliver the final speech of the campaign cycle at the site of his most absurd, failed renovation project.

To the dismay of Republicans everywhere, Trump stood in front of the Reflecting Pool on the eve of Election Day and delivered a defense of a project that was doomed by algae and the shoddy work of a no-bid contractor. The project—which Trump had falsely claimed was derailed by vandalism—ran millions of dollars over budget and surely earned not a single vote in places such as Michigan, Kansas, and Alaska. Worst of all, the detour to revisit the Reflecting Pool distracted from Trump’s true closing message: that Senator Jon Husted of Ohio has a great body.

[Read: Trump is closing from behind]

Trump was a late entrant on the campaign trail, and had to be talked into holding rallies over the past month or so. But once he was, he insisted that the election be about himself and his successes, and make no acknowledgment of any missteps or empathy for those who are struggling. He insisted that many campaign ads feature him. He demanded a first-of-its-kind midterm political convention, though few who attended the festivities in Dallas seemed happy to be there. Trump threw himself into the campaign, holding nearly-two-hour rallies night after night across the country. But these road shows weren’t quite the same as those that became the defining political event of the past decade.

They were smaller than normal, and few were outdoors (in part because of security concerns). One rally in Ohio in the campaign’s final fortnight was held in a small gym, and others featured plenty of empty seats. More striking was where the rallies were held: nearly half in deep-red states where Trump has easily won in the past and has rarely needed to campaign. That led to bigger, more enthusiastic crowds—people in a place like Corinth, Mississippi, were more excited to see Trump for the first time than, say, those in Grand Rapids, Michigan, where he has held more than half a dozen rallies over the years. But the geography also told a story: Trump had to visit Republican strongholds such as South Carolina and Montana because seats in those states were suddenly in play and his faithful had to be summoned. But once there, what did Trump really have to offer?

Voters, per polling, are angry over his string of broken promises, including his vows to bring down prices, not start new wars, release the Epstein files, and deport only the “worst of the worst.” The parties of incumbent presidents traditionally fare poorly in the midterms, but Trump is also underwater on several of what used to be his strongest issues, such as the economy and immigration. He forced out multiple incumbent senators for being disloyal and backed some problematic candidates, all of which put more races into play. He gave Republican candidates very little to run on, as voters furiously reacted to an economy that seemed to benefit only the rich (a category that includes the president’s family and Cabinet). Instead, Trump tried to recycle culture-war issues from campaigns past, including fears about transgender surgeries, Muslim terrorists, and even communism.

[Read: Going hungry in Trump’s economy]

He seemed out of step not just with the public but, in many cases, with his own party. Despite warnings that AI could wipe out humanity (or at least a lot of jobs), Trump backed the nascent technology and its data centers—even as the public turned on them—because tech companies are propping up the stock market. He supported a controversial attack ad in the Ohio governor’s race that the state’s Republican governor denounced. He sided with Russia in its war with Ukraine. He threatened to once more bomb Iran. And there really was a lot of talk about butts, which will surely confuse future historians.

Americans didn’t seem to go for it. As of this writing Democrats were well on track to decisively take the House. But the story is the Senate: Democrats have already flipped North and South Carolina and easily held on to Georgia. Ohio, Texas, and other deep-red strongholds may soon join them.

As those results came in, Trump took to social media to duck blame—and contradict the central message of his campaign, writing, “I wasn’t on the Ballot and if I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!”

Trump is now in a new place, and one he won’t like.

In his first midterm election, in 2018, Democrats gained control of the House, but that victory was offset by GOP wins in the Senate. House Democrats slowed the president’s agenda, but their 2019 impeachment effort over Ukraine only bolstered Trump’s approval ratings—and he still felt bullish about his reelection chances on the back of a strong economy. (Then, of course, came the pandemic.) Many Democrats say they’ve learned their lesson. Senior members in the party, including Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next House speaker, have been cagey as to their plans for power. The Democratic Party’s approval ratings remain low, and although not being a Republican boosted many Democrats in this year’s campaign, that won’t be the case going forward. Jeffries has signaled that he will focus on measures to try to address affordability and health-care challenges. Democrats are going to say “no” a lot, and now that it appears that they will capture the Senate too, they will likely make it difficult for Trump to confirm any of his nominees, including possible Supreme Court justices.

But the Democratic Party’s base is hungry to hold the Trump administration accountable: for DOGE’s destruction of the government, for war, for foreign business deals masquerading as diplomacy, for profiting off the presidency. Accusations of corruption against Trump and his administration have proliferated compared with in his first term. Democrats have promised to issue subpoenas and parade administration officials through the Capitol’s hearing rooms—if they agree to show up. Some close to Trump have theorized that he doesn’t mind having a foil in Washington, and that he would be comfortable tangling with congressional Democrats again. “He likes to have someone to fight—and someone to blame,” one close outside adviser told me on the condition of anonymity to discuss internal dynamics.

But Trump is leery about some of the possible investigations and does not want to be impeached again. (Democrats might be more inclined to try to oust a Cabinet member anyway.) The probes he likely faces next year could act as a preview of what would happen in 2029 if a Democratic president follows him. Trump enjoys a sort of immunity granted by the Supreme Court, and he has vowed to give preemptive pardons to many people in this administration. Yet advisers say he is unhappy at the prospect of his inner circle facing punishing investigations once he leaves office.

[Read: Here’s who Democrats want to impeach next]

Today’s vote is a clear marker on Trump’s road to irrelevance. Many close to him believe that his driving motivation—more than power or money—is attention. And for a decade now, he has blocked out the sun, dominating the discourse in a way that no other president has in generations. But that will begin to change as Washington begins to look beyond him. Once a colossus, Trump will quickly find himself to be a secondary storyline. It’s not as if he will suddenly vanish; he’ll aim to play GOP kingmaker, and he’ll still surely inspire loyalty from his hard-core believers, even if that group shrinks. He’ll still be commander in chief, and even lame-duck presidents have broad authority when it comes to foreign policy. A return to war with Iran? A move on Cuba? Revisiting Greenland? With Trump fixated on his legacy and desperate to stay in the spotlight, anything feels possible.

Trump also may start to see the complete fealty of the Republican Party erode. This stunning midterms wipeout suggests that Trump’s instincts and influence seem to be waning, and Republicans may be more willing to defy him. As the next round of presidential contenders begins to emerge, Republicans will begin to sort out what their post-MAGA future will look like—and what role, if any, Trump plays in it.

Long before he entered politics, Donald Trump divided humanity into winners and losers, and nothing has mattered more to him than counting himself among the former. This need drove his refusal to accept the 2020 election results—how could a man defined by winning lose?

Whatever misgivings some Republicans may have about Trump’s character or his policies, many subscribe to the theory that, if nothing else, the man knows how to win. And his aura of mastery has extended beyond his followers to the Americans who most detest him. The disgust with which many liberals regard Trump is mixed with fear and envy. His powers of persuasion are a source of endless fascination. They have launched a thousand diner safaris to explore his mystical hold on Real America, and inspired innumerable Democratic activists and intellectuals to ponder how they could create a Trump of their own.

But Trump is not a winner. He is, for the most part, a loser. In 2016, he lost the national popular vote—that is, the measure of which candidate Americans wanted to win the election—by two percentage points. His party lost control of the House in the 2018 midterms. Trump lost reelection in 2020. His sole unambiguous victory came in 2024, when he faced the clumsy fill-in candidate for an unpopular administration, against which he prevailed, almost by default.

And now he has lost again. The 2026 midterm elections are delivering a repudiation of historic magnitude to the president’s party, extending far deeper than the customary pendulum swing against the party in power. Not only did Trump’s presidency as a whole repel the electorate, but also his specific interventions in Republican primaries proved counterproductive. He handpicked Senate candidates, such as Ken Paxton in Texas and Darline Graham in South Carolina, for their loyalty, assuming that any warm body could prevail in such deep-red states. Yet Graham went down to defeat, and Paxton currently trails his Democratic opponent, James Talarico.

[Read: What Donald Trump fears most]

The results help clarify the precise nature of Trump’s political talent. He does possess a certain animal cunning that allows him to home in on human weakness, among both his allies and his targets. (“Jeb’s a loser,” he said in February 2016. “The last thing we need, frankly, is another Bush.”) His speech is so ambiguous and discursive that he avoids being pinned down on particulars. These traits have made him relatively effective as a challenger, exploiting discontent with his opponents while making grand promises (a golden era of wealth, world peace) without specifying any steps he will take to achieve them. None of these skills translates to effective governing.

After his election victory two years ago, Democrats feared, and expected, that Trump would coast on the economic recovery he inherited. The post-COVID inflation that had poisoned Joe Biden’s presidency was settling back to normal, and Trump needed only to allow economic gravity to do its work while taking credit for the result.

Instead, Trump carried out the wrenching economic changes that he was unable to enact during his first term, in part because he was obstructed by his advisers and by senators from his own party. In 2017, he came one John McCain vote short of defunding the Affordable Care Act, a defeat that spared Trump’s party the political repercussions of stripping health insurance from millions of Americans. In 2025, a more compliant Republican Congress granted him his wish to gut the ACA, along with food stamps, courting the pain they had avoided eight years earlier.

After successfully taking advantage of the Biden administration’s failure to contain a surge in migrants, even winning support from millions of Latino voters, Trump proceeded to alienate swing voters with mass deportations carried out through a frequently brutal and occasionally deadly campaign of mass terror.

After promising during the 2024 campaign to bring down prices, he took active measures that economists knew would push them higher. Rather than using tariffs mostly as a bluff and settling for modestly rejiggered trade agreements for which he could claim credit, as he did during his first term, he imposed the costliest tariffs since the Herbert Hoover era.

When enterprising members of Congress decided, without Trump’s involvement, to pass a bill earlier this year to encourage more housing construction, Trump could have taken credit for their work and demonstrated at least an interest in addressing a major cost driver. Instead, angry that Republicans had failed to pass his bill restricting voting, he petulantly boycotted the signing ceremony. Declining to sign a bipartisan housing bill at a moment when housing costs are a top public concern is the political equivalent of being handed a baby and refusing to kiss it.

Most incredible of all, 10 years after he astonished the commentariat by standing on a Republican debate stage in hawkish South Carolina and declaring the Iraq War “a big, fat mistake,” Trump replicated the George W. Bush administration’s signature blunder by launching a war of choice in the Middle East without any plausible exit strategy.

Trump might have received a little bit of grace from voters if he had made visible gestures of concern for the pain that Americans felt from these policies. Presidents in the past have grasped the need to show the country that they are working hard, because, if nothing else, regular people typically assume that improving the economy is a function of presidential diligence, like a landlord fixing a leaky toilet. Instead, Trump turned his attention to decorating palaces, erecting monuments, renaming buildings after himself, and demanding awards.

He has weirdly inverted a classic populist trope, which posits that the wealth and comfort of the capital comes at the expense of the prosperity of the decent, God-fearing people of the heartland. Trump obsesses in public about his focus on the tiny area in the immediate vicinity of the White House. He devoted his closing message the day before the election to a lengthy recitation of improvements in the capital, from his war against algae to a proposed golf course, which he promised would give residents of Washington a discount. This fixation is a source of pride only for those whose chief goal for this presidency is Trump’s glorification.

The theme connecting all of these baffling choices is a misread of the 2024 election results. The MAGA cult was never large enough to win a majority of the Electoral College. Trump won because he offered an alternative to an unpopular Biden administration, and because enough of the public fondly recalled his first presidential term before COVID struck—and retroactively excused him for the consequences of his pandemic mismanagement. The myth of Trump’s political genius is primarily a by-product of this first-term good fortune. He inherited a healthy economy and had little effect on its trajectory, paradoxically benefiting from failure. The buoyant recovery created a floor beneath his approval ratings that no gaffe or scandal could crash through.

Trump, however, convinced himself that he had won because the public shared his conclusion that his first term had been a treacherous betrayal by his enemies. And so, while Trump’s 2024 coalition hinged on Americans who felt nostalgic for his first term, these same voters empowered a president determined not to repeat it.

[David Frum: Only losers play the madman]

He ruthlessly purged the bureaucracy and his party of all dissent so that he could build the dream palace of his imagination—an imperium that would silence his critics in the news media, reduce the business elite to sniveling courtiers, and venerate him as a king.

Many of these efforts have met with success. But the unwavering loyalty he has commanded within his party has come at the price of revulsion outside of it. Trump made the autocrat’s error of believing his own propaganda, thus denying himself the benefit of realistic advice that might help him avert or correct his misjudgments. And by treating disagreement as treason, he locked the exits, trapping his fellow Republicans with him as he burned down their reputation.

While the Americans who worship Trump as a culture-war champion or god-king remain enthralled, the ones who thought they were hiring him for a four-year gig to get a handle on the border and inflation regard his second term as a failure.

Those two constituencies are often conflated with each other, and Republicans have encouraged the conflation by treating his fans as the authentic Volk. In reality, Trump’s base is not synonymous with the country. His base has never constituted anything close to a national majority. Stripped of the protective gloss he enjoyed from the healthy economy during his first term, Trump has no more political magic than Jimmy Carter.

When Jon Ossoff was first elected, in January 2021, he felt a little like an accidental senator. He was 33 years old and had won by just 55,000 votes, defeating the unpopular Republican incumbent, David Perdue, in a runoff election overshadowed by President Trump’s bogus claims of voter fraud.

He doesn’t feel accidental anymore.

Ossoff cruised to reelection tonight. The final margin of victory is not yet known, but networks called the race early; soon after the polls closed, any drama was gone, even though he had been campaigning in a state that is barely purple. His triumph does not change much about the Senate’s electoral calculus overall, because Democrats were expected to hold his seat, but the decisive victory immediately propels him into the top ranks of the party and any discussions about the 2028 presidential contest.

Ossoff’s win is a testament to his deftness as both senator and candidate. In office, he has emphasized constituent services but also mastered the art of getting under the skin of Republican officials during Senate hearings, without ever seeming ruffled himself. On the stump, his campaign showed an acute sense of how to use media, even producing a new coinage—the “Ossoff angle”—to describe the upward camera perspective that his team worked to produce.

He paired these good visuals with a knack for the sound bite. In the most famous instance, in August, he slyly said that Trump “doesn’t want to do the job. He wants to build his ballroom and travel with Natalie.” Although he omitted her last name, the moment made the president’s assistant Natalie Harp—who was previously familiar only to political reporters and political junkies—into a prominent figure. The ensuing news cycle was both damaging and distracting to the White House. As the youngest senator, Ossoff has shown a mastery of short-form video that few of his peers can dream of.

Like many other Democrats this cycle, Ossoff benefited from the president’s unpopularity, disastrous agenda, and lack of political focus. He was running in a state that Trump had won by barely two points in 2024. Ossoff was also very lucky in his opponent. After failing to recruit a stronger candidate, Republicans landed on Mike Collins, whose many connections to white nationalists, including his son-in-law, helped sink him. But Ossoff made some of his own luck by intimidating better candidates into not running. He also showed a killer instinct on debate stages, savaging Collins over his ties to racists.

Ossoff’s campaign was impressive enough that his name is on the lips of what the columnist Russell Baker called “The Great Mentioner,” the rumor mill of possible candidates for president. During the campaign, Ossoff promised to finish his next term as senator if he were reelected. “I have zero interest in running for president,” he said during a debate. “I’m not running for president. I’m running for reelection to the Senate.” But candidates who wish to do so have squirmed their way out of stronger commitments.

What an Ossoff presidential campaign might look like is unclear. He resides more on the moderate wing of the party than on the left, but he does not have a strong ideological profile, and he has avoided confrontations with more progressive Democrats. To become a major national figure, he’ll have to define himself more clearly. And although in many ways the 2028 cycle has already begun, that election is a long way off in political time.

The skills required to run for president are also on a different tier from those needed to win at the state level, as many senators and governors have learned the hard way in the past. Ossoff’s talent outside of carefully packaged media has not yet been demonstrated on the national level. One of the backhanded compliments he gets is that someone so good at direct-to-social video messaging can’t possibly be as charismatic one-on-one. His triumph tonight guarantees that he’ll instantly be in heavy demand on television and at Democratic events across the country, where he’ll have a chance to test and hone those skills.
