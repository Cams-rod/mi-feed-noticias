You know things are bad when panic-buyers start emptying the shelves at Costco. As you might have noticed, everything is breaking. Over the past week, Outlook has gone down, U.S. air traffic control has gone down, banking systems have gone down, and the National Security Agency’s confidential-telephone system has gone down, and just today, Apple announced that some of its services are down.

There is an air of something nefarious afoot. “If this is all a coincidence,” Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri said yesterday, “it’s like the greatest coincidence in the history of the world.” Others have traded apocalyptic theories: Are rogue AI agents taking over everything? Is this the End Times?

The answers to these questions are, respectively: No, AI agents are not taking over, and isn’t it always the End Times?

Let me explain.


I believe strongly in Hanlon’s razor: Never attribute to malice what is easily explainable by stupidity or incompetence. So even on Friday, when the federal government halted all air travel in the United States just one day after a massive email outage, I was doubtful that these events were connected. But now mass outages and vulnerabilities to critical-infrastructure systems have surfaced day after day, sometimes multiple times a day. Even I have found it hard to shake the feeling that this is more than a coincidence.

[Read: What on earth is going on?]

There are three likely hypotheses to explain these events. The first explanation is benign: All of these breakages and failures are happening because we have old legacy systems that are running on outdated code. Crumbly old computer systems are crumbling.

The second is more nefarious: foreign interference. This is not a crazy notion, especially given the crucial nature of the systems that have gone dark. (A special thought and prayer to the lucky ones who had to rebook a canceled flight on Friday night and couldn’t access their Outlook inbox.) The constant onslaught is also suspicious in light of recent events. U.S.-China relations are at a low point after Jensen Huang was pushed out as CEO of Nvidia, in part for having an affair with a woman U.S. intelligence has reportedly identified as a Chinese spy. (China continues to deny that this is the case.) If not China, perhaps another foreign actor—possibly Iran.

The third hypothesis is that third-party actors are using powerful AI tools to find a number of vulnerabilities and bugs in our digital infrastructure. But given that there reportedly have been no demands for ransom and no one bragging and claiming responsibility online, as is typical of hacking groups, this explanation seems unlikely. (Many ransomware attacks go unreported.) Hacks are very hard to pull off, Scott Shapiro, a cybersecurity expert at Yale Law School, told me. One hack after another across multiple sectors of critical infrastructure would be a feat so great that it seemingly could be pulled off only by nation-state actors—even in the age of AI. “What’s hard to understand is how these events could be stacked,” he said. “You would think that one is causing the other, but that does not seem to be the case.”

I spoke with two federal officials, both of whom requested anonymity because of the classified nature of their job. Although not directly involved in the response to the outages, they did not see any reason to blame the incidents on foreign interference. That leaves one explanation: This is all (relatively) benign. Indeed, it turns out that a lot of stuff breaks all the time. Major outages on government sites, cloud services, and a number of low-profile systems have been going down over the past few months, all with mundane software issues brought on by new code slapped haphazardly onto old software.

Moreover—and the government officials couldn’t emphasize this enough—high-level engineers who understand legacy code have left their job at the same time that there’s been an influx of people happy to throw anything they vibe code into the existing system. So when things break, as they are going to do, there aren’t people around who can fix them anymore.

As for the timing with the Nvidia fallout, those I spoke with generally saw it as happenstance. Similarly, a number of people told me that the NSA communication system has been slowly failing over the past year and has had multiple (shorter) outages already. “No one is surprised about this inside the NSA,” an employee who works at the agency told me—an observation that echoes the agency’s own public statements.

The biggest tell is in the solutions to these problems, none of which were particularly complicated or revealed any kind of virus, worm, or other type of hacking. Microsoft blamed the outage on a months-old bug. The Federal Aviation Administration cited problems stemming from a router update in Arkansas. Chase Bank attributed its brief outage to a software bug. “The solutions to these problems are banal, because the problem is banal,” one Defense Department official told me. “You run a patch. It’s fixed. That tells you right there what’s going on.”

As tempting as it is to blame these outages on some Mission: Impossible–style foreign-bad-guy operation, there’s a lesson here: When you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras. But that can be a hard sell in the current moment of AI hype. It’s in vogue, among a certain set, to be an AI doomer and to worry about a grand catastrophic event that will render humanity either irrelevant or nonexistent.

There is some truth to this: AI is responsible for the failures, but not because the singularity is here. Rather, most of the complex systems we run on are held together with duct tape. AI itself isn’t breaking those things; we’re using AI to break them faster. Most of our essential systems run on software that is old, out-of-date, and near-impossible to cohesively update. But now code changes are happening so quickly at the same time, and all sectors are losing the senior talent who are in the best position to stop these mistakes in the first place or fix them after they happen.

In that sense, the outages are a preview of what AI disruption will really look like: not some swarm of Terminators, but everyday inconveniences that affect millions of lives and cost billions to fix.

‘No Take, Only Throw’ Politics

This month, fed-up, stressed-out Americans headed to the ballot box and tacked hard to the left, supporting the party that is promising to restore billions of dollars in federal spending and billions of dollars in federal tax revenue. This month, fed-up, stressed-out Americans headed to the ballot box and tacked hard to the left, supporting the party that is promising to restore billions of dollars in federal spending and billions of dollars in federal tax revenue. Democrats won in blue states. They won in purple states, such as Georgia, Michigan, Maine, and North Carolina. They flipped seats in Trump-hat-red Nebraska, Indiana, Kentucky, Texas, Iowa, and Montana. Voters in South Carolina sent a Democrat to the Senate for the first time since 1998; in Mississippi, since 1982.

At the same time, fed-up, stressed-out Americans headed to the ballot box and curved to the right, supporting a bevy of measures to restrain government spending. Some revenue-raising initiatives won; Coloradans created a graduated state income tax, for instance. But in Florida, voters gutted the property-tax system. In Louisiana, they sheltered seniors from taxation. In Arizona, they limited levies on groceries and banned them on vehicle-miles traveled. In progressive California, they turned down a onetime wealth tax on billionaires and made it harder for the state to impose one in the future.

The midterm landslide was another instance of the electorate rejecting incumbents and revolting against the establishment. It was another example of American voters raging against the country’s brutal cost-of-living crisis. It was also a triumph of what I have come to think of as “no take, only throw” politics, a reference to an old Tumblr meme, itself a reference to a common experience among dog people: Fido demands that his human servant throw the ball, but won’t drop it. That’s politics today, more or less. Voters want big-government budgets and small-government taxes. Politicians act like they can give it to them.

cupcakelogic / Tumblr

But you can’t throw without taking unless you have a pocket full of tennis balls, which the United States Treasury, at the moment, does not. Americans can turn the tax code into Swiss cheese and keep levies on middle-class and upper-middle-class families low. They can spend trillions on initiatives to bolster the spending power of working households. They cannot do both of those things while keeping politically and societally destabilizing inflation in check.

Inflation hasn’t been in check since the arrival of the coronavirus pandemic. Prices are increasing nearly twice as fast as the Federal Reserve wants them to, and the costs of many essential goods and services are spiking even faster. Gas prices shot up nearly 60 percent from January to May. Employer-sponsored insurance premiums jumped 5 percent this year and are expected to increase another 8 percent to 11 percent next year. (The premiums on marketplace plans will rise as much as 13 percent come 2027.) The price of electricity has risen 11 percent year over year in Idaho, 14 percent in Maine, and 23 percent in Hawaii. The price of home insurance is up 9 percent around the country. Even coffee costs 41 percent more than it did two years ago, if you buy ground beans to brew at home.

These isolated bumps add up, compound, and bleed families dry. If prices had increased at the central bank’s target rate since Donald Trump was reelected, the typical middle-class family would have $1,800 more in its bank account than it does right now—cash to spend on Christmas presents or invest in a retirement fund. If inflation had been subdued since Joe Biden’s election, that same family would have saved more than $36,000 on everyday expenses. That’s enough to buy a used car. It’s a big chunk of a down payment on a typical apartment.

The post-COVID bout of inflation is not in and of itself responsible for the country’s cost-of-living crisis, which has been decades in the making. Land-use restrictions have throttled the housing supply, pushing up prices in dense cities and remote towns. A surfeit of subsidies, lack of cost controls, and consolidation in the health-care market has spurred insurers to jack up the price of coverage.

Still, not only has Washington failed to fix the housing shortage and rationalize the health-care system; it has stoked price increases across the whole economy. This year, it is spending $2 trillion more than it is receiving in revenue, a budget deficit equivalent to 6.2 percent of GDP. That’s almost as big a stimulus as the government was running in 2012, during the miserable jobless recovery from the Great Recession. Back then, the country desperately needed the deficit spending: The unemployment rate was four percentage points higher and the GDP growth rate markedly lower. Now Washington’s largesse is pumping up prices, forcing up interest rates, and making the rich richer. Indeed, Trump’s signature tax bill slashed federal tax revenue by $460 billion this year alone, with much of the relief going to wealthy households and wealthy corporations. (I should be fair: He did soak up some of the red ink by defunding HIV-prevention programs and taking food stamps away from kids.)

On top of that, Trump has engaged in a foolish trade war, driving up the cost of imported goods. He has engaged in a foolish war on immigration, shrinking the labor pool and increasing the cost of child care and construction. He has engaged in a foolish actual war, pushing up the cost of oil and gas. He has also promoted the AI build-out, which has pumped up borrowing costs and utility bills.

Democrats have regained power promising to undo Trump’s policies, in particular his cuts to Medicaid, SNAP, and health-insurance subsidies. It’s a priority for affordability reasons and moral reasons as well. The SNAP cuts are already stoking widespread food insecurity in Arizona, Florida, Georgia, and Nevada, among other states. The Medicaid-coverage losses are expected to spur dozens of safety-net hospitals to close and are projected to cause 51,000 unnecessary deaths. If Democrats regain the White House, they want to do a lot more: universal child care, prescription-drug-price controls, lowering the Medicare-eligibility age, maybe even Medicare for All.

These policies come with costs: $90 billion a year for restoring Medicaid, $20 billion for SNAP, $35 billion for the Obamacare subsidies. To cover them, Democrats are planning on reversing Trump’s tax cuts for rich families and corporations. Yet they are torn on whether and how to pay for new proposals going forward. (Lowering the Medicare-eligibility age to 60 would cost something like $25 billion annually; universal child care, $80 billion; single-payer health care, $2 trillion, offset by the evaporation of private-insurance premiums.) They are also torn on whether to make winnowing the deficit a goal unto itself. In an interview with The Wall Street Journal, Brian Schatz, a Democratic senator from Hawaii, objected to the idea that Democrats should have to worry about the budget when Republicans didn’t: “I don’t think we should play this game where the things we want have to be paid for and the things they want can be put on a credit card.”

On the revenue side, Democrats have vowed not to raise taxes on the 97 percent of American households earning less than $400,000 a year. (Trump’s tax cuts have made that vow a lot more expensive.) Democrats are considering further reducing the burden on working-class and middle-income households by expanding the earned-income and child tax credits. They’re also joining in the American public’s rollicking tax revolt, pushing carve-outs for seniors, tipped workers, hourly workers, police officers, public-school teachers, and homeowners.

Democrats are promising a whole lot of throw and not asking for a lot of take, in other words. This kind of budgetary posture would have worked when the economy was limping out of the global financial crisis in 2012, or when it screeched to a halt in 2020 because of COVID. It doesn’t really work now that deficits are contributing to excess inflation—as voters let Joe Biden and Kamala Harris know in 2024, and as they informed 132 Republican incumbents earlier this month.

Still, Democrats are not totally out of tennis balls. Ending the Iran war, easing immigration restrictions, and eliminating tariffs would blunt price increases or even lower prices without running up the debt, as would requiring the AI hyperscalers to pay for their own water and electricity and land, as would implementing cost controls for utilities, as would continuing to fast-track homebuilding in high-priced communities, as would breaking up corporate monopolies, as would having Washington negotiate prescription-drug prices for the American consumer market. (Heck, why not have it set payment rates for medical procedures and doctor visits too?) Getting the IRS to collect the taxes it is statutorily owed would reduce the deficit without harming the 97 percent. Pulling shut loopholes for and raising marginal tax rates on millionaires and billionaires would do the same.

In time, borrowing costs would ease, inflation would cool down, and the affordability crisis would let up, as Americans so desperately want. Maybe then we could finally have the conversation about how important drop it is to fetch.

Jared Kushner was incensed. On October 1, CNN had published an investigative report suggesting that he was profiting from the war in Gaza even as he was serving as a U.S. mediator working to resolve the conflict. Kushner’s private-equity firm, Affinity Partners, was the largest shareholder in an Israeli company called Phoenix Financial, which CNN journalists found had stakes totaling hundreds of millions of dollars in nine Israeli defense contractors. All nine companies saw their stock values rise last year, according to the news network, and many of them credited the Gaza war with boosting demand for their products.

In a set of lengthy internet posts, Kushner assailed the CNN report—and the conflicts of interest it accused him of—as unfair. Affinity Partners had made its first, $129 million investment in Phoenix in July 2024, four months before President Trump was reelected and half a year before Kushner returned to public service, at Trump’s behest, as a volunteer peace envoy, trying to negotiate settlements in the Middle East and Ukraine. Kushner stressed that he had no control over Phoenix’s investment choices—he called Phoenix “the Israeli equivalent of Vanguard or Fidelity,” companies that manage many investments—and that neither he nor Affinity owned any of the defense contractors directly. “Misleading stories,” he said on X, “will not distract me from the work President Trump asked me to do: help end wars, bring people together and pursue peace.”

Unmentioned in any of Kushner’s posts, however, was that Affinity also owned a $32 million stake in a sprawling American defense contractor—a stake purchased after Trump’s reelection and Kushner’s return to public service but not disclosed until November 5, two days after the midterms, when Affinity belatedly updated a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The filing, which has not been previously reported, revealed that Affinity had acquired 689,411 shares in a Connecticut-based company, called Arxis, that makes “mission-critical” components for F-35 fighter jets, Navy destroyers, nuclear submarines, and anti-missile systems, most of which have been deployed in the Middle East or Russia-Ukraine theaters in which Kushner has acted as a key negotiator.

[From the January 2027 issue: How Jared Kushner sparked a revolution in the Balkans]

Arxis comprises more than 30 aerospace and defense companies that were joined under a single umbrella and taken public this past April. In SEC filings this year, Arxis emphasized its dependence on the U.S. government for revenue, regulatory approvals, export controls, and security clearances. “The current administration is prioritizing investment in high-tech platforms including those already supported by Arxis (e.g., F-35 Joint Strike Fighter, DDG-51 destroyers, Virginia-class submarines and Next-Generation Overhead Persistent Infrared satellites),” Arxis said in the prospectus for its initial public offering.

Of the company’s $1.6 billion in revenue last year, some 47 percent came from defense and space spending. “We generate a significant portion of our revenue from suppliers and contractors for the U.S. government, particularly the Department of War,” Arxis said in its latest quarterly report. It also sells high-end components for military equipment directly to the U.S. government, and to foreign governments and their suppliers. The future, Arxis said in the prospectus, looked bright: “Ongoing global conflicts, coupled with the potential for future conflicts, have accelerated global defense investments.” U.S. military spending was projected to “reach $1 trillion by the fiscal year ended September 30, 2026, a substantial increase” over prior years that the company said was “driven by multi-theater conflicts and a more advanced and complex threat environment,” as well as “rising geopolitical tensions.” War, in short, had been good for business.

Arxis lost money in 2023 and 2024, according to its SEC filings, but turned profitable in 2025, the year Trump returned to office. Since going public in April, Arxis has seen its stock price rise by about 60 percent, peaking in early August before declining slightly. “In defense and space, we continue to benefit from our alignment to key government-spending priorities,” Kevin Perhamus, Arxis’s CEO, said in an earnings call this year. When a stock analyst asked for details, Perhamus said, “that’s air defense, radar, missile systems, missile-defense electronics, electronic warfare, modernization. You know, all of the priorities, the key government priorities that exist, we are broadly tied to and always have been.”

Under SEC rules, investment managers, such as Affinity, that own more than $100 million in certain kinds of public securities must disclose those holdings within 45 days of each quarter. But in mid-August, when Affinity filed its required paperwork, known as an SEC Form 13F, for the second quarter of 2026, the form made no mention of Arxis, even though Affinity had already acquired its shares in the company by then. The only shares it disclosed on the August form were $565 million worth of stock in a building-supplies company called QXO. Not until November 5—nearly three months after the August filing—did Affinity amend the form. The amendment declared the $32 million investment in Arxis as an omission from its second-quarter report, without explanation.

Neither Kushner nor the communications staff at Affinity Partners replied to repeated requests for comment by email, text, and phone today.

Kushner, Trump’s billionaire son-in-law, has been dogged for a decade by accusations of too freely mixing private and public business, first by failing to fully divest from his family’s real-estate empire and more recently as the owner of Affinity, which he founded in 2021. Kushner capitalized on relationships he built as a Middle East envoy during Trump’s first administration to raise as much as $6 billion for Affinity, the better part of it from sovereign-wealth funds in the same Gulf states he now deals with as a peace negotiator.

[Read: Jared Kushner’s mysterious role in the Trump administration]

Affinity has invested the funds it has raised in a variety of international businesses, including an online-classifieds company in the United Arab Emirates, a Brazilian fast-food chain, and the American video-game maker Electronic Arts. Arxis appears to be its first direct investment in the defense industry, a major departure from its other ventures, ripe with potential conflicts. As a commander in chief with little regard for bureaucracy or the will of Congress, Kushner’s father-in-law has broad power over military spending. Kushner, meanwhile, is mediating multiple global conflicts—Iran, Ukraine, Gaza—whose duration and outcomes bear directly on the defense industry.

Had Affinity disclosed its Arxis stake before the election—and had reporters picked up on it—it would have lent support to a spate of recent stories about Kushner’s alleged conflicts, including the CNN report, a New Yorker article from September, and a controversial book, published in late October, that claims that Kushner had access to secret bank accounts funded by the rulers of Saudi Arabia and the UAE. (He denies the book’s claim.) It would have also undercut his responses to the coverage, which included an uncharacteristically large number of social-media posts, denials, and counteraccusations from the usually reticent Kushner. His outpouring of indignation now seems to have taken place during a period in which he owned, but failed to disclose, a $32 million stake in an American defense company dependent on the government he served, and on the status of conflicts he was mediating, for a substantial portion of its revenues.

Was Kushner worried about how news of the investment might affect the midterms? It wouldn’t have been surprising. Democrats in Congress had named him among the top Trump-world figures they planned to investigate should they retake the majority.

As the wars in Ukraine and the Middle East have dragged on, Kushner’s interest in the defense industry appears to be growing. On November 12, a week after Affinity reported its stake in Arxis, another company announced Kushner’s firm as a new investor. The company, Q-Ctrl, sells a novel navigation technology sought by the U.S. military because it lacks the vulnerabilities of satellite-based systems such as GPS, which enemies can jam, spoof, or block—a problem endemic in recent conflicts. Last year, Q-Ctrl won a $24.4 million contract from DARPA, the Defense Department’s advanced-research arm, and a joint award, with Lockheed Martin, from the department’s Defense Innovation Unit. Among Q-Ctrl’s other investors is In-Q-Tel, a government-funded nonprofit created by the CIA to identify and develop new tech for intelligence and national security.

Before founding Affinity, Kushner had no substantive experience in private equity. One of the questions he’s faced is why nearly all of the money that Affinity has raised comes from Gulf royals with an existential stake in the results of his diplomacy. He now faces a new question: Why would a man working for peace be seeking to profit from war?

Election Denialism Is All but Dead

Out of all the surprises of the 2026 midterm election, among the most heartening was Donald Trump’s willingness to accept the results. “We’ll probably work something out,” he told The New York Times of the incoming Democratic majority in Congress. The next day, he walked the press around the site of...

Out of all the surprises of the 2026 midterm election, among the most heartening was Donald Trump’s willingness to accept the results. “We’ll probably work something out,” he told The New York Times of the incoming Democratic majority in Congress. The next day, he walked the press around the site of the planned White House ballroom, less interested in battling over the election than showing off his construction project.

This newfound tranquility did not last: In the weeks since the midterms, Trump has begun sharing posts on his Truth Social feed casting doubt on the integrity of the vote. “Does anybody really believe that this ‘Election’ was honest. How could the Polls be so wrong???” he asked on November 9. But a few stray posts on a bespoke social-media site are trifling compared with the aggressive campaign of propaganda, litigation, and brute force that Trump used to attempt to subvert the 2020 presidential vote. Six years after Trump launched the election-denialist movement, the Big Lie of election fraud might finally be ebbing as a defining force in American politics.

Kate Starbird, a University of Washington professor, monitored online election rumors in 2020 as they gained momentum. She has described the growth of election denialism that year as a dynamic of “participatory propaganda”—a feedback loop between political elites such as Trump, who seeded ideas about voter fraud, and the many Americans who responded to those suggestions by seeking out further evidence of supposed trickery. Through this process, what started with claims by Trump in the spring of 2020 about the supposed unreliability of mail-in ballots escalated into the attack on the Capitol on January 6. In the following years, election deniers continued to build their movement, taking positions as state and local election administrators and preparing to subvert—or, depending on your point of view, protect—the vote.

When Trump took power again in 2025, election denialism essentially became the official policy of the United States government. The FBI seized ballots from 2020 in Georgia’s Fulton County; the Justice Department demanded voter records from every state in the union, fueling theories that the voter rolls were riddled with inaccuracies; and, as the midterms grew closer, federal authorities grew more and more aggressive in seeking to shape the election in the GOP’s favor. Experts, election officials, and civil-society groups were wary that grassroots election deniers, together with the White House, might seek to upend either the voting process or the counting of ballots.

[Read: The election deniers are in retreat]

Yet many Republican candidates who had previously complained about rigged elections stayed quiet on the subject, perhaps worried that emphasizing claims of election fraud could push their supporters to stay home rather than vote. And as the magnitude of the Republican loss on Election Day became clear, election deniers remained similarly docile. Scott Perry, a Republican representative from Pennsylvania who drew the attention of federal prosecutors for his close involvement in the effort to overturn the 2020 election, conceded promptly to his Democratic challenger in remarks that emphasized the importance of “the peaceful transfer of authority that is paramount to our Republic.” Ali Alexander, a far-right activist and founder of the group Stop the Steal, which helped organize the rally at the Ellipse on January 6, 2021, released a statement on Telegram that “STOP THE STEAL SAYS MIDTERM WIPEOUT ELECTION WAS LEGITIMATE” and “WILL NOT PROTEST.”

As Election Day unfolded, Starbird told me, her team at the University of Washington’s Center for an Informed Public found that rumors of fraud didn’t gain the same traction as in 2020. When polls opened that morning, election workers in Westchester County, New York, discovered that voting machines were not properly scanning ballots—a logistical hiccup that generated conspiratorial online posts on both the left and right. The incident was similar to glitches in Arizona’s Maricopa County during the 2022 midterms, which grew into viral rumors of election fraud and became the focus of long-running complaints by Kari Lake, the GOP candidate who lost the Arizona governor’s race that year. Comparing posts on Twitter (now X) from both incidents, Starbird’s team found that “the overall content and attention around the events in Westchester was about 20 percent on Election Day as the attention around Maricopa,” Starbird said. In the days immediately after the vote, they continued to see only “very low” activity concerning election denial online.

At first, that quiet might have been attributable to Trump’s own apparent lack of interest in such conspiracies. Midterms typically occasion less public attention than presidential elections, and perhaps that was personally true for the president—who, unlike in 2020, was not on the ballot. In the absence of angry posts from the president, election deniers had little to coalesce around. Online interest in conspiracy theories increased once Trump started to dabble in his old fraud claims, Starbird told me, though still nowhere close to where it was in 2020. “They just don’t have all the ingredients they would need for a large-scale attempt to try to subvert election results,” she said.

The results themselves were arguably the biggest problem for any such attempt. The midterms were not just a Democratic victory but a total blowout—the biggest margin of House victory in a midterm for any party since the post-Watergate election of 1974. The resounding rejection of GOP governance left Republicans dejected, too stunned to attempt any unified subversion of the results. Major right-wing outlets such as Fox and Newsmax, which pushed election denial aggressively in 2020, did not attempt to rally the troops after 2026. “We’re seeing a few more activist journalists, people that work for hyperpartisan outlets, begin to play with this,” Starbird told me, but not “more mainstream conservative political voices.” After Trump shared speculation about rigged voting machines on Truth Social, the former Fox host Megyn Kelly posted on X, “Just stop.” Republican politicians, meanwhile, appear to have largely moved on to intraparty bickering.

[Jonathan Chait: The left’s election denial]

MAGA may have been cannibalized in part by its own success. In 2020, Starbird has argued, the Big Lie thrived on the participation of true believers. In 2026, with the federal government itself promoting election denialism, those supporters were less inclined to take action themselves. “They were waiting for the Trump administration” to sweep in and seize ballots or otherwise save the day, Starbird told me. When Trump didn’t—because he had no power to do so—the grassroots was no longer primed to take action. On X, posts from right-wing influencers toying with the notion of a stolen election have accreted bitter comments. “Nothing will be done. It’s all a big dog and pony show. Sick of it,” complained the prominent Trump supporter @catturd2 in a Friday post, responding to a post from GOP Senator Ron Johnson casting doubt on election results in the Alaska Senate race.

Perhaps conspiracy theories of election fraud have just gotten old. Like a network television show renewed one too many times, the characters are stale, the plots familiar, the twists lacking surprise. When the election-denial movement first took off in the spring and summer of 2020, it was responding to circumstances that were genuinely novel—a global pandemic that necessitated a rapid shift toward mail-in balloting—and drawing on the crackling right-wing political energy of anti-mask and -lockdown protests. Six years later, that energy has dissipated. Trump himself is no longer a crusading outsider but a symbol of the stagnant political establishment that, a decade ago, he promised to upend. Election officials have worked hard to allay public fears about the integrity of the electoral process. People have different things on their minds, such as inflation and high gas prices caused by Trump’s war of choice in Iran. A half-decade-old story about Democrats stealing elections doesn’t speak to those concerns.

“The kind of widespread election denialism that undermines trust in democracy, that could be used and weaponized to contest election results—I’m hopeful that that is not as much of a threat going forward,” Starbird told me. Still, she emphasized that election lies are not entirely gone. “On the right, there is a deep story about how elections have been rigged against them in recent years,” she said. “And we may see another rigged-election narrative” arise in the future.

What that narrative will be, though, remains undetermined. As Trump’s power wanes, the Republican Party and the American far right, newly freed from his orbit, will be forced to search for new causes to rally around. Election fraud may no longer be among them.

Pope Leo XIV is leading the Catholic Church during a period of extraordinary demographic change. A century ago, Europeans dominated the Church, representing some two-thirds of the world’s Catholics. Today, Latin America and Africa together make up a majority. As a result of this shift, influence within the Church is quickly being redistributed from the West to the global South, two regions with often-competing ideas about the faith.

As Leo emphasized on his landmark trip to South America this month, one of his principal goals is to keep the Church united. Maintaining unity has been a challenge for nearly every pontiff, but few of them have overseen a group of believers who are as far-flung and culturally diverse as the one he heads. Given his biography, Leo may be an ideal pope for the job. But he’ll have to bridge a set of divisions that are likely to intensify as the Church’s center of power continues to move.

In many ways, the Church’s demographic transformation was personified by Leo’s immediate predecessor, Pope Francis. In 2013, Jorge Mario Bergoglio of Argentina became the first pontiff from the global South. As pope, he paid special attention to migration, poverty, and climate change, focusing on the disproportionate burdens these issues placed on the developing world. He also emerged as one of the most prominent critics of the West, condemning capitalist excess and even challenging NATO.

[Read: Why Pope Francis isn’t with the West on Ukraine]

Leo, too, has embodied the Church’s transition from the West toward the South. He’s the first pope born in the United States, but his formative pastoral experience came in Peru, where he served as a priest and bishop for 20 years and, in 2015, became a citizen. “That act symbolized a bond that this people had been weaving into my life long before,” he recalled during his visit to Lima this month. “Today I understand how much of this land remains with me and how much of my own story also belongs to you.” The pope’s connection to Latin America will be an important asset to him now that the region accounts for more than 40 percent of Catholics, twice Europe’s share.

The global South’s growing representation has given it a stronger voice within the Church, and pastors there seem more and more unwilling to let Europe tell them what to do. Much of this resistance has come from Africa, the Church’s fastest-growing region, which Leo visited in April—the longest trip of his papacy until this month’s journey through South America.

In recent years, African bishops have criticized the Church’s “Eurocentric” agenda, which they felt imposed an overly liberal approach on certain social issues. At a Vatican synod in 2014, one German cardinal complained that merely discussing homosexuality with his African peers was “taboo” and therefore impossible. “They should not tell us too much what we have to do,” the cardinal said, a remark that prompted the African leaders to organize a separate meeting and come up with a unified response. Two years later, many African bishops condemned Francis’s efforts to allow divorced and remarried Catholics to receive Communion in limited circumstances. In 2024, they refused to implement a declaration by Francis that authorized formal blessings of people in same-sex unions.

During a synod held from 2021 to 2024, pastors from the Middle East, Asia, Africa, and Oceania repeatedly condemned efforts by Western leaders in the Church to push “niche issues” such as women’s ordination as priests and the relaxation of Church teaching on homosexuality. As Leo told me last year in his first sit-down interview as pope, a cardinal from the Middle East approached him during the synod and lamented that pastors in the West are “obsessed with sexuality.”

Like Francis, Leo has become one of the world’s leading advocates for the global South. In response to a question about migration during his return flight from Africa, he issued one of his most direct appeals on its behalf: “What is the global North doing to help the global South, or those countries where young people today cannot find a future and therefore dream of moving North?” the pope asked.

Leo has also become one of the loudest voices challenging the West, drawing the ire of some of America’s most powerful men. In my interview with Leo, he expressed concern about Elon Musk becoming the world’s first trillionaire, which prompted Musk to respond on X with a quote from the Gospel of Matthew: “Why do you see the speck in your neighbor’s eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye?” In September, the tech mogul Peter Thiel called the pope a “useful idiot” for the Chinese Communist Party because of his advocacy for stricter AI regulation. And most notable, President Trump lashed out at Leo in April when the pope condemned the Iran war.

[Peter Wehner: The American pope vs. the American president]

As part of his effort to restore U.S. dominance in Latin America, Trump has tried to curtail migration—another source of tension between the president and the pope. Thanks to the region’s swing to the right, Trump has found many leaders there who are sympathetic with him on the issue. Leo met with one of them this month in Argentina: President Javier Milei, who has courted Trump’s favor by offering his nation as a haven for data centers. Local Catholic leaders have criticized Milei, especially over his austere economic policies, which led some observers to expect that his meeting with Leo would be tense. But Milei became tearful as he said goodbye to the pope, a likely sign of how much influence Leo’s voice carries in the region, where he drew the largest crowds of any event he’s held, in Rome or abroad.

Throughout his travels, Leo has appealed to the Catholic roots shared by both secular European societies, such as Spain and France, and Latin America, where the faith remains culturally prominent but Mass attendance is falling. Last week in Argentina, Leo pointed to that shared heritage as a means of “learning to live together, even amid differences of opinion.”

This lingering cultural faith has been essential in Leo’s efforts to keep the Church united. But secularization is depleting its supply. As the Church’s demographics continue to change—and as its priorities change with them—Leo’s successors may have to find another source of unity.
