Toward the end of Homer’s Odyssey, Odysseus and Penelope finally see each other after 20 years apart. Odysseus, disguised as “an ancient old man” by the goddess Athena, tells Penelope that her husband will return to kill the suitors who have tormented her. “Not one will avoid his death and destruction,” he says. This is Homer’s protagonist: wily, aided by a god, and eager for revenge—reflective of a culture that prized honor, strength, and fealty to a set of interventionist deities.

This is not, however, Christopher Nolan’s protagonist. At key moments in the recent blockbuster adaptation of the ancient-Greek poem, Odysseus’s story seems more biblical than Homeric.

[Read: The Fear of Missing ]The Odyssey

In the film, when Odysseus reunites with Penelope, he hasn’t been turned into an old man. Nolan keeps his identity secret through more naturalistic means: a dimly lit room, a cloak over his body, and latticed sliding doors that separate the two characters. The doors, which are not found in Homer’s poem, are evocative of the screen between priest and penitent in Catholic confession—a setting that Nolan, who was raised Catholic, would be familiar with. And although in Homer’s poem, Odysseus is thirsty for retribution, Nolan emphasizes a different emotion in this scene, one that is integral to the confessional and to Christian life as a whole: contrition.

Odysseus describes deep guilt over his central role in a famous episode of the Trojan War: when Greek soldiers hid inside a wooden horse to trick the people of Troy into letting them into their city. “We left them a gift,” he tells his wife, “an offering of peace, that they took into their home.” In sacking Troy, Odysseus says, he and his fellow soldiers “violated all that’s ever sacred between people”—he recognizes a link between mistreating humanity and disrespecting divinity. He confesses that he “lost his way,” not just temporally or physically but in his soul.

Other aspects of Nolan’s adaptation feel biblical, even Christian, rather than Homeric—a quasi-baptism scene at sea, for example, and the conspicuous absence of the references to sex that populate the poem. The core principle of the film’s moral universe seems to echo the Bible. “Treat others as you would be treated,” Penelope declares early in the film, expressing an ethic that various characters refer to as “Zeus’s law.” Many versions of the idea exist, in secular and religious forms. But in the film, Penelope’s words sound more like Jesus’s Golden Rule than anything in Homer, where the heroes—though bound to host strangers as if they might be gods in disguise—seek to achieve glory more than convey empathy.

[Read: Reading a 3,000-year-old poem to a 3-year-old boy]

The film’s treatment of Odysseus’s lies, too, seems consonant with the Ten Commandments instead of Homer’s moral worldview. In the poem, Odysseus’s lies are often portrayed positively, as evidence of his intelligence and resourcefulness—when he tricks the Cyclops, for example. In the film, however, Odysseus’s falsehoods have dire consequences, an apparent reflection of the biblical assertion that false witness is a sin. Odysseus tasks a young soldier named Sinon—a character who does not appear in the poem—with offering the horse to the Trojans. But he doesn’t tell Sinon that its purpose is to attack the city. Sinon dies as a result of Odysseus’s deception. Later, in Hades, he admonishes his commander: “Did you have to lie to me?”

So did Christopher Nolan make a Christian film, which the Daily Wire founder Ben Shapiro and others have suggested since the movie’s release? Not quite. As my colleague Gal Beckerman wrote, Nolan diminishes the role of the gods, treating them as socially useful but not necessarily real. It’s hard to call definitively “Christian” a movie that regards the divine as a possible fantasy. The film is God-haunted, though. Homer may have viewed Odysseus’s deceit and pillaging as glorious. Nolan’s adaptation seems to pass a different judgment. Animated in part by the wisdom of the Bible, this modern Odyssey can’t help but see how the deceptions of war degrade a person and a culture.

Americans have two stereotypes of Britain. The first is the cozy, parochial world portrayed in my country’s biggest cultural exports—the “soggy bottoms” of The Great British Baking Show, the boarding-school backdrop of Harry Potter, the aristocratic deference valorized by Downton Abbey. The second, now promoted by a zillion MAGA podcasts, is a postapocalyptic hellscape of sclerotic socialism, repressed speech, and rampaging Muslims.

Rupert Lowe, the leader of the British far-right party Restore, has pulled off a neat trick. He sounds like an emissary from the first Britain, thanks to his refined accent and his taste for anecdotes about shooting pheasants. But he understands exactly what the modern American right wants to hear: that the second Britain exists, and only Donald Trump is preventing the United States from heading in the same direction.

Indeed, cranks and conspiracists on the American and British hard right now exist in happy symbiosis. In their ecosystem, Lowe fills a crucial niche: a guy with a posh voice who validates all of their prejudices. He recently appeared on The Joe Rogan Experience to, among other things, push the claim that gangs of Muslim men had raped 250,000 white girls in Britain.

This allegation is his entry ticket to the American podcast circuit. It arises from Lowe’s crowdfunded investigation of “grooming gangs”—a real and troubling phenomenon over the past few decades in several English towns, where men colluded with one another to recruit girls and women to sexually abuse. Early evidence suggests that, although the majority of perpetrators in these cases are white, British Pakistani men are overrepresented. Elon Musk loves talking about grooming gangs, because they justify his belief that Britain is a land of naive “hobbits” who need to be protected by “the hard men of Gondor.” Rupert Lowe, presumably, is one of those hard men.  

[From the July 2026 issue: How Britain became as poor as Mississippi]

Lowe warmed up for his star turn on Rogan by appearing on PBD, hosted by the Trump fan Patrick Bet-David, and DarkHorse, hosted by the former professors Bret Weinstein and Heather Heying. Weinstein now promotes conspiracy theories so arcane that the mainstream brain struggles to comprehend them, but he still remains friends with many members of the “intellectual dark web”—a group of heterodox commentators lionized by The New York Times in 2018. Right at the start of Rogan’s show, the podcaster thanked Weinstein, along with Musk and Jordan Peterson, for suggesting Lowe as a guest.

These chummy connections explain why Rogan granted a platform to a man whose party polled just 6.8 percent in a recent special election and has only a single member of Parliament, Lowe himself. (It’s as if a British podcast had seriously interviewed the U.S. Green Party’s Jill Stein about her plans for the presidency.) Lowe used to be a member of a much larger and more popular right-wing populist party, Nigel Farage’s Reform, but he was forced out in an enjoyably catty and melodramatic schism after calling for mass deportations.

Lowe’s marginal position in Britain doesn’t seem to bother his allies in the United States. These days, Farage—despite his personal relationship with Donald Trump—is not hard-line enough for the MAGA elite. They much prefer Lowe, who recently described part of the city of Manchester, in northern England, as a “third world shithole” and has promised that “under a Restore Britain Government, all immigration from Pakistan into our country will end.”

Rogan is a self-declared conspiracy theorist, and one of the reasons he has moved so sharply rightward in recent years is that American conservatism has become a reliable, prolific generator of conspiracy theories. Thanks to Trump’s influence, anyone who might have acted as a gatekeeper or guardian of reality has folded in the face of grassroots pressure to endorse a demon-haunted world of celebrity pedophiles and assassination cover-ups. The audience demands to have its suspicions upheld. Witness the awful spectacle of Kash Patel and Dan Bongino trying and failing to convince MAGA influencers that, now that they’d looked at the Epstein files, they’d realized they were no big deal.

Knowing Rogan’s attraction to the idea of numinous forces controlling the world, Lowe duly played him like a fiddle. He alluded darkly to the World Economic Forum, and the elite Bilderberg group, for reasons that did not need to be explained, because it was assumed that the audience already understood. Also, did Rogan know that the departing British prime minister, Keir Starmer, was a member of the Fabian Society? Of course Rogan did not, because—why would he? The Fabian Society is a gradualist-socialist think tank founded in 1884 by (among others) a vegetarian playwright. It was named after the Roman general Quintus Fabius, whose nickname was the Delayer, to emphasize that it was not a revolutionary organization. Membership involves absolutely no hooded robes, geopolitical puppet-mastering, or child sacrifices.

Nevertheless, Lowe represented the Fabians as a deeply sinister fifth column because their logo was once a wolf in sheep’s clothing. Rogan asked his producer, Jamie, to pull up the logo, and the two men marveled at it as though they were looking at a signed confession from Lord Voldemort himself. (Sadly, they did not inspect the society’s other early symbol, the tortoise, for potential darker meanings.) This is the conspiracist mindset in its full glory: They put their evil plan right there on the logo! That just shows how crafty they are.

Interview podcasts are where fact-checking goes to die. At one point, Lowe and Rogan joked about the hoary aphorism that says anyone who isn’t a liberal at 25 has no heart, but anyone who isn’t a conservative by 35 has no head. Wasn’t this something Winston Churchill had said, they wondered? Producer Jamie was dispatched to look this up, and the VAR judgment came back positive. Except the International Churchill Society has no record of the British leader ever saying this.

This relaxed attitude toward the facts allows Lowe to go on MAGA podcasts and offer a distorted vision of British public opinion, presenting himself as a persecuted truth-teller who is simply saying what everyone is thinking. He pandered to Rogan’s well-established love of hunting by claiming that his own freedom to bear arms was curtailed because Britain had banned handguns after “one murder”—an odd way to refer to the massacre of 16 children and their teacher at a school in Dunblane, Scotland. Since that incident, in 1996, the United States has had more than 1,500 school shootings. Britain has had zero. There is absolutely no wellspring of untapped energy in the country in favor of relaxing gun laws. Lowe was offering pure America-bait.

The most serious failure of fact-checking, however, came when the discussion turned to the report from Lowe’s grooming-gang inquiry, which he has promoted as the antidote to the liberal elite’s refusal to take the issue seriously. The document claims that a quarter of a million “young white girls have been subjected to repeated rape, gang rape, trafficking, torture, pregnancy, forced Islamic conversion, and lifelong trauma” in recent years. Even Rogan found this figure implausible: “The number, I want you to say it because it sounds so crazy,” he told Lowe. “If I say it, people—it’s going to sound wrong.”

Imagine a claim so outrageous that even Joe Rogan is nervous about repeating it—appropriately nervous, as it happens. The 250,000 figure comes from taking the estimated prevalence of sexual grooming in Rotherham, one of the worst-affected towns, and extrapolating that across all of Britain. That reasoning is obviously flawed. The report assumes that similar gangs are operating on the same scale everywhere—even in places where no such activity has ever surfaced. The real number of victims is unknown. Nevertheless, Lowe told Rogan that “a minimum of a quarter of a million rapes” was an “incredibly conservative” estimate. Musk has gone further and promoted posts claiming that there were 1 million victims, based on an unsourced figure used by a single politician a decade ago.

The existence of what experts call “group-based sexual exploitation”—wherein men band together to recruit and control victims—is not a conspiracy. Successive legal cases have demonstrated that in several towns across England, these gangs were disproportionately led by men of South Asian origin, and that police and local authorities were wary of publicizing this fact and of moving aggressively to smash the gangs, for fear of stoking racial tension. In three high-profile cases that resulted in criminal convictions—in Rotherham, Rochdale, and Telford—the majority of the men involved were South Asian. (White grooming gangs also exist, such as the ones in the rural county of Cornwall and in Glasgow, Scotland.) However, it is also true that the British far right—which is not normally noted for its ardent feminism—has latched on to this issue to press for mass deportations and to make the broader case against any Muslim or nonwhite immigration to Europe at all. During Lowe’s appearance on The Joe Rogan Experience, he frequently referred to America and Britain’s shared “Anglo-Saxon” culture.

[Idrees Kahloon: Burnham is Britain’s last chance before Farage]

Lowe is convinced that mainstream news outlets are ignoring his inquiry because of political correctness. The real reason is that his report is an unfocused jumble of personal testimony, with no evident attempt to corroborate the lurid and sometimes unbelievable accusations made by anonymous sources. In one case, a witness known as Kate reported that her captors had filmed her being raped by a dog—an extraordinary allegation. (When a New York Times opinion columnist made a similar accusation against Israeli prison guards, American conservatives were openly skeptical.) Kate also claimed to have seen two other girls killed in retaliation for snitching, and another anonymous victim is quoted as saying that she remembered seeing “a man opening the back of a van and I saw maybe 15, 20 girls locked in dog cages.” Corroborating evidence for those allegations has yet to materialize.

Lowe’s report has had little impact because of its obvious limitations. The government is currently setting up its own official inquiry into the gangs. Rape Crisis, one of the biggest mainstream charities working in the sector, commended the “courage of the survivors” in coming forward to Lowe’s team but added that “framing the issue squarely in terms of immigration” was reductive.

In the MAGA podcast-sphere, nailing down facts is less important than building a vivid narrative. Talking to Rogan, Lowe barely mentioned the victims of the gangs, because his interest was making the case for the mass deportation of Muslims from Europe. At the same time, he told Rogan that Britain is a “matriarchal society” where women are “omnipotent,” which is hard to square with girls being sexually exploited on an industrial scale.

Here in Britain, Lowe is one step up from a nobody, the leader of a second-rate populist party. Our right-wing media much prefers Nigel Farage, who is more careful with his language and who leads a genuine political movement that wins elections.

Farage has lost favor on the American right for the same reasons he has gained ground at home. He fell out with Musk because—I paraphrase lightly—the trillionaire felt that the Reform leader was insufficiently racist. Farage has always refused to admit the prominent far-right activist Tommy Robinson to his party, because the latter was once a member of an explicitly white-nationalist organization, the British National Party.

Farage’s serious desire to be prime minister has left a vacancy for chief British doomsayer to the American right—a vacancy into which Rupert Lowe has gratefully stepped. On the MAGA podcast circuit, the new kid on the block just has to call Britain a “shithole” to be received as a great oracle. Sorry, America, but I suspect that you haven’t heard the last of him.

Travel has a way of making us curious. We wander unfamiliar streets, strike up conversations with strangers, linger in museums, and notice details that we’d otherwise rush past. But that mindset doesn’t need to be reserved for vacations. Below, Atlantic writers and editors share their advice on how to rediscover the places they know best.

Stop at your neighborhood ethnic restaurant. This is on my mind because the World Cup just ended, and for each day of the tournament, I tried a meal from one of the countries playing that day. Over the course of 32 days, I ate food from 32 different places (the final was the first time I had to duplicate, eating Argentinean cuisine again), mostly without leaving the Washington, D.C., area.

Every day, I felt like I was taking a new trip. Two blocks from where my kids go to school, I found an Uzbek restaurant that I’d never known existed. I walked into an Egyptian place down the street from my house that I’d previously overlooked. When I went to a Haitian restaurant one evening, I felt like I’d been transported to Port-au-Prince. I had Senegalese food for the first time. I sampled ćevapi at a Bosnian restaurant and vori vori soup at a Paraguayan spot.

By doing so, I saw my neighborhood in a brand-new light and discovered what surprises are around the corner in even the most familiar places.

— Matt Viser, staff writer

***

One way to see the place where you live through fresh eyes is to borrow someone else’s. When a friend visits from out of town, I'll give them a little time, in deference to custom, to stretch their legs and get their bearings. Then they’d better buckle up, because I’m going into tour-guide mode. Did my college buddy who drove up from Baltimore bargain for a whirlwind circuit of my objectively not-that-interesting Delaware town? Doesn’t matter. The nerdy facts and local lore that I’ve accumulated over the years will come spilling out with rapid-fire enthusiasm and questionable accuracy. (“See that hill over there? General Washington once walked up it looking for the British, saw none, and walked back down.”)

Landmarks that I haven’t been to in years will be explored anew. Drinks and subpar nachos will be shared beneath the musty rafters of the tavern where the surveyors Charles Mason and Jeremiah Dixon once imbibed. Inevitably, my friend will ask questions about my town that I had never thought to ask. We’ll go looking for the answers. And new local knowledge will be gleaned, with which future visitors will someday be regaled.

— Will Oremus, staff writer

***

I live in Chicago—which means there’s no shortage of places to try and things to see. However, getting stuck in a routine of going to the same neighborhood spots can be easy. One way I’ve found to break myself out of that pattern is to tack on a new experience to something I already have to do: I’ll visit a nearby coffee shop after getting my driver’s license renewed downtown, I’ll get on the L train to dinner early enough to wander through an unfamiliar neighborhood, I’ll pop into a new grocery store after a workout class, or I’ll take the scenic route along the lake as I drive out to the suburbs. It’s a low-effort way to discover new corners of the city without the pressure of planning out an entire excursion—and it makes even the most mundane errands a little more exciting.

— Katie Anthony, associate editor

***

I’ve always found meeting strangers easier while traveling than when I’m at home. Planes are like dive bars, in the sense that within these dimly lit, claustrophobic spaces where drinks are served regularly, you may find yourself making friends with a seatmate in 32C. My brother once asked to borrow a laptop charger from a stranger at the airport, and somehow, that stranger ended up at my next birthday party. In new cities, taxi drivers and tour guides become temporary companions.

So why not re-create that openness at home? Host a party and tell people to bring plus-ones. Use those bruised bananas to make bread for the neighbor you still haven’t met. Buy coffee for the person standing in front of you in line at the café. Or here’s a ridiculous idea: Ask a stranger for directions—even if you already know where you’re going. Perhaps they’ll send you along a new route that you never would have chosen yourself.

— Lila Shroff, staff writer

***

This week, I’m visiting my hometown of Chapel Hill, North Carolina, for my grandfather’s 90th birthday. I come here at least three times a year, so I’m used to the same routine: my daily hour-long morning walk around the neighborhood, pickleball with my very competitive and skilled father, a pilgrimage to Franklin Street to relive my college days, and so on.

What’s new on this particular trip is the addition of my six-month-old son. He’s at a fun age where, with bright, Carolina-blue eyes, he perceives his surroundings and knows he’s somewhere unfamiliar but comfortable. Bringing him along for all of my Chapel Hill activities has given me a fresh perspective on coming home. By going with him to the local pool, putting him in a new He’s Not Here Blue Cup onesie, and seeing as much family with him as possible, I’m reexperiencing my hometown through his eyes. As my son takes in the sights and sounds of North Carolina, I gain a deeper appreciation for where I grew up and for my own parents, who created those traditions for me.

— Michael Leibel, senior editor

***

Take a city tour. A long time ago, I spent a couple of summers working as a tour guide and bus driver in Alaska, where I would lead tours of Anchorage and Valdez. Whenever somebody local would come aboard (usually with family from out of town), I’d be nervous, afraid they’d be bored or that I might get some facts wrong. Instead, I almost always heard that they had come away with a new outlook on a place that they passed by every day or had always meant to visit.

You don’t even need to have out-of-town guests as an excuse—just hop on a tour bus or boat for an afternoon, and you’ll almost certainly notice something you’d overlooked before.

— Alan Taylor, senior editor

The Problem With Shutting Down ICE in Blue States

Photographs by Klaus Galiano

It was probably inevitable that the Delaney Hall immigration detention center in Newark, New Jersey, would become a battleground in the broader fight over President Trump’s mass-deportation campaign. The privately run jail reopened last year as the only sizable facility of its kind in the New York City metro area, with capacity for 1,000 ICE detainees. Trump officials have been threatening for months to intensify ICE enforcement in New York, making Delaney Hall essential to their plans. It’s also smack-dab in a blue sea of state and local governments dominated by Democrats whose constituents are furious at ICE.

Protesters have rallied outside Delaney Hall since May to denounce poor conditions inside the facility, operated by the GEO Group on a $1 billion, 15-year contract. Trump officials say that complaints about the facility are overblown and that protesters’ true goal is to drive out ICE.

New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill and other prominent Democrats have joined calls for Delaney Hall to be shut down, and they’re suing the Trump administration to block ICE plans for a larger jail in another part of the state. 

In recent years, blocking or limiting ICE jails has generally been an effective tactic for immigration activists because it constrains the government’s ability to arrest and hold large numbers of immigrants. It also drives up costs by forcing ICE to relocate detainees. Squeezing ICE’s capacity has worked in tandem with so-called sanctuary policies adopted by state and local jurisdictions that try to limit or prohibit cooperation with federal immigration enforcement. The result is a geographic imbalance in ICE infrastructure: There are relatively few detention centers close to northern and coastal cities with large immigrant populations, while a disproportionate number of the agency’s jails are concentrated in Texas and Louisiana. 

But the keep-ICE-out approach has downsides that Democrats rarely acknowledge. And now that ICE is swimming in cash, a strategy that relies on starving ICE of resources might turn out to be not just futile, but counterproductive. 

Klaus Galiano
A protest outside Delaney Hall on May 28

Since Trump returned to office, Republicans have used the budget-reconciliation process to channel more than $100 billion in additional funding to ICE, 10 times the agency’s annual budget. ICE opponents can make it harder to open new immigration jails in blue cities and states, but the administration now has all the money it needs to ship detainees to southern states, where detention costs are generally lower. When Tom Homan, Trump’s “border czar,” traveled to New Jersey last month to defend conditions inside Delaney Hall and meet with Democratic officials, he said protesters’ calls to close the jail would backfire.

John Gihon, a former ICE attorney in Florida who is a board member of the American Immigration Lawyers Association, told me that when ICE facilities shut down in Democratic-run states and cities, it’s “a small-time win” for activists but it leaves detainees worse off.

“ICE is going to find the bed space,” Gihon said. “A lot of time, it’s going to be farther away from a blue state, and farther away from friendly federal courts, in a place with terrible conditions.” In addition to being more distant from family members, immigration courts in Texas and Louisiana have judges who reject asylum claims at some of the highest rates in the country, federal data show. And sanctuary cities such as New York and Chicago generally have more legal-aid organizations that can help ICE detainees get out of custody and fight deportation. “When I have a detention center near me that I can drive to, I never have to worry about my client missing a phone call,” Gihon told me. 

That’s not to say that advocates have no reason to fight for better conditions, or to get immigrants out of ICE custody. At least 52 detainees have died while detained by ICE since Trump returned to office, two and a half times the rate of fatalities from his first term, according to a report published last month by Human Rights Watch.

But Democrats calling for Delaney Hall to close should be careful what they wish for, Gihon told me. “If you shut that place down, everybody gets sent to Louisiana,” he said. “And most of them are not going to get out.”

ICE relies on a patchwork of jail facilities and immigration detention centers, many of which are privately run, to hold the immigrants it arrests and seeks to deport. The agency currently has about 65,000 detainees in custody nationwide, down slightly from January but still close to record levels. Trump officials say they need to boost detention capacity to 100,000 to hit their goal of 1 million deportations a year.

Opponents of Trump’s immigration crackdown have scored some victories lately in their battle to stop the expansion of ICE detention centers. The “Alligator Alcatraz” camp in the swamps of south Florida has been closed. In Maryland, a federal judge has blocked ICE’s attempt to open the state’s first large immigration detention facility. And Trump officials have scaled back plans to create a national network of ICE megajails out of converted warehouses, and are instead preparing to sell or repurpose several properties purchased during a billion-dollar spending spree last winter.

The backlash to ICE has picked up momentum around the country as more Democrats condemn the agency and endorse calls to eliminate it. A YouGov poll conducted in March found that three-quarters of Democrats and more than half of independents favor abolishing ICE. The survey was conducted after the killing of two U.S. citizens protesting the ICE crackdown in Minneapolis.

[Read: ‘Abolish ICE’ is back ]

Homan, who used to run ICE, has waged a counter-messaging campaign in defense of his former agency and its legal authorities. He met with Newark Mayor Ras Baraka to ask for more support from city police, and ate a meal in the Delaney Hall cafeteria, pronouncing the spaghetti “good.” And he leveled a warning last month to Sherrill and other Democrats calling for the jail’s closure. "You want to shut down the facility?” Homan said. “Then every illegal alien we arrest in that state, we’ll fly to Texas. We’ll fly to Arizona, detain them there, because we’re going to keep arresting people. We’re going to keep detaining people. We’re going to keep removing people.”

Detainees picked up by ICE in the New York City area and held at Delaney Hall remain close to their families. (One of the demands of recent protests was increased visitation hours.) If the facility is shut down, Homan said, immigrant families will see their loved ones shipped out of state. “How does that benefit the migrant community?” he asked.

Attorneys and immigrant advocates I’ve spoken with deplore Homan’s threats, but acknowledge that ICE’s funding surge has put them in a bind. They’re not giving up calls to shut down immigration jails and stop ICE from opening new ones. Yet for detainees who are already in ICE custody, they concede, that approach is likely to make outcomes worse. The rates at which immigration judges grant protection to immigrants seeking asylum vary widely, for instance; New York City courts have some of the highest approval rates, while courts in Houston have among the lowest.

The advocates I spoke with said ICE opponents, by and large, have not yet reckoned with the implications of ICE’s new financial resources, and remain committed to tactics that try to limit arrests and deportations by making it as difficult as possible for the agency to operate in their communities.   

Claire Trickler-McNulty, an attorney who oversaw ICE policy and planning under Joe Biden and who now advises advocacy organizations, told me the agency’s geographic imbalance developed partly because detention facilities in southern states were lower cost and logistically convenient for deportation flights or bus transfers to Mexico. Trickler-McNulty said activists who want to fight Trump’s crackdown should call for safe, humane detention and, more important, fewer detainees. “My view is you want to be very clear on who needs to be detained and for how long, and manage those cases very closely, so that people aren’t spending too long in detention,” Trickler-McNulty said. “If we accept the premise that we are going to have immigration detention for a certain segment of the population, then that is what you would want.”

ICE officers arrested nearly 40,000 people last month nationwide, the highest one-month total since Trump reentered office, according to the agency’s most recent data. Thanks to a hiring surge last fall, ICE has added about 8,000 officers, doubling the size of its workforce. Homan has threatened to marshal those forces to surge operations in New York City and other sanctuary jurisdictions, where immigrants generally face lower risk of arrest and deportation.

Last January, soon after Trump took office, I was invited on a ride-along with William Joyce, the second-highest-ranking official in New York City for ICE’s Enforcement and Removal Operations division, which handles deportations. Joyce told me he had fewer than 100 detention beds available, leased from a jail about 60 miles north of the city, in Orange County, New York. When space ran out there, he had to send detainees to jails in Pennsylvania, Virginia, or farther south. The lack of ICE-jail capacity in the New York City metro area has led to overcrowding at the agency’s processing center inside 26 Federal Plaza in Lower Manhattan. (A suspect was arrested there Monday after a failed arson attack at the building’s entrance that did not result in serious injuries.) Joyce, like a lot of ICE officials in sanctuary jurisdictions, had a limited budget to work with, and prioritized using the available jail space for detainees with serious criminal histories over those whose only violations were immigration-related. 

Such trade-offs are no longer necessary. Nearly 40 percent of immigrants taken into custody have no criminal records, according to Homan. “We’re arresting everybody we find,” he said Tuesday on a podcast hosted by Katie Miller, the wife of the Trump aide Stephen Miller.

The ICE detainee population has also soared because the Trump administration has made it harder for judges to issue bonds that would allow immigrants fighting deportation to be released and continue working while they await their next court appearance. And the Laken Riley Act, signed by Trump nine days after taking office, has expanded the range of criminal charges that render someone ineligible for release, to include shoplifting and theft.

Last fall, then–Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem oversaw the purchase of at least 11 large warehouse properties around the country at a cost totaling more than $1 billion. The acquisitions were part of a $38 billion ICE plan to build a national network of megajails with capacity for as many as 10,000 detainees. But the agency faced intense opposition to the plan, including in Republican-led states, and ICE officials found themselves facing a red-tape labyrinth as they sought permits from local governments for their sewer and water upgrades. The warehouse purchases, which were directed by Noem and her de facto chief of staff, Corey Lewandowski, are now under internal investigation by DHS’s inspector general.

DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin, who replaced Noem in March, has said the department is scaling back and reevaluating the ICE-warehouse plan. Department officials say they may repurpose or sell several of the warehouses, boosting ICE capacity elsewhere by purchasing privately run facilities from ICE contractors and adding space at existing jails.

Trump officials are still looking to boost their jail capacity in the Northeast. After the administration bought a large warehouse in Roxbury, New Jersey, for $129 million in February, state officials and the Republican-led town council sued to stop ICE from converting it into a detention center, citing environmental concerns. The government said last month that it was no longer planning to go forward with the conversion, but after Sherrill celebrated the announcement as a “major victory,” Mullin abruptly reversed course, and declared that the department “will NEVER back down.” As of last week, attorneys for the Trump administration told the court they’re still figuring out what to do with the warehouse, and they gave no timeline for the decision.

Suavity is a sorely missed ingredient in the American presidency. The effortless charm of a Roosevelt or a Reagan, a Bush or a Clinton, projected a particular brand of power. Suave means a kind of athletic ease and self-command in difficult, spotlighted circumstances, the assurance that solutions are at hand if only we all hold our composure and act right-headedly. Here was the ultra-suave Barack Obama’s message to the basketball player Caitlin Clark in a small but noteworthy exchange over the WNBA All-Star weekend: It never hurts to be winsome. In fact, it can help you win.

Can you imagine Donald Trump in the same situation, what he’d have done or said, the race-baiting and appeal to baseness? As if Trump would even deign to meet a WNBA player, that is. He prefers to use the league’s players from afar for his own purposes. His White House has made the WNBA a flash point for attacks on transgender athletes, and he and his hard-right allies have tried to make Clark out to be a victim of racial violence on the court. Congressional conservatives even called for the federal government to investigate the WNBA for civil-rights violations because of players fouling her. Here is how Trump used another young woman he views as an adversary, the CNN anchor Kaitlan Collins, at the rescheduled White House Correspondents’ Dinner yesterday, while Obama was visiting WNBA players in Chicago: After Collins received an award for “excellence under deadline pressure,” Trump barked from the podium that it was a “fake” prize, that she didn’t deserve it, and then he went to some lengths to demean her. Not suave. Not winning.

The WNBA has been difficult to watch at times this season, so rife with racial tensions, toxic fan behavior, and carping over officiating that it seems a microcosm of a country in crisis over dirty play in seemingly every societal arena. Much ill will has swirled around Clark, the superstar 24-year-old guard for the Indiana Fever, who can be either mesmerizing to watch or tiresome, depending on how aggrieved she feels on the day. Clark has complained that she is intentionally targeted with hard fouls—and sometimes she is, but not because she’s white. Mauling is the guaranteed, time-honored way to stop an otherwise insuperable player. Clark is the fastest in league history to hit 200 three-pointers, the first to ever score 45 points with 11 assists in a game, and such a head-clutchingly great passer that she can make defenses look like they’re lost. And Clark’s got a plenty hard edge of her own. She can jaw so vociferously at referees that she’s gotten seven technical fouls this season, one away from a suspension.

That has done nothing to help calm matters in a league whose product is being torn apart by internal resentments and external malignity. In mid-June, when Alyssa Thomas of the Phoenix Mercury was suspended for a game for hitting Clark in the throat—inadvertently, according to Thomas—she received death threats, was subjected to racial slurs, and inspired those bad-faith congressional demands for an investigation. “Now we’re being painted as thugs,” Thomas told reporters. In mid-July, Chelsea Gray of the Las Vegas Aces, who is almost as superb a player as Clark, received a vile message after her team lost to the Fever. Gray decided to post it on social media so everyone could see what Black players can deal with when they go against the league’s white darling. You s* a** ugly a** read a message that also called her the N-word. Gray wrote on her post, “People act like we just make this s* up.”

That was the backstory when Obama surprised the WNBA All-Stars players by walking in on them during practice for tonight’s game. They were milling around an indoor court at the newly opened Obama Presidential Center on Chicago’s South Side when the man with his name on the building sauntered across the floor in jeans and a white broadcloth shirt to welcome them—and to gently remind them of certain truths: It has taken 30 years of effort by underpaid and unsung athletes for the league to finally arrive as a popular mass spectacle, and no matter what team the players are on, they’re all in that endeavor together. The WNBA’s growing commercial success is based in its relatability and affordability compared with men’s pro leagues, but also in the audience’s sense that it is watching not just a game but the slow triumph of women gaining power for themselves. “People paying attention to your excellence, I think, has been something, for basketball fans generally, a sight to see,” Obama told them.

The former president took pains to make clear that he’s a genuine follower of the league. He made small remarks to various players about their seasons as he posed for photos. Then Obama spotted Clark. “Oh hey!” he said. “Caitlin! You’re great!” He leaned in closer, to deliver a more personal message, picked up by a hot mic. “Don’t let all this nonsense get you down,” he told her. “I always say one of the things that was luckiest for me was that I didn’t get famous ’til I was like 45. So I was an old-ass man. I mean, you’re my daughter’s age. And to have that much burden and, you know, nonsense. So don’t lose that joy. The game’s, like, the game. And you’re a hooper. And you hoop.”

“I try—sometimes,” she said shyly.

“You’re always hooping,” he said. “And you’re on a nice little run right now.”

There was a sweetness in the moment, a subtle teaching. In the nicest way, Obama shored Clark up, but also reminded her that she is a greater player and public figure when she just plays the game, accepts the inevitable bad calls and frustrations, and doesn’t let the web-brewed poisons into her blood. Left unspoken was the fact that no one has been subject to more unfairness than Black women who can’t buy a call from refs, and don’t get their own signature sneaker deals, either. Obama chose not to corner Clark on that point. Instead, he gave her grace. And perhaps because he did so, she will have more grace with her opponents—and they will, with her.

Clark is a peerless young player who can be a bit of a brat when she gets batted around. She’s learning. “Sometimes, I think, people don’t always realize, like, I’m 23 or 24 years old and I’m trying to navigate a lot of different things while trying to play basketball at the same time,” she told the press after meeting Obama. “So, it was cool to hear that from him and I think, honestly, it’s probably a good reminder for all of us to treat people with respect, treat people with grace.”

It was a quietly interesting and complicated encounter, and a heartening reminder of what “presidential” can look like. Clark already knows how to win. But Obama gave her a lesson in how to be winning.
