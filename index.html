Hegseth’s Pentagon Lurches off the Rails

Making Pete Hegseth the secretary of defense was always a huge gamble. Making Pete Hegseth the secretary of defense was always a huge gamble. A television host with little background in national-security or defense affairs, who topped out as a major in the National Guard, he was nominated based on the same qualification shared by most of Donald Trump’s other appointees: loyalty. And now the price of that gamble is evident. The Pentagon is careening offtrack under a secretary of defense who is firing or forcing the resignations of officers and civilian officials, not because they are unqualified but because he believes they are insufficiently loyal to him, personally.

The latest casualty of Hegseth’s ongoing purge is Army Secretary Dan Driscoll, who resigned today after repeatedly clashing with the secretary of defense. As my colleagues Nancy A. Youssef, Missy Ryan, and Michael Scherer report, a person familiar with Driscoll’s resignation said that he had “brought up concerns with the administration surrounding Army transformation and readiness and Hegseth’s blocking those efforts specifically by firing the Generals who were responsible.” In the careful circumlocutions of Washington-speak, that means that Driscoll went to the White House to complain about Hegseth, and the president sided with Hegseth.

But more issues were in play than “Army transformation.” Hegseth’s tenure has been disastrous on multiple scores. Instead of giving sound advice to the commander in chief and ensuring that America remains secure while the president fights a deeply unwise war of choice, he has been pursuing his political grievances about DEI and other pet peeves against the U.S. military. Hegseth seems to have a special animus toward the Army, an institution he claims “spit me out.” He fired Army Chief of Staff General Randy George, and he has pushed out other senior Army officers; at this point, the Army has no Senate-confirmed chief of staff or secretary. (George’s replacement, General Christopher LaNeve, so far does not have the necessary votes to be confirmed.)

Even in less dangerous times, Hegseth’s irresponsible behavior would be a hazard to American national security. The Defense Department is a huge bureaucracy; it requires a leader who understands more than the mechanics of teaching trainees how to do push-ups or how to handle a weapon. Secretaries of defense must grasp the economics of the defense industry, the nature of alliances, congressional politics, the policy process, and dozens of other issues for which Hegseth seems to care little and about which he knows even less.

In a time of war, however, Hegseth’s shortcomings may prove deadly. Leave aside his early stumbles, including the mishandling of sensitive information. Leave aside, even, that Hegseth dissembled about the severity of Iranian attacks on U.S. bases in the Persian Gulf that had succeeded in disrupting the logistical support for American forces in the theater and led to the kind of deprivations that were suffered by the crew of the aircraft carrier the USS Abraham Lincoln. Hegseth’s incompetence, and his commitment to staying in Trump’s good graces, helped launch the war itself in the first place.

Hegseth was reportedly virtually alone among senior officials in supporting Trump’s decision to attack Iran—when he should have been the one person in the room who knew better. The secretary of state and CIA director were against the idea. According to the The Wall Street Journal, even then–Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard, another of Trump’s unqualified-but-sycophantic appointees, mustered the courage to give the president bad news, telling Trump that killing the reigning ayatollah and striking Iran would “likely usher in a more hard-line regime open to acquiring nuclear weapons”; that the Iranians “would rush to close the Strait of Hormuz, destabilizing the global energy market”; and that Iran “would strike U.S. forces and partners in the Middle East,” all of which happened almost immediately after Trump gave the order to go to war.

Hegseth, however, supported Trump’s fantasies of a quick victory. A man of principle would have warned his president even at the risk of his job, but Pete Hegseth is not that man.

And now Hegseth has banished another enemy, Driscoll, from the Pentagon. But as the saying goes: Play stupid games, win stupid prizes. Hegseth, more than ever, now owns every aspect of the Pentagon’s dysfunction. Yesterday, The Washington Post reported that multiple senior military leaders—those still left, that is—have warned Hegseth that the United States cannot indefinitely extend military operations against Iran. Even the chief of naval operations, Admiral Darryl Caudle (who campaigned for the job by signaling his agreement with the secretary’s culture-war obsessions), told Hegseth that the Navy cannot “sustain its current level of support for the Iran conflict because there is no anticipated end date.”

Hegseth’s response to this story was to send his spokespeople out to castigate the press for revealing classified information. In an amateurish bungle, the Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell said that the reports were “inaccurate” but also a “betrayal of the force”—leading sensible people to ask how the stories could be false and dangerous at the same time. And these were just the responses from people who are officially authorized to speak for the Pentagon; another bombshell report over the weekend revealed that the department has recruited online influencers into paid roles, but without either the influencers or the Pentagon telling anyone about their cozy arrangement with the government.

Hegseth, meanwhile, has dutifully overseen the raiding of assets from other theaters to support Trump’s war with Iran, leaving the U.S. and its allies exposed to potential threats from China and Russia. When Trump returned to office, Hegseth and others supported a reorientation away from the Middle East and Europe. Now the secretary and the other supposed China hawks have discarded those positions and dumped everything into a war that still has no clear strategy and no clear goal beyond stopping Iran from attaining a nuclear weapon it didn’t have in the first place.

Fortunately, even under the strain of a petty and incompetent secretary, and a president mired in his delusions, senior military officers are still speaking up and telling their boss the truth about America’s parlous defense situation. One of the advantages of a large bureaucracy staffed with professionals is that much of it can run and function, at least for a while, under even the most inept leaders.

But Hegseth is apparently close to getting the Pentagon he wants: Much like his boss at the White House, Hegseth seems to desire a building full of loyalists who put the boss’s personal fortunes over the national interest. Some reports indicate, for example, that Hegseth wants to replace Driscoll with Parnell—who has no significant qualifications for the post beyond his time fighting with the press on the secretary’s behalf.

The result of all this is that the United States is losing a war to a third-rate power in the Middle East. The Russians, meanwhile, are continuing their rampage in Europe and apparently considering things so perilous that the CIA director had to fly to Moscow just to warn the Kremlin off of whatever it was thinking of doing. The Chinese are watching as Trump and Hegseth have stripped the Pacific theater of its only American aircraft carrier. In the midst of this chaos, Hegseth does chin-ups while he ponders calling all of America’s admirals and generals back to another mass meeting so he can again carp at them about fat soldiers and bearded sailors.

Pete Hegseth has failed in every mission except one: keeping his job. But firing or forcing out everyone else in the Pentagon will not make Pete Hegseth into the secretary of defense America and its men and women in uniform need. With each dismissal, he exposes only his own insecurities, even as he manages to stay employed—but the people and the armed forces of the United States are paying the inevitable price of his incompetence.

Dan Driscoll’s Parting Shot

During a White House meeting last week, Army Secretary Dan Driscoll brought his concerns about the future of the Army under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth directly to President Trump—a sign of how far tensions between the two Pentagon leaders had escalated, and of the dysfunction inside the building, four people familiar with the conversation told us.

Trump was surprised when Driscoll told him how many generals and other top officers had been fired, pushed out, or passed over for promotion under Hegseth, one of the people said. Driscoll argued that the Army could not transform itself the way it needs to, because of the departure of so many leaders, this person said, adding that Trump expressed concern about the deep cuts to the Army’s senior leadership. Driscoll informed the president of his intention to resign—and by today, Driscoll was on his way out.

Hegseth’s purge of the military has been so dramatic that with Driscoll gone, the military’s largest service is left without a top civilian leader, a Senate-confirmed top general, a confirmed Army general in charge of operations, or a confirmed commander of Army troops in Europe. Because of all the firings across the service over the past year, the Army, with more than 450,000 active-duty soldiers positioned across the globe, has fewer generals than at any point in recent history. Driscoll’s personal appeal to Trump just days before his exit was the most direct articulation of high-level concerns among Pentagon leaders about the strains facing the force—including Hegseth’s intervention in Army personnel decisions and the mounting demands of the Iran war.

The White House spokesperson Anna Kelly said that Driscoll had been “highly effective” in improving soldier readiness and advancing other administration priorities. “The United States Army is more powerful than ever thanks to his work alongside the Commander-in-Chief and Secretary of War,” Kelly wrote in a statement, using the administration’s preferred term for the Department of Defense.

Driscoll’s spokesperson declined to comment, and Driscoll did not respond to a call seeking comment. But a person familiar with his resignation told us that Driscoll had “brought up concerns with the administration surrounding Army transformation and readiness and Hegseth’s blocking those efforts specifically by firing the Generals who were responsible.” Hegseth did not have an immediate response to Driscoll’s departure.

[Read: The military Pete Hegseth wants]

Since his arrival at the Pentagon early last year, Hegseth—a former Fox News host who appealed to Trump loyalists by deriding a “woke” military and advocating for leniency for troops charged with war crimes—has sought to reshape the Army. He has placed like-minded generals in positions of influence, intervened to stymie the promotions of lower ranking-officers, and gutted diversity initiatives. The result was an Army depleted of leaders, and an Army secretary and a Pentagon chief at constant odds. Hegseth has ended dozens of Army careers, all without any clear explanation. He now has driven out the secretary of the Army.

Driscoll’s departure puts the Pentagon even more firmly under the control of Hegseth and his MAGA agenda. Driscoll is a confidant of Vice President Vance, a relationship that gave him access to the West Wing, and at times Driscoll served as a counterweight to Hegseth’s impulses, but only up to a point. Some current and former defense officials told us they believe Driscoll’s enmity toward Hegseth actually had the unintended consequence of broadening the purge of the uniformed ranks. And yet Driscoll’s departure, which had been expected for months, and the prospect that he will be replaced by someone more politically aligned with Hegseth are already raising fears inside the Army. “What happens then? Generational damage,” a defense official told us.

Driscoll arrived at the Pentagon in February 2025, a third-generation Army soldier who shared several things in common with Hegseth, his new boss. Like Hegseth, Driscoll joined the military in the most muscular years of Washington’s post-9/11 wars. Both men served in Iraq in a company-grade role. (Hegseth, a National Guardsman, also deployed to Afghanistan.) Both returned with sharp criticisms of America’s overseas adventures and the military’s hidebound ways. Both attended Ivy League schools: Hegseth, Princeton and Harvard; Driscoll, Yale Law, where he befriended the fellow veteran Vance. Both Driscoll and Hegseth have made unsuccessful runs for political office. Both have also eyed a return to politics. As Army secretary, Driscoll frequently reminded people that he had a direct line to the vice president, people familiar with the matter said, and told others that he had ambition to someday run for president himself—an ambition that Hegseth is also said to harbor.

But the two men differed starkly in temperament and style. Hegseth, who required an unprecedented tie-breaking Senate-confirmation vote from Vance after failing to win any Democratic support, is one of Trump’s most reliable MAGA warriors. He has publicly boasted about firing “woke” generals, dared military dissenters to quit, and demonized the mainstream media. Driscoll also supported administration policies but has done so in a more genial way that appealed to many lawmakers more than Hegseth’s combative approach before Congress. And unlike Navy Secretary Hung Cao, who has echoed Hegseth’s scornful and partisan tone on social media, Driscoll has steered clear of endorsing Hegseth’s most controversial actions or most of his culture-war priorities. Unlike Hegseth, Driscoll is friendly with the press, cultivated ties with lawmakers of both parties, and courted technology leaders as he promoted the Army’s sprint to transform how it wages war. That served him well in the administration, earning him additional responsibilities as director of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms, and Explosives and later as an unofficial mediator for the war in Ukraine. None of that sat well with Hegseth.

Army personnel appointments were a particular point of friction. Hegseth has not hesitated to push aside or promote officers as he sees fit, in many cases in ways that have irked others in the Pentagon. Driscoll advocated for General Randy George, the Army chief of staff whose name appeared on a list circulated before Trump’s return to office of senior officers who should be fired due to perceived transgressions against the MAGA agenda. Although Driscoll may have bought George time, Hegseth ultimately fired the general in April. Chris Donahue, the four-star general who headed U.S. Army Europe and Africa, followed several months later.

The Army has been without a top commander since George’s firing. General Christopher LaNeve, who was picked by Hegseth as the No. 2 officer in the Army, has been acting in that role, often going straight to Hegseth for decisions, bypassing Driscoll, according to defense officials. Hegseth is expected to nominate LaNeve to lead the Army, but Capitol Hill is already signaling that LaNeve would struggle to be confirmed, because of many lawmakers’ antipathy to Hegseth. “We are not going to reward Hegseth for what he did to the Army,” one congressional staffer told us.

[Read: Americans deserve answers from Hegseth]

The Army has long prided itself on representing the diversity of the country, but under Hegseth, minorities and women have been pushed out or barred from promotion. The military has long sought to be apolitical, but under Hegseth, troops are measured not by combat experience but by their perceived loyalty to the administration. Among the generals fired have been some of the most experienced and decorated in the service. Each departure marks the loss of decades of investment in that soldier and raises questions about how future troops can rise through the ranks.

Although Army Undersecretary Michael Obadal is expected to take over in the near term, many Pentagon officials fear that the administration will nominate the Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell, another Army veteran, to replace Driscoll. Parnell, who previously made a failed bid for U.S. Senate in Pennsylvania, shares Hegseth’s pugilistic spirit and interest in culture and personnel issues. Like Hegseth, Parnell has no experience managing a large organization and would face a contentious confirmation hearing. A new Army secretary who is more politically driven—and likely less experienced and less well connected than Driscoll—could keep the service embroiled in cultural fights rather than focused on the long-term planning that Driscoll and others believe it desperately needs.

Desensitization is one of Donald Trump’s most powerful tools: Through repetition, he acclimates Americans to troubling and dangerous ideas. Occasionally, though, his statements and actions are so outrageous that they can still provide a shock. The president’s hostility toward the media is familiar, but this weekend provided a clear reminder that Trump hates freedom of the press and will use any government power at his disposal to try to get rid of it.

Yesterday morning, Trump started off with a post on Truth Social demanding that the Federal Communications Commission do something about “THE FAKE POLLS USED BY OUR CROOKED MEDIA.” About 15 minutes later, he followed this with a long rant about Kristen Welker of Meet the Press, complaining that she had noted that Trump’s record in recent endorsements had been “a mixed bag.” Along with some casual misogyny, Trump wrote, “How can anyone be allowed to say this, working for freely given Public Airwaves? Results are attached. Because of this purposeful inaccuracy, she will be reported to the FCC for rebuke or punishment.”

The reasons for Trump’s anger are plain: Polls consistently show that he is deeply unpopular, matching or exceeding his own past lows in many opinion surveys. Republicans look likely to lose the House and perhaps also the Senate in this fall’s elections, and more Democratic power in Washington would make Trump’s life more difficult. As for the endorsements, the record is clear. My colleague Will Gottsegen noted last week that most Trump-backed candidates still win, but he’s had some notable misses as well.

Not only is the reporting that infuriates Trump accurate, but siccing the FCC on it makes no legal sense. The agency regulates radio and TV outlets, but much polling is conducted by print outlets and other organizations. The FCC doesn’t have any authority to punish NBC News for reporting on things the president doesn’t like, whether accurate or not. The FCC’s site states (for now) that intervening in such cases “would be inconsistent with the First Amendment to replace the journalistic judgment of licensees with our own.” If the FCC or any other federal agency can do that, freedom of the press no longer exists.

But Trump doesn’t care about this bedrock civil liberty. He’s tried to wield the FCC as a political tool, and he’s been helped by its chair, Brendan Carr, a Project 2025 co-author who has advocated for the use of regulatory agencies as partisan hatchet men. Just because the FCC doesn’t have the power doesn’t mean that Carr won’t try. Before Trump was president, MAGA figures complained about what they saw as “jawboning,” when government officials found informal ways to pressure companies; now they are enthusiastic about the practice. Carr has had some success bullying major outlets, though companies have recently been finding strength to resist.

Trump has also attempted to silence the press via lawsuits. He has sued ABC, CBS, The New York Times, the Des Moines Register, the publisher of The Wall Street Journal, the pollster J. Ann Selzer, the Pulitzer Prize Board, and the journalist Bob Woodward. Some defendants have settled; other suits have failed, and still more remain in process. Last week, Trump updated an absurd defamation case against the Times. Part of the goal is intimidation. Huge papers with strong legal departments such as the Times and Journal may not blanch before such lawsuits, but smaller outlets might think twice about what they publish. (Trump’s FBI director, Kash Patel, also recently filed a lawsuit against The Atlantic, demanding $250 million, in response to reporting he did not like. The Atlantic has filed a motion to dismiss, along with a request to begin seeking factual discovery from the FBI and the Department of Justice. “If Director Patel did not want to face this process, he should not have filed this suit,” The Atlantic’s editor in chief, Jeffrey Goldberg, has said.)

The attitude from the president permeates the rest of his administration. The finance ministers of the G20 are meeting today in Asheville, North Carolina, but the Treasury Department barred specific reporters from the Times, Journal, and Bloomberg News. The reasons these reporters were singled out is not clear. The Treasury Department noted that a wide range of outlets had been credentialed for the event but declined to discuss the specific cases on the record. The United States won’t have much credibility to criticize crackdowns on the press in, say, China or Russia (both attendees), but then again, this administration probably wouldn’t bother. As the National Press Club said in a statement, “When government officials decide which journalists may enter the room, they begin to control which questions get asked, whose scrutiny they face and what the public gets to see and hear.”

The situation is even bleaker at the Department of Defense. The Washington Post yesterday reported that top military leaders have warned Secretary Pete Hegseth that extending the war in Iran “is unsustainable and risks weakening their ability to confront threats elsewhere, including the U.S. homeland.” The reaction from the Pentagon, which has consistently tried to limit and hide information about the floundering war, was furious. One can understand why the department is, well, defensive: Failed and failing military operations have brought down administrations before.

The Defense Department spokesperson Sean Parnell claimed that the report was “inaccurate” and added that “publishing highly classified assessments from the Secretary’s orders book is a crime.” That allegation is, itself, broadly misleading and arguably just false. But MAGA supporters outside the administration eagerly agreed. The Trump ally and Post columnist Marc Thiessen, who keeps insisting that the war is going swimmingly, criticized the leak that his own paper received. (Pro tip: If you’re not willing to stand for transparency, journalism is the wrong line of work.)

These are the latest steps in a clampdown at the Defense Department. The Pentagon is trying to turn Stars and Stripes, a venerable military newspaper, into a propaganda outlet. (Firings there are paused during litigation.) Whether it makes sense for a government agency to house an independent publication is open for debate, but what’s clear is that Trump officials are targeting Stars and Stripes because of unflattering reporting—including on conditions aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln—that is truthful and relevant to the public and troops.

Under Hegseth, the department has drastically reduced press access at the Pentagon and, as the Post also reported yesterday, begun hiring right-wing influencers. Despite these efforts, the Iran war is deeply unpopular, and as the Post stories demonstrate, traditional outlets continue to report with depth, rigor, and accuracy on the administration’s actions. This should be a warning for the enemies of free speech who run the federal government: Targeting the press isn’t just un-American; it also doesn’t work.

Photographs by Alexandra Howland

Updated at 7:35 p.m. ET on August 31, 2026

On one of the final days of the trial, dozens of women streamed into the back of a Plymouth, Massachusetts, courtroom, filling two long rows of wooden benches. They stood and watched the judge and jury take their seats. Then they strained for a view as a nurse wheeled in the defendant, Lindsay Clancy, who has admitted to strangling her three young children to death in her suburban Boston home in January 2023, before swallowing prescription pills, slashing her wrists and neck, and throwing herself out of a bedroom window headfirst.

Seeing her there in a wheelchair, one woman crossed herself and whispered, “Please, God.” As an officer gaveled the court into session, others pulled out notebooks, folders, and scraps of paper. “WHY,” Marta Quijano wrote as a prosecutor called a psychiatrist to the stand. “Motivation,” she added, starring the word as the doctor began explaining his opinion that Clancy was not in the throes of psychosis on the night of the killings, as her defense has argued, but deeply suicidal and still capable of knowing right from wrong. In the back row, a woman who had flown in from Cleveland shook her head. She had a true-crime novel in her lap and turned to a blank page at the end. “Stupid experts,” she wrote.

Americans have long been obsessed with murder trials, especially those involving the primal nightmare of mothers who kill their children. The public loves to watch mothers on trial and partake in their judgment. There was Susan Smith, who had drowned her three children in a South Carolina lake and was found guilty of murder in 1995, despite having a history of trauma and sexual abuse. And there was Andrea Yates, who had drowned her five children in a bathtub and was convicted of capital murder in 2002 and then retried in 2006, when she was found not guilty by reason of insanity after witnesses testified that Yates had experienced severe postpartum psychosis and schizophrenia.

The Clancy trial is now part of this sadistic American tradition, but it has also become something else for the women who have been showing up in person. It was not enough for them to watch the proceedings on television, or listen to some lawyer on YouTube, or even stand outside the courthouse wearing pink and waving signs in support of Clancy, as hundreds of women did one day earlier this month. They did not want to be outside rendering judgment; they wanted to be inside, on the third floor, sitting on the wooden benches, as close as possible, in case they could notice some detail, some gesture, some pause or expression or word that might  reveal a truth they could recognize from their own experience. Each was there for their own reasons.

A jury of nine women and three men will decide in the coming days whether Clancy, a 36-year-old former labor-and-delivery nurse, is criminally responsible for killing her children or not guilty by reason of insanity. The two prosecutors have argued that the killings were the premeditated decision of an entitled and controlling woman who had been frustrated that she could not master her worsening depression and sought a way out of a life that she no longer wanted. Clancy’s defense attorney, Kevin Reddington, has cast her as a loving mother failed by a fragmented mental-health system even as she repeatedly sought help for intensifying depression, anxiety, and insomnia after giving birth to her third child. 

[Read: Why Lindsay Clancy’s insanity defense might fail]

Clancy saw her psychiatrist 14 times, tried 13 different medications, called a suicide hotline, went to an emergency room, tried to enroll in an inpatient program, and confided to her husband that she’d had thoughts of harming her children. Defense experts testified that doctors had missed an underlying bipolar disorder and prescribed pills that made Clancy’s symptoms worse, starting a downward spiral that culminated in a rare condition called postpartum psychosis that can cause hallucinations. Clancy has said that she heard a male voice telling her to kill her children, Cora, Dawson, and Callan, on January 24, 2023.

After being resuscitated following her suicide attempt, which left her paralyzed from the waist down, Clancy was handcuffed, placed under armed guard, and arraigned from her hospital bed. A judge allowed the moment to be livestreamed from the courtroom; a video screen showed her unblinking eyes above a white surgical mask. The trial itself has also been livestreamed, fueling conspiracy theories that have tended to blame the killings on Clancy’s husband or an intruder, attempting to find clarity where there is little, as many women at the trial already understood. At first, only a few showed up. By last Monday, they were waiting in an hour-long line to get into the courthouse, and another that stretched down a bright hallway to the courtroom doors.

A crowd of mostly women waits in line to get inside the courthouse.

“I can’t imagine what’s going through her head,” one woman was saying to her friend.

“It’s not just cold-blooded murder,” the friend said.

“I had suicidal thoughts, clear as a bell,” a retired teacher named Dottie Kiley told another woman in line, explaining her experience with antidepressants. “So bad I called the police and told them, and they had a cop come down and sit with me. It was a small town, and they had time to do that.”

The woman behind her nodded. “She could be any one of us,” she said as the courtroom doors opened.

Inside, the women listened to Avram Mack, a psychiatrist and prosecution witness, explain his clinical opinion that Clancy, having made up her mind to kill herself, then made the decision to kill her children to prevent their suffering a life without her—a theory called “altruistic filicide.” Quijano leaned over to the woman sitting next to her.

“What is the validity of this three years later?” she whispered, shaking her head.

After the morning break, she stood in line again for the afternoon session.

“With my first daughter, because it was so difficult breastfeeding, I had insomnia and anxiety—I was worried the baby is hungry,” Quijano told a woman she had just met. “I remember one time—she was a cranky baby—and I remember not so much shaking her but going like this,” she said, demonstrating how she squeezed her daughter’s shoulders and screamed. “They are called intrusive thoughts because your brain says something, and you don’t recognize it.”

Ashlyn Rizzo, who’d been attending the trial for two weeks straight, nodded.

“I had been planning to run away,” Rizzo said, referring to a moment after her first child was born. “I liquidated all my 401(k) and squirreled the money away. I was going to go without the baby. I figured everyone was better off without me. I had delusions. I couldn’t tell wake from sleep. I didn’t confide to anyone. I wrote a note.”

“My cousin had a plan,” Quijano said, not needing to finish.

“I was just going to start driving,” Rizzo continued. “Stay in hotels.”

As it happened, Rizzo said, she had called her psychiatrist, who that very day got her into the same specialized program in Rhode Island that had rejected Clancy one month before she killed her children. “Saved my life,” Rizzo said.

On Tuesday, the line was longer.

“I wonder, what does justice look like in this case?” a retired teacher named Lisa was saying. She had been keeping the livestream of the trial on in the background of her daily life all month, until she decided to come to see it for herself. She had driven up from Virginia.

“My daughter had a moment,” Lisa, who didn’t give her last name, told the woman standing behind her, referring to postpartum depression. “I’ve been there, trying to get psychiatric appointments.”

“I had a miscarriage in the second trimester and drifted into depression,” Sheila Sullivan replied, explaining that she’d wound up committed to a psychiatric facility in the 1970s. “They gave me shock treatment. Back then, they didn’t know.”

“I’m sorry,” Lisa said, hugging her.

“Thank you,” Sullivan said.

“They don’t do any research on how hormones affect mental health,” Lisa said.

“She didn’t have any symptoms until they put her on the drugs,” Sullivan said.

“It’s all interconnected,” Lisa said as the courtroom doors opened and the women filled the back benches.

“Can you tell us what peritraumatic disassociation is?” a prosecutor asked an FBI forensic psychiatrist, Gregory Saathoff.

“It’s when you feel things are unreal, not you—like you’re watching yourself,” Saathoff began.

A woman wrote down, “Witness staring at jury,” and whispered, “I think that’s weird.”

Sullivan took out her notebook.

“Moral judgement??” she wrote.

She listened as Reddington, the defense attorney, questioned Saathoff, going through his 75-page résumé and pointing out that he had treated only two women with postpartum depression, and that that had been decades ago. Reddington went over the times that Clancy had described feeling “like a zombie” and had been in a “disassociated state,” and asked why health-care providers had not asked more questions when Clancy indicated that she was suicidal.

“She was trying hard to get help,” the doctor acknowledged.

“I can relate to all of this,” Jodi Fournier said as she stood in line later that afternoon. “Not at the extreme, but I can understand.”

For several days, she had been driving to court with two friends from a town 30 minutes away.

“The other thing is, when you work in health care, you learn not to trust the doctors,” her friend Susan Hatfield said. “She was questioning her doctors. She didn’t trust them, and neither did I.”

“See that woman over there?” Fournier said, noticing a blond woman standing by herself and praying at a window outside the courtroom.

“She’s does it every day,” Hatfield said.

Sullivan joined the line behind them.

“I had a miscarriage, then depression, then thoughts of suicide,” she began, retelling her story, and soon they went inside.

Every day, the court officer gaveled in the session, a call to order that ended with the phrase “You will be heard.” Every day, a nurse wheeled in Lindsay Clancy.

“That’s her,” Fournier whispered.

On Tuesday afternoon, Reddington continued questioning Saathoff. “Do you remember Linsday talking about how she can’t feel love? She can’t feel fear?” Then he quoted from Clancy’s journal: “It takes all the effort in the world just to breathe,” she had written, as well as “I want help” and “I want to be well.”

In the back benches, the women leaned forward so they could hear. They pulled out notebooks.

“WTF,” one woman wrote as Reddington went over Clancy’s medication list.

“I am afraid,” another woman wrote down on Wednesday, quoting something Clancy had said.

Rebecca Olszewski, a pharmacist and mother of three, had flown in from Cleveland to attend the trial. She told me that she was recovering from anorexia, bulimia, and generalized anxiety disorder, all of which she said she had learned to mask, as Clancy had apparently done at times. Prosecutors submitted into evidence photos of her smiling with her kids. “There would be outings, and my mom would be like, ‘You seem in good spirits,’” Olszewski said. “And I’d say, ‘Mom, I’m just here going through motions.’”

She wrote down a list of questions: Why hadn’t the people who’d answered the suicide hotline asked Clancy more questions about her suicidal thoughts—not simply whether she had a plan at that moment, but whether she’d ever had a plan? Why hadn’t doctors placed her in an inpatient program? Why hadn’t they paid closer attention?

The prosecution delivers closing arguments.

On Thursday, the day of closing arguments, the line was longer than it had been all week. Not everyone could get into the main courtroom, so another one was made available, and women filled more wooden benches, including Joni Villemez-Comeaux, a mental-health counselor there from Texas.

“This is shining a very poor light on our field, and it needs to,” she was saying, and now several women began asking her about how she would have handled Clancy—which sorts of details mattered, and which didn’t. “Every fucking thing matters,” Villemez-Comeaux said, and soon the courtroom was quiet.

In closing, Reddington once again went over all the ways that Clancy had sought help. The medications. The psychiatrist visits. The journal in which she’d chronicled her worsening symptoms until her handwriting had turned into what he described as “chicken scratch.”

“My God, what does she have to do?” Reddington said, and when he finished, women clapped and cheered, and then listened as the prosecutor for the Commonwealth, Jennifer Sprague, delivered her closing argument.

“This case is not about the mental-health system,” she began.

On the wooden benches, a woman quietly said to herself, “Yes, it is.”

After that, Sprague continued for nearly an hour. She talked about how depressed and suicidal Clancy had been but emphasized how much support she’d had. Her parents, a nanny, health insurance. “She had more access to care and treatment and support than most people do.” She once again painted Clancy as an entitled woman who’d known that what she was doing was wrong, who’d decided to strangle her children anyway, leaving them “discarded on the basement floor like broken toys because she was done playing the game.”

By the time she spoke those words, though, many of the women who’d stood in line and listened to days of testimony had already left the courtroom. They had heard enough.
