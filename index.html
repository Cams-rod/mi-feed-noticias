Jared Kushner’s Strategic Pixie Dust

For reasons known only to himself, the normally enigmatic Jared Kushner—who, together with Steve Witkoff, another wealthy real-estate guy, does most of the Trump administration’s heavy-duty negotiations—has opened up. In a post on X on November 22, and a lengthy interview with Mathias Döpfner of Axel Springer, he has laid out his thoughts about the talks with Russia and Ukraine. Kushner’s comments are revealing in unintended and often uncomplimentary ways.

To begin with, there is his truckling to Vladimir Putin and his henchmen. Putin is a “grand master of politics,” Kushner said. This will be news to the bereaved families of 700,000 Russian dead, the even larger numbers of those permanently maimed, the population suffering shortages of gas and groceries, and the million-odd émigrés who fled their country. Kushner also described the lead Russian negotiator, Kirill Dmitriev, a man of reptilian smoothness, as “an incredible friend,” and the Russian national security adviser Yuri Ushakov as “fantastic.”

The sycophancy toward Moscow does not end there. Russia, he explained, has done the West a great favor by agreeing “not to use nuclear weapons, which obviously is a big thing.” The war did not result from Russia’s brutal decision to destroy the Ukrainian state and dismember it, but rather “bad decisions and miscalculations by many leaders over the years.” Russia needs to be convinced that “NATO is not seeking confrontation,” and Putin, who is “a historic figure,” has “a very deep understanding of the issues” and “wants peace.”

Meanwhile, Kushner’s attitude to America’s allies is distinctly cooler. He claimed that the Ukrainians blew up an earlier phase of the negotiations by attacking one of Putin’s homes—which the CIA assesses they did not. The White House, he insisted, has not put any pressure on Ukraine by withholding intelligence or weapons (or, presumably, access to Starlink and Starshield): “That’s a lot of misinformation.” President Trump’s call for elections in Ukraine was both a defense of democracy and a pushback at Volodymyr Zelensky’s criticism of the White House—Trump is “a very strong counterpuncher,” after all.

America’s NATO allies do not come off well: “There’s a lot of fear in Europe that’s unfounded,” he said, ignoring assassinations real and attempted, and potentially lethal sabotage conducted by the Russian secret services. The Europeans were excluded from peace talks in Abu Dhabi “because President Putin sees a lot of provocative statements out of Europe and a lot of provocative actions out of Europe. And so his view is he does not see that they are ready for peace.” Who, Kushner asked, could blame him?

One key to Kushner’s view is a throwaway line: “This isn’t about fairness or who’s right or wrong.” To the contrary, this is about a war in which the distinction between aggressor and victim, predator and prey could not be clearer. For that alone the ghosts of Bucha and a thousand other massacres and cruelties perpetrated by Russia should haunt his sleep, but one doubts they will.

That Kushner can say and believe these things is bad enough. No less stunning is his hubris, and that of the other members of the administration. The X post, the interview, and other statements indicate that the administration wishes for nothing less than a redesign of “European security architecture,” a grand settlement of the kind that Lord Castlereagh, Charles Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord, and Klemens von Metternich concocted after the Napoleonic Wars, and that a no less acute group of statesmen—Dean Acheson, Clement Attlee, and Konrad Adenauer—devised after World War II.

One of the leading aspirants for the role of master geopolitical architect is Elbridge Colby, the undersecretary of defense who insists that the time has come for NATO 3.0. At a conference of the Ukrainian Defence Contact Group in Brussels on November 17, he denounced Ukrainian strikes on Russian infrastructure—the kind of thing the United States recently did to Iran—as being militarily valueless. He then walked out of the meeting before the Ukrainian defense minister could give a classified briefing on the war, including, presumably, Kyiv’s deep-strike campaign.

Colby, who sat through the meeting with the Trumpian pout masquerading as scowl that characterizes so many administration officials, convinced some German observers that he too sympathized far more with Russia than with Ukraine. The German defense minister, Boris Pistorius, a man of few but choice words, merely observed that such criticisms of Ukraine were “quite a stupid thing.”

Part of the inanity of these proceedings is that Kushner, Colby, and other officials believe that they can reshape Europe’s security arrangements while (1) sympathizing with its enemies, (2) denouncing America’s allies, and (3) serving an administration that repeatedly insists on its intention to withdraw as much as possible from European commitments, when it is not actively discussing seizing some of Europe’s territory.

To moral blindness, then, add political idiocy. But there is something even deeper at work, a profound unseriousness that is rooted not merely in ignorance of history and indifference to the actual circumstances of the greatest act of international criminality in this century, but faith in magical diplomacy. It is a vision of peace through strategic witchcraft.

The tell here is in Kushner’s X post: He floats the idea of “a credible Article 5–like guarantee, backed by deterrence and deconfliction arrangements,” saying that President Trump would agree to such an offer for Ukraine only if it were “highly unlikely that the guarantee would ever be invoked.” It sounds at first blush like a grift: Make promises only under the condition that you know you will never have to fulfill them. It is worse, however.

Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty guarantees nothing, an inconvenient truth long obfuscated by American and European rhetoric. It states that “an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all,” but it promises reactions by any country only “as it deems necessary.” If, say, the United States does not deem it necessary to defend Ukraine from another Russian invasion, it would not be obliged to.

For much of the Cold War, “deterrence” was the strategic pixie dust that was supposed to keep Europe safe. Early on, at least, it was bolstered by substantial conventional forces on the ground. At one point, for example, the British Army on the Rhine was larger than the entire British Army today, while the Bundeswehr had half a million men and women under arms, 12 divisions, and a thousand aircraft—and behind those forces, American nuclear superiority and more than a quarter million U.S. troops on the ground.

Guarantees without the ability to deliver on them, though, are as meaningless as those Great Britain gave to Poland in the summer of 1939. They deter nothing, because deterrence is the by-product of the ability and will to fight, not a thing unto itself.

The irascible Admiral Jacky Fisher, the dyspeptic and histrionic first sea lord in turn-of-the-century Britain, was closer to the truth when he said (at a peace conference in 1899, no less):

What you call my truculence is all for peace. If you rub it in, both at home and abroad, that you are ready for instant war with every unit of your strength in the first line, and intend to be first in, and hit your enemy in the belly, and kick him when he is down … then people will keep clear of you.

Fisher spoke partly for effect and went further than any sane or even ethical leader might, but he had a point. At least he did not live, as Jared Kushner and Elbridge Colby apparently do, in a world of amoral, vapid, and self-serving geopolitical delusion.

Spies, at least for now, are back in the public eye: Robert Pattinson is the new James Bond—I’ve said all I’ll say on that—and the Nvidia CEO, Jensen Huang, confessed to canoodling with an alleged Chinese-intelligence asset, so we’re thinking about espionage again. But after the Cold War, with a few notable exceptions, including the eternal 007 series, the entertainment industry lost interest in spies for the most part. On television, they have reemerged only in occasional treats such as the wonderful Slow Horses, a series about a bunch of dedicated but often incompetent MI5 operatives.

And that’s too bad, because the days when spies were everywhere in American culture were a blast.

The heyday of pop-spy mania ran from the early 1960s to about the mid-1970s. Some of these offerings are well worth revisiting, not only as entertainment but also as fascinating cultural artifacts of an era when America was leading the free world in the fight against the Kremlin. (Given the current state of U.S. foreign policy, you can imagine why I am nostalgic for such times.)

During that spy craze, American culture mostly imported products from the spy-thriller mother country, the United Kingdom. (As early as 1954, CBS ran the first television adaptation of an Ian Fleming novel, a version of Casino Royale that features an American James Bond—named Jimmy Bond, I kid you not—played by Barry Nelson.) But the cultural obsession with spies really took off in the mid-1960s, by which point Sean Connery had established 007 as an international film hero.

Why were we so infatuated with espionage during the swingin’ ’60s? The Cold War obviously had a lot to do with it. Americans for the first time faced an enemy superpower, but in the atomic age, when the price of war was too high, they had to fight their opponents in the shadows. This era of spies was also a product of the liberated mores of the times. Movies, especially, were allowed to be racier, and spy flicks soon featured handsome cads cheerfully engaging in plenty of sex and violence. Bond and his colleagues represented a new image of virility and savoir faire, freed from the uptight 1950s. But they also defiantly rejected the more progressive changes of the ’60s. Feminism? Criminal rights? Forget it. These new spies had a license to kill, to use women like toys, to drive like maniacs, to gamble, to drink, and to do anything else a manly man thought was necessary to save the world.

British spy shows, more grounded in the reality of European espionage, were generally grittier and less flashy than the Hollywood-ized Bond movies. Some of them, however, were sexed up when exported to more gung-ho U.S. viewers. From 1960 to 1961, for example, Patrick McGoohan played John Drake on Danger Man, an agent who works for some nebulous NATO intelligence agency. As Bond mania grew, the series was revived in 1964, losing some of its film-noir elements in favor of more 007 influences. Drake was moved over to British intelligence, the show was retitled Secret Agent for U.S. syndication, and Johnny Rivers performed a new theme song that became a ’60s classic.

After Secret Agent ended its run in late 1967, McGoohan followed it up in 1968 with The Prisoner, a kind of spy-fi (spy-and-science-fiction mash-up) series about an agent who tries to retire and is sent to a secret halfway house for wayward spooks. The series, which is still a cult favorite, calls his character only “Number Six”—but we all knew it was Drake.

Likewise, when The Avengers premiered in the U.K. in 1961, it was mostly a pedestrian private-eye show. But soon, it turned into a spy-fi romp, with John Steed (Patrick Macnee) and his female colleague Mrs. Gale (Honor Blackman) as the agents who served Queen and country. In a case of spy-fiction cross-pollination, Blackman left to play Pussy Galore in Goldfinger, the 007 adventure about a plot to blow up America’s gold reserves in Kentucky. After Diana Rigg arrived as Steed’s new partner, Emma Peel, the show winked at the audience: In a 1965 episode, she hands Steed his mail, including a Christmas card from Gale. “How nice of her to remember me,” Steed says. “What can she be doing in Fort Knox?” The next year, the series was snapped up by America’s ABC, and Rigg, in her leather catsuits, and Macnee, in his bowler hat and Pierre Cardin suits, became spy-fiction icons.

Spies quickly flooded U.S. television screens after what must have been an endless stream of high-concept pitches: “James Bond, but in the old American West as a Secret Service agent, against supervillains with names such as Dr. Loveless and Count Manzeppi.” (The Wild Wild West.) “James Bond, but as an incompetent boob in a comedy.” (Get Smart!) “James Bond, but he’s a Black American tennis coach.” (I Spy. The series’ co-star, Bill Cosby, now lives in shame, but back in 1965, it was groundbreaking to get Americans to accept a Black man as a hero instead of a sidekick.)

The Man From U.N.C.L.E., a show that first hit TV screens in 1964, was spy-fi from the start. Not only was it derived from the Bond successes, but Fleming reportedly helped develop an early pitch for the show; U.N.C.L.E. Agent Napoleon Solo was even named for a Bond character. Solo and his Soviet partner, Ilya Kuriakin, work for U.N.C.L.E., a kind of international superagency that battles the psychos from T.H.R.U.S.H., an obvious stand-in for Fleming’s baddies, SPECTRE. (U.N.C.L.E. producers came up with these names because they sounded hip; they filled in what the acronyms meant later.)

The Americans had their own homegrown spies, too. In 1966, Mission: Impossible premiered on CBS featuring a team of operatives so secret that the U.S. government hides their instructions on a tape recorder in a telephone booth or a vending machine and other wacky places, after which the tape self-destructs into a hissing, smoky mess. (The Tom Cruise Mission: Impossible reboots, much like the Jason Bourne movies, are fine as generic action flicks, but for some Cold War spy fun, treat yourself to the original series.)

Even cartoons got in on the act. Everyone remembers Bullwinkle’s enemies, Boris and Natasha, and you might remember Secret Squirrel, but do you remember Cool McCool, a 1966 Saturday-morning secret agent drummed up by Bob Kane, a co-creator of Batman? Probably not, and that’s fine, because Cool McCool is terrible. (But not as terrible as “Lancelot Link, Secret Chimp” a 1970 spy spoof for kids that had a cast of, yes, live chimpanzees.)

Eventually, the spy craze got out of hand, as all fads do. James Coburn and Dean Martin both signed up for spy spoofs, and saw varying results: Coburn’s two outings as Derek Flint, an agent of Z.O.W.I.E. (don’t ask), are fine, but Martin’s Matt Helm movies are unwatchable—at least they provided some raw material for Mike Myers when he created Austin Powers.

After the 1960s, the cynical ’70s were upon us, and the fascination with spies dissipated. Events such as Watergate made government spookery seem a lot less admirable; in 1975, a congressional committee outed a lot of the CIA’s uglier tricks to the American public. Bond movies thrived, but 007 had much of the spy-genre field to himself at the theaters and on television.

It was a great ride, and you can catch many of these programs on stations that run vintage TV shows, random YouTube channels, or a few streaming platforms. That’s your assignment over this holiday weekend, Agent Reader, and remember: This newsletter will self-destruct in five seconds.

What We Got Wrong About Comas

People in a coma, according to medical textbooks, cannot possibly be aware. They’re utterly unresponsive. They cannot be roused by sound or smell or touch—or anything at all. Their eyes are closed. When Brian Edlow, a neurologist at Massachusetts General Hospital, sees these comatose patients in the ICU, their brain injuries are in many cases so severe that recovery or even survival is not certain.

And yet, after his team examined the brain activity of a comatose 80-year-old woman recently, she appeared to be responding to their instructions. Asked to imagine opening and closing her right hand, the woman’s brain buzzed with a pattern similar to that of a fully conscious person. The doctors confirmed the finding with two different methods: EEG, which detects brain waves via electrodes on the surface of the head, and fMRI, which tracks blood flow inside the brain. She is the first comatose patient to show signs of consciousness on both tests, which Edlow and his colleagues detail in a report published last month.

The case of this 80-year-old woman adds to the nascent evidence that at least some patients in a coma are actually conscious. Using the EEG method, doctors at Columbia University in 2019  found eight comatose people—out of 56—whose brain activity suggested that they could respond to verbal instructions. “I remember reading that and just thinking, How is that possible?” Edlow told me. That study inspired his own efforts to find consciousness in comatose patients. Varina Boerwinkle, a pediatric neurologist now at the University of North Carolina, has used fMRI to look for consciousness in comatose children—including one memorable 6-year-old boy she originally did not think would survive. (He did, after the fMRI gave his parents hope to try a subsequent surgery.) Every day, Boerwinkle told me, she teaches medical residents who are “understandably incredulous” that comatose patients might hear and understand what is being said in their room.

All of these findings build on the earlier discovery of consciousness in a different group of patients—those who are in vegetative or minimally conscious states, which are gray zones between coma and normal consciousness. Unlike people in a coma, these patients have eyes that open and flick around the room. They look “awake” but are still physically unresponsive. A significant fraction of such patients, perhaps a quarter, exhibit consciousness on EEG or fMRI tests, according to a landmark paper published in 2024. I profiled the family of one patient, Ian Berg, last year. He was diagnosed as vegetative after a car accident 40 years ago, but his mother never stopped believing he was still conscious inside. Now researchers agree.

But the detection of consciousness in coma is both more surprising—comatose patients do not even look awake—and in some ways more pressing. Coma is by definition a short-term phenomenon, which ends in one of three outcomes. Patients can regain consciousness, recovering fully or with some level of disability, from minor to significant. They can enter a long-term vegetative or minimally conscious state. Or, if their injury is too severe, they can die. During the days or weeks a patient is in a coma, their family faces the agonizing choice of continuing aggressive medical care for an uncertain outcome or discontinuing care, which will have a certain outcome: death. The second choice is common. In a study of several trauma centers in Canada, 70 percent of brain-injury deaths resulted from withdrawal of care. Some of the patients, the recent research suggests, could have been conscious when they died.

How should the discovery of consciousness affect life-and-death decisions for ICU patients? Those who exhibit consciousness on EEG go on to have better recoveries, according to the Columbia study. Nearly half of unresponsive ICU patients with consciousness recovered enough to follow commands by the time of discharge; only a quarter of those without consciousness did. Their divergent trajectories held 12 months later too. (That analysis pooled all unresponsive ICU patients, whether comatose, vegetative, or minimally conscious.)

Covert consciousness is therefore a good sign for recovery, but Jan Claassen, the Columbia neurologist who led that study, warns of overinterpreting the results. A negative test does not mean that a patient cannot recover, he told me. Many in the study did. In the seven years since the report was published, Claassen has continued to investigate better ways of predicting recovery from coma or testing for consciousness via EEG. Even at his ICU, though, these tests are not routine clinical practice.

In fact, very few ICUs in the United States look for covert consciousness. Edlow’s hospital had examined the 80-year-old woman as part of a pioneering Emerging Consciousness Program he launched in 2022. If she had shown up at an average hospital, neither her doctors nor her family would ever have learned she was likely still aware.

“Coma has been a dehumanizing condition,” Claude Hemphill, a neurologist at UC San Francisco and co-chair of the Curing Coma Campaign, told me. In the ICU room, doctors and families might speak over a comatose patient or act as if they aren’t there. These recent findings deepen the case for  small but telling actions Hemphill takes to acknowledge the patient, such as sitting next to their bed or looking back and forth when speaking with their family.

Even for doctors open to the possibility of consciousness in coma patients, testing for it is logistically difficult. An fMRI machine is big and expensive, and moving a severely ill patient inside one can prove challenging. Even EEG, a more portable technology, can be tricky to perform or interpret for doctors unfamiliar with testing for covert consciousness. A third technology called fNIRS, which works by shining light through the skull, is being investigated by Adrian Owen, a neuroscientist who found the very first vegetative patient with covert consciousness back in 2006. Owen, now at the University of Western Ontario in Canada, published a paper last year showing that fNIRS found covert consciousness in a quarter of the 32 ICU patients tested. At the time the study was conducted, he was not allowed to share the results with families, because the technology had not yet proved reliable. Some of those families ultimately chose to withdraw care.

Owen now knows, in retrospect, that some of those patients were aware when they died. They may have even been aware that their families were choosing for them to die. “It gives you some sort of sense of the importance of why we pursue this work,” he told me. He hopes one day to  communicate with these patients using fNIRS, by asking yes-or-no questions that the patient might answer by imagining one task or the other. He and his collaborators have done it before using fMRI, and in one case even asked a 22-year-old man, diagnosed as minimally conscious, if he would like to die. His answer was inconclusive. But could comatose patients in the ICU, given the opportunity, eventually express their wishes this way? For now, it’s still their families who have to decide for them.

At Mass General, Edlow says that families given a choice of knowing the results of the EEG and fMRI tests always say yes. The husband of the 80-year-old woman in a coma weighed the evidence that she was still conscious. In the end, he decided that, at age 80, she would not want a feeding tube and colostomy bag, even if she could make a full recovery over time, which was by no means a guarantee. Science can now detect signs of consciousness in comatose patients, but it cannot predict exactly what kind of life they will ultimately live.

Berkeley, California, a quintessential university town, may well be the birthplace of anti-growth politics. In 1916, local reformers adopted the country’s first single-family zoning, prohibiting apartments in most residential neighborhoods as a way to exclude disfavored minorities. Progressives would carry on this legacy for the next century in the name of protecting residents from unwanted development. Beginning in the 1960s, housing opponents tried to block construction on environmental grounds—and eventually argued that new residents qualified as “pollution.” Permitting rates were anemic for decades, keeping the city’s population flat even while enrollment at UC Berkeley skyrocketed. Rents and home prices rose accordingly. By the mid-2010s, Berkeley ranked as one of the most expensive college towns in America.

But around that time, it began to undergo a remarkable reversal, as students, staff, and young faculty at UC Berkeley came to realize that housing shortages hurt their interests and began working together to push for more inclusive policies. In 2017, they helped organize the group East Bay for Everyone, which showed up constantly to public hearings and helped elect a city council aligned with the “Yes in My Backyard” movement then emerging statewide. With the aid of new state laws loosening zoning requirements—some of which I advocated for as part of my work at YIMBY organizations—permitting of new housing surged. As of 2024, nominal rents barely exceeded 2018 levels, despite inflation.

Today Berkeley represents a major success for the YIMBY movement, and it’s not the only one. Across the country, college towns have become boomtowns. As longtime strongholds of progressive politics, their evolution from NIMBY holdouts to YIMBY hotbeds serves as a testament to the left’s shift on housing. If the movement’s next decade is going to deliver on the promise of its first, my fellow housing advocates nationwide will have to replicate the methods that allowed college towns to so quickly build and become more affordable.

[Jonathan Chait: The misguided Democratic opposition to permitting reform]

One obvious lesson is that students and young academics can be persuasive advocates. These groups tend to be temporarily poor and highly social and to have flexible schedules. To put this in criminological terms, they have the motive, means, and opportunity to show up in numbers at the weekday public hearings that determine what does and doesn’t get built. As a result of such activism, once-stagnant college towns across California, including San Luis Obispo and Santa Cruz, have undergone similar transformations as Berkeley: Permitting is up, and rents are starting to stabilize or sometimes even fall. At UC San Diego, campus advocates successfully pushed for new on-campus high-rises that will house thousands of students, helping alleviate the state’s extraordinary rate of student homelessness.

In many college towns across the country, off-campus housing is proliferating too: Real-estate-investment trusts, often known as REITs, have plowed billions of dollars into student housing, building roughly 35,000 beds in 2024 alone. According to an estimate from 2020, some 12 percent of all rental units are for students.

Throughout the United States, college towns have taken steps to allow for greater housing density, as Ann Arbor, Michigan, did last month. Perhaps the most extreme example of this is Austin, where the flagship campus of the University of Texas is located. After a decade of trying and failing to overhaul the city zoning code, local YIMBYs began to get their way in the late 2010s, when increasing demand for housing kicked off a construction frenzy.

A local backlash grew, threatening to upend the reforms that were turning Greater Austin into America’s fastest-growing metropolis. But YIMBYs were able to preserve their progress thanks in large part to one key group. “When students started to show up to Austin city-council meetings and endorse candidates based on their housing policies, and generally make it clear they want to be viewed as true constituents, it was a game changer,” Felicity Maxwell, a board member of a local pro-housing organization, told me.

In 2023, with strong support from local students, lawmakers in Austin were able to sustain the city’s home-building surge by ending both single-family zoning and minimum off-street-parking requirements. The city has been building so much housing that rents are down, even as migration continues to soar.

Students and junior academics cannot change decades-old urban-planning habits on their own. Senior faculty have also been instrumental in dislodging a kind of progressivism that treats all development as predatory. Well-respected professors have repeatedly called attention to the need for more housing, drawing on a growing scholarly consensus on the issue. Leaders in more and more college towns have come to see exclusionary housing as incompatible with liberal politics.

“I was troubled by the tensions between Ithaca being a progressive, welcoming community that was also staggeringly unaffordable,” Robert Cantelmo, the pro-growth mayor of Ithaca, New York, told me. Cantelmo is a product of Cornell University, where he received a master’s degree and later helped lead a research center. During his tenure, Ithaca has had more active cranes than many far larger American cities.

A similar story has played out in Cambridge, Massachusetts. In 2022, a recent MIT grad named Burhan Azeem mobilized students at his alma mater, who in turn helped him become the youngest councilor in the history of the city. Azeem became vice mayor earlier this year and has led a successful campaign to legalize Paris-scale density citywide.

[Read: A perfectly crude way to solve America’s housing shortage]

“Cambridge is one of the youngest towns in Massachusetts, and not just because of undergraduates,” Azeem told me. “A lot of people stay here after graduation, or stay on for years in graduate school, and their big question is, Will I ever be able to buy a home here? That makes for a natural pro-housing constituency.”

In some places, however, college communities have limited political influence. At Stanford University, 98 percent of undergraduates live on campus, which is just outside Palo Alto’s municipal limits, leaving them effectively iced out from politics in that city. UC Santa Barbara is located in a county of nearly 500,000 residents, which even the most organized students would struggle to sway. In other college towns, such as Gainesville, Florida, intransigent homeowners and state governments have thwarted reform.

Moreover, there are reasons to think that the college-town boom may be short-lived. University enrollment is headed for a death spiral, and dozens of colleges are closing each year. Although flagship universities and elite schools probably won’t close anytime soon, the student body at many institutions will likely shrink. But even if college towns do begin to stagnate, their runaway growth over the past decade has provided a model that YIMBYs can reproduce nationally.

Housing advocates have plenty more work to do in California, where much of my advocacy has been focused. Despite its fast-growing college towns, statewide permitting is flat and housing costs are rising. In too many cases, much-anticipated reforms have failed to produce much housing. But college towns have demonstrated that transforming a community requires only a small group of highly motivated people. In California and elsewhere, such groups have made the Sisyphean task of advocacy fun, hosting happy hours, run clubs, neighborhood meetups, and workshops. “It’s really easy to point to all the abstract changes that YIMBY reforms can make at some vague point in the future,” Katie Caceres, the organizing director for the Student Homes Coalition, told me. “But real friendships are what distinguish the most successful campus groups I’ve worked with.”

To ensure that college towns are a prologue to a bigger housing revolution, rather than a curious exception, YIMBYs will have to follow the example these communities set by showing up to hearings, supporting candidates, organizing lobby days, suing cities, and running for office. These steps can sometimes feel fruitless in the moment, but they’re the same ones that NIMBYs took in the 1960s and ’70s to keep Berkeley so exclusive and small. These strategies worked then to suppress growth, they worked over the past decade to restart it, and they will work now to extend it across the country.

A cataclysm that destroys all human life is bad for many reasons, among them the resulting uselessness of a gift guide. Setting aside an extinction scenario, the more interesting—and more likely—outcome of an AI apocalypse is one in which ordinary life continues, but changed. This is closer to the biblical sense of the term apocalypse, which means a pulling back of the curtain on a divine truth. And of all the ways to prepare for an apocalypse brought about by artificial intelligence, surely the most American would be to go shopping.

Before we start, here are the rules of this AI-apocalypse shopping spree. First, the calamitous scenarios (and the giftable baubles!) must be specifically connected to artificial intelligence. A backup generator and a supply of nonperishable foods would be useful in all sorts of situations: natural disasters, conventional warfare, climatological stress, terrorism. But owning them makes you a prepper of a more generic stripe. You can find lots of gift guides of those sorts in other types of publications. Godspeed.

Second, the gifts need not attempt to prevent an apocalyptic outcome from arising—as if that would even be possible—nor solve a calamity once it does. The wares we seek will not avert AI ruination, but they should help curtail its impacts in a meaningful way. Perhaps more important, pondering the gift ought to help elucidate the threat itself—and reframe our understanding of what counts as an AI calamity in the first place.

[Read: Prepare for the AI apocalypse like you’d prepare for a snowstorm]

And finally, a good AI-apocalypse gift ought to be handy now, in advance of civilization-scale doomsday. The forces that might lead to bad outcomes live in the present as much as the future.

Gifts for the Loss of Shared Reality

The internet has undermined truth for decades now. Blogs and social-media platforms offered an alternative to the gatekeeping of traditional media. They democratized expression but also fragmented communities. Eventually, everyone’s ability to say anything all the time helped erode the institutional authority of science and the government. Accuracy became less important than engagement. These incentives industrialized misinformation, making fabrication profitable. Personalized feeds mated to algorithmic recommendations accelerated the culture of conspiracy theories.

AI threatens to eradicate the fragments of truth that remain. Misinformation used to spread by accident, simply because billions of people could say anything online. Then disinformation duped people on purpose. But AI has installed automated and personalized synthetic text, images, video, and audio absolutely everywhere. It’s overwhelming. Humankind risks suffocating in so much seemingly real but actually fake information that the distinction between the two might become erased.

Reality can’t be brought back entirely, but some tools can help. The Leica M-11P is a digital camera that includes hardware-authenticated digital signatures to verify the images it creates, including a record of their provenance and changes that have been made to them. The device is somewhat bulky and extremely expensive. A related alternative: any film camera. A physical negative offers material evidence of what the camera saw.

Reality Defender is a software service that uses a variety of methods to detect AI manipulation in images, audio, and video. The service is intended for corporate and industrial use at a cost of about $400 a year for 1,000 scans a month, but a free plan offers 50 image analyses each month.

A print-newspaper subscription is a fixed, physical version of the daily or weekly news that allows you to hold the same information as your friends and neighbors. It also offers a record of what was reported at a given time, unperturbed by questionable summaries, screenshots, or social-media posts.

Gifts for the Collapse of Expertise

Artificial intelligence offers an effective solution to almost any intellectual or practical problem. It can compose an email, write a job application, tell you what to eat for dinner, build a stock portfolio, offer instructions for servicing a septic tank. Or, better put, AI can appear to offer such solutions, cheaply and instantaneously. As more people and organizations take AI outputs as presumed expertise, the people who possess actual expertise in any subject could become displaced. People may stop learning how to do things for themselves—or they may come to trust the confidence of a machine, delivered effortlessly, more than they trust other people.

Unlike reality, expertise really is something you can purchase, by hiring people with knowledge, experience, and credentials to carry out tasks or offer advice. This might be a graphic designer, a structural engineer, a turf-lawn-management expert, an accountant, or a tutor in a specialized subject. Your situation, and that of the individual who might receive your gift, will be particular. The trick, of course, is finding an actual expert amid the din of faux competence. If you can, recommend people you know or have worked with personally, but if you can’t, Bark offers a directory of professionals in many domains.

Wikipedia isn’t a perfect host of reliable expertise, but it’s human-made and better than many alternatives. Kiwix offers an offline version of the complete English Wikipedia—along with emergency planning, repair guides, medical references, and other resources. It’s $350 as a hardware device or $35 for the data alone. The offline version is meant to defend against infrastructural collapse, but your gift recipient could use it just to access knowledge in a deliberate, internet-disconnected way.

Your local library also offers an easy way to find expertise offline. Many local libraries also give you the ability to borrow ebooks, audiobooks, and magazines via the Libby app. Libraries are generally free for residents of the municipality they serve, but you need a library card to use them—and getting one typically requires an in-person visit. Overcoming that hurdle might be harder than people think. The gift, then: Take someone to lunch and then the library to get them signed up.

Gifts for the Decline of Relationships

Lunch or dinner is great for people you already know. What about people you don’t? In 2000, the political scientist Robert Putnam published Bowling Alone, the original screed against American solitude. The title refers to bowling leagues, which were once popular sources of community but fell out of favor. Putnam’s book has been enormously influential, but many readers treat bowling leagues as a metaphor rather than a concrete activity people can and do participate in. You can still go bowling! The United States Bowling Congress can help you find local leagues, memberships in which can cost as little as $10 a week. You don’t need a bowling ball to start, but affordable sets of ball, bag, and shoes make for something you can wrap.

It is curious that human relationships have become so challenging to steward, given that technology allows us to connect with one another constantly and for free. Perhaps writing letters, making long-distance phone calls, and paying people a visit in person offered greater connectivity because they demanded a break from ordinary life. Some defamiliarization might help human connections thrive. You could, I suppose, give someone a stationery set and invite them to write letters again. But that feels retrograde. Instead, consider one of the oddball examples of telepresence that have been commercialized. The Friendship Lamp, for example, comes in a set of two glowing objects, one for you and one for someone else. When one of you touches the device, the other one lights up too. Bond Touch offers a similar idea in bracelet and necklace form.

Gifts for Cultural Stagnation

Being an artist of any kind has always been difficult, but generative AI could erode the market for human cultural production so much that such work becomes commercially unviable. When partnered with a consolidated industry for distribution, AI could even become the dominant source of what is currently known as human expression. And if AI were to replace a sufficient quantity of original human works, culture would enter a kind of death spiral, in which no new art can arise except as rehashings of what came before.

The best way to combat the automation of human culture is to support culture itself. This can’t be done just once, but must happen over and over again, as a practice. A season pass to your local symphony, theater, or ballet offers one way to help someone develop such a relationship. So does buying real art from real artists—galleries in your area are bound to offer affordable drawings, prints, paintings, and plastic arts that can help start a collection. Many bookstores feature works by local writers too.

Culture would be better if more people produced it in addition to consuming it. Digital life confused that process. It appeared to democratize creation, giving everyone the tools to make music, writing, art, and moving images—and then distribute their wares globally. Online platforms also distorted culture into a thing primarily meant to produce attention and monetization. Nothing was really ever stopping you from learning to produce (and then improve) your own cultural work, whether music, writing, painting, ceramics, or whatever else that might strike you. Buy a violin or an easel (or anything of the sort that seems right for your gift recipient), along with a local class to get them started.

Gifts for the Unabiding Din of Machines

AI chats you up constantly now. Word processors, apps, even Google searches now typically initiate a conversation with an AI agent, whether you asked for one or not. Digital-detox gifts offer one way to quell the noise: for example, a redesigned but old-school media device such as the Fosi Audio compact-disc player, a deliberately limited digital device such as the Light Phone, or the Brick smartphone lock. But those options are defensive. By contrast, an array of objects that offer simple, quiet, and unmediated attention remains available. A nice mechanical wristwatch—or even a simple, cheap Casio—can do it. So can a good kitchen tool, such as an ice-cream scoop or a potato ricer. A delightful notebook and writing implement are always good for some quiet, as is a print book of almost any type.

Gifts for the Digital Panopticon

Years ago, information such as your home address and voting history might have been available in public records, but they were obfuscated by the need to access them in person. Online services ended that. And then, Big Tech started collecting and sharing information about you, with or without your permission. AI extends that tendency by improving machines’ abilities to infer knowledge about individuals—including intimate facts—from the ever-enlarging supply of data (including apparently innocuous data) constantly collected by apps, services, sensors, facial-recognition cameras, transaction records, and more. The ability to carry out one’s life in modest obscurity had already become difficult. AI could do away with it entirely.

[Read: Read this website while you still can]

You can buy tools that can, to some extent, defend against digital surveillance: a Faraday bag can block incoming and outgoing signals when you put your smartphone inside it; a service such as DeleteMe finds and removes information about you online; and adversarial apparel can stymie computer-vision surveillance. But you can also reduce your digital footprint in simpler ways. An envelope of cash allows your gift recipient to spend money without creating a transaction record for data-broker discovery. A nice paper planner allows you to keep a calendar without sharing your deeds with Google. Gmail can scan all of your emails for advertising targeting, something a private email account on a service such as Proton Mail or Apple iCloud+ does not. A subscription, along with a custom domain name, would make for a privacy-forward gift.

Gifts for the Swamp of Sycophancy

Large language models tend to affirm what a user provides as input—“I agree”—even if that entails validating flawed views or circumventing accurate responses. Over time, AI models’ designers may reduce this sycophancy effect, but they will have to overcome a strong incentive not to do so: an agreeable and even obsequious AI creates greater engagement, use, and thereby value for the AI companies. Should AI’s tendency to grovel increase while human reliance on it also expands, then a plurality of people may come to expect and acclimate to sycophancy in every nook and cranny of ordinary life.

To help someone wade out of the swamp, a gift needs to offer psychological and social effects rather than utility or even delight. A demanding book of puzzles is an option, such as Cain’s Jawbone (a legendary 1934 literary puzzle) and The New York Times Seriously Hard Crossword Puzzles. A difficult or complex board game (Car Wars, perhaps, or High Frontier 4 All) or video game (such as Dark Souls) could fit the bill too.

Physical activity also has a role to play: A couple of trips to an indoor-climbing wall or a few consultations with a personal trainer might be enough to reveal that the world was not built for you alone.

Gifts for Trust’s Twilight

The loss of shared reality, fueled by the swamp of sycophancy, could cause members of the public to withdraw into their own addled, private realities. Just as likely, it could ignite paranoia. Every email, text message, phone call, viral video, and internet post that seems authentic might also be fake. AI impersonation, already enormously convincing, is poised to improve, and tools to detect evidence of AI intervention in text and audiovisual material remain dubious. Once every shard of apparently earnest or human-created content raises suspicion, almost nothing will seem trustworthy. When trust itself cannot be assumed, perhaps ever, an almost delusional suspicion may become the default mode of interaction.

Some companies are trying to solve the trust problems that computers create with … more computers. Hardware-security keys such as YubiKey offer better protection against phishing and hacking attacks than password managers or two-factor authentication. And Vicem, designed to avert telephone scams, offers an AI phone monitor. Other, simpler tools also exist. You can still get a landline number that you don’t share publicly—or a supposedly private virtual number from a service such as GetUnlisted. A more baroque (and affected) solution from the past: a personalized wax seal, along with stationery on which to apply it.

Gifts for the End of Serendipity

Optimization and efficiency, once values of business alone, have leaked into ordinary life. The practice of living one’s life—dating, parenting, dining out, working, even seeking leisure—has turned into accomplishing tasks along the way to supposedly even better tasks. In the end, human life may turn away from any encounter whose outcome is not predictable, even known in advance. Contingency, accident, and improvisation could disappear as people eschew those putatively useless disomforts—and then forget about them entirely.

In a way, a good gift of any kind—AI-aware or not—counts as a gift of serendipity. Something given as a true surprise, and perhaps even absent of need, offers a door to an unexpected act. I can’t tell you what might count as such a gift for the individuals on your list; consider it a gift of judgment to ponder the matter. One idea: a paper map or atlas, the kind that can live in a car’s seat-back pocket. It might inspire someone to use a city absent computer guidance.

Serendipity may be on the decline, but many of the sources that once delivered it remain available, if in fragile forms. A monthly print-magazine subscription (such as this one) puts a package of surprising stories in the mailbox on the regular. A short-wave radio, or an HDTV antenna and HDHomeRun device, delivers whatever is being broadcast right now to your home. A gift certificate to a local shop with no online ordering, or to a local restaurant that does not offer delivery, can ignite the delight of browsing again.

An apocalypse feels like something you can do nothing about beyond panicking or hunkering down, as if awaiting a meteor’s collision with Earth.

[Read: What if the apocalypse is already here?]

But “apocalypse” may not be the right way to understand the distinctive challenges that artificial intelligence presents to human life. The temptation to catastrophize—an urge the internet rewarded long before AI—can obscure the ways that ordinary life can steer nimbly around cataclysm simply by each of us making modest choices where alternatives are available.

When carried out in the best spirit, gifts allow recipients and givers alike to see the world, and their place in it, in a marginally different light. This makes the holidays an ideal time to imagine and then embrace slightly different ways of living. None of artificial intelligence’s apocalypses, at least the ones humankind might survive, represents wholly new circumstances that AI invented. Instead, artificial intelligence wore deeper ruts into prior habits.

One such rut is the discourse surrounding artificial intelligence: Either the world will be saved by AI, or else AI will ruin it. You and I and all of the people on your holiday gift list get cast as passengers, careening toward deliverance or doom. The reality will probably be more banal. Most of AI’s threats are not existential, and likewise many of the defenses against those threats will not defeat the technology. The survival equipment suitable to fortify against that erosion will be correspondingly ordinary. A notebook preserves the capacity to write. A violin hands down the experience of making music. A dinner out demands face-to-face interactions with friends. A telephone whose number only a few people know rings with delight rather than foreboding. A paper map requires interpretation; a library invites perusal; a bowling lane can facilitate new conversation. The world is not ending.
