Two Risk-Takers Walk Into a Football Stadium

A lot of NFL games this time of year are as appealing as day-old oatmeal. By late November, half of the teams in the league have a losing record and muddle around at midfield. Coaches call plays that seem calculated to keep their job, soothe critics, and justify their run-pass and go-or-no-go decisions to their owners. Sunday night’s matchup between the San Francisco 49ers and the Minnesota Vikings, however, will be genuine must-see TV for anyone interested in artful, inventive football—and for anyone who cares about leadership.

Kyle Shanahan, the head coach of the 49ers, and Brian Flores, the Vikings’ defensive coordinator, are the two most nonconformist minds in the NFL, and each has been punished at times for high-risk unorthodoxy. They will bring the No. 1 offense and the No. 1 defense, respectively, to the game—a meeting that promises to be revealing for each of them. These guys take chances, and they are worth watching to see how comfortable they are with the consequences of their decisions.

Both go all-in with their play-calling. According to a CBS Sports analysis, Shanahan leads the league in sending all five eligible receivers downfield, declining to hold anything back for extra quarterback protection. The same analysis found Flores leading the league in blitzing—sending at least five men rushing headlong at the passer—at risk of leaving gaps on the field. “There’s going to be a lot of hit and miss both ways,” Shanahan said at a press conference this week.

[Read: The Los Angeles Rams are trying to defeat time]

Shanahan has lost the Super Bowl three agonizing times as a head coach or deputy. In 2017, he was blamed for surrendering the biggest comeback in the game’s history as the play caller for the Atlanta Falcons, who at one point had led the New England Patriots 28–3, only to be defeated in overtime. Shanahan’s own quarterback, Matt Ryan, openly second-guessed his decisions in the aftermath. Three years later, Shanahan was back in the game with the 49ers and lost a 10-point lead to the Kansas City Chiefs. In 2024, he lost another Lombardi Trophy to the Chiefs in overtime, 25–22. “Everyone talks to me about How do you live with what happened in the Super Bowl?” Shanahan told me a few years ago. He said that if he makes a wrong call, “I never regret it. I’m like, Fuck it. Maybe I didn’t make the right call.”

As for Flores, he won four Super Bowls as an assistant coach with the Patriots before becoming the Miami Dolphins’ head coach, and then was fired in 2022 despite having gone 10–6 and 9–8 in his final two seasons. When he couldn’t get another head-coaching job, he risked retaliation and career immolation by filing a racial-discrimination class-action suit against the NFL, which is headed to court.

His creative defense has carried the 7–2 Vikings all season by blitzing at a rate of more than 70 percent. “The only regrets I ever have come when we didn’t blitz,” Flores said at a press conference this month. To put the numbers in context, no other team in the league blitzes much more than 40 percent of the time, avoiding it for fear of giving up foolish-looking big plays. “Usually, people get got, if you do it too much,” Shanahan told journalists this week. “That’s what’s been so impressive.” The Vikings have allowed 12 touchdowns all season, but have sacked opposing quarterbacks 37 times.

One reason Flores’s strategy has been so successful is that he is a master of disguise. He gives his players discretion to make on-the-fly adjustments, so opposing teams have trouble knowing who is coming and frequently hesitate or are thrown into confusion. The Vikings’ 37 sacks have come from a dozen different players, which means quarterbacks often don’t know where to look.

[From the July 2026 issue: As American as the forward pass]

But the Vikings will meet an equal and opposing force in the 49ers, who are 8–1. Most teams regularly commit at least one receiver to help protect the quarterback. Not these guys. Shanahan fans out an array of versatile, sticky-handed speed burners, including Christian McCaffrey, who runs like a downhill boulder, and the human streamer kite George Kittle. Opponents wrench their necks trying to cover 11 players who have caught five or more passes. According to the CBS Sports analysis, the 49ers play “five out” on 80.1 percent of their passing plays. They don’t attack for the sake of attacking; instead, they deploy a coherent design that creates vertical and horizontal states of emergency for the other team. “It’s thought out,” Flores said at a press conference this week. “There’s a rhyme and reason behind it all.”

Shanahan and Flores are not flinchers. In a 2022 podcast, Shanahan explained his play-calling philosophy: “It’s not ever going to be based off of, Yeah, this is probably a little risky to do this. I totally believe it’s right, but I’m not going to do it, because it’s risky, perception-wise, and this could get me blamed,” he said, and added, “That’s not what you want from a play caller. That’s not what you want from a leader.”

This will be one game that doesn’t need Carrie Underwood’s “Waiting All Day for Sunday Night” power-yodeling to amp it up. On exhibit will be two strong decision makers who have prepared as thoroughly as possible, make their calls clearheadedly, and don’t worry about appearances.

Hollywood Needs a New Pixar

Hollywood earned its cultural monopoly by absorbing radical technologies that it first resisted: sound, color, computer graphics. People in the industry initially feared that Pixar’s computer animation would replace human actors with “digital doubles.” Similarly, most of the conversation about AI today revolves around cost savings and artist displacement. These issues are important, but they miss an even bigger shift that AI is accelerating. Ask a teenager where their favorite characters come from, and the answer is as likely to be a creator platform—YouTube, Roblox, TikTok—as a Hollywood studio. Unless moviemakers evolve, they risk losing their place at the center of culture. Those who combine new technology with storytelling, and who bring audiences into the creative process, will define the next century of entertainment.

Silicon Valley and Hollywood went through a version of this before, during the early partnership between Pixar and Disney that was crucial to Pixar’s success. Disney storytellers gave Pixar pointed feedback on early Pixar characters, leading John Lasseter, Pete Docter, and the rest of the brain trust to take screenwriting classes. Pixar didn’t believe that technical people were any less creative than artists. Every discipline mattered. The result: Toy Story.

Disney didn’t produce Toy Story by hiring more CGI artists into its 2-D pipeline. Instead, it partnered with Pixar, whose mission was to make movies with the new technology, and eventually acquired it. Pixar had tech that worked, artists who were intimate with it, and a culture of mutual admiration among its artistic and technological creators. Disney had marketing, distribution, major capital, logistical know-how, and a glorious legacy. Neither could build what the other had.

Something similar needs to happen now. Hollywood has an opportunity to partner with studios built for AI, but the rise of creator culture will make the transition harder. During the shift to computer animation and graphics in the 1980s and ’90s, the distribution formats stayed the same—theaters, home video, and broadcast. Today, format, distribution, and production technology are changing all at once.

[Read: The corner of Hollywood that’s most susceptible to AI]

YouTube made more than $60 billion last year, about $15 billion more than Netflix brought in. To keep up, Hollywood studios are adapting properties from creator platforms. They found success with Backrooms (which cost about $10 million to make but collected more than $400 million at the box office) and Obsession (less than $1 million to make; more than $520 million at the box office), and are now rushing to find more YouTube properties and talent. Though this is smart, these audiences were formed and cultivated on other platforms. The Minecraft movie made almost $1 billion, a fraction of the game franchise’s $10 to $12 billion. The movie was effectively a marketing event for the game.

AI didn’t cause the shift to creator platforms, but it will accelerate it. MrBeast, who has more than 500 million YouTube subscribers, built an empire without AI, but the technology allows would-be creators to realize their visions, and it enables existing creators to work more efficiently.  

But what about the ethics of using AI models that were trained on artists’ work without their permission or payment? Artists should be compensated for the work that was used to train models. These issues need to be settled in court and through regulation and licensing. The technology won’t go away, so we need artists to help set its terms.

But rejecting AI altogether is not the answer. Across Asia, creators face many of the same concerns, but the technological and cultural shift is further along. In China, microdramas—scripted series typically filmed in portrait mode and designed for smartphone viewing, with episodes lasting one to three minutes—have compressed the distance between creation, distribution, and monetization; generative AI fits naturally into an industrial ecosystem built around rapid iteration, low production costs, and huge volumes of content. In Korea, webtoons and web novels move across streaming, games, music, and other formats. In India, a multilingual audience creates especially strong incentives for AI-assisted dubbing. It is a misperception that creators in the global South care less about copyright, labor costs, or artistic integrity—if anything, Asia’s creator industries are quickly converging with the West on these questions. The difference is that much of the experimentation is happening within entertainment industries that have been more entrepreneurial and are more willing to embrace new technology.

The first “Toy Story moment” for both AI and the new formats it enables—a work whose artistic and cultural appeal makes the technology itself almost beside the point—may not be far away. The companies and countries that achieve it first could change perceptions of AI in creative work and accrue outsize cultural reach. In that sense, the U.S.-China technology competition is also becoming a competition over who builds the tools, platforms, and creative communities that shape global culture.

Hollywood has defined mainstream culture for a century, and storytelling remains its greatest asset. However, treating YouTube, Roblox, and TikTok as farm systems to feed traditional distribution misses that audiences are on the farm.

Netflix recently laid off 1,000 employees; Disney laid off roughly 1,500 this year and has said more layoffs are coming. Skydance’s co-CEOs have told staff to expect layoffs too. Studios have focused on cutting costs, but Hollywood needs to build for new formats, not just make old ones cheaper.

Hollywood needs to fundamentally shift the way it operates, moving from producing finished products meant for passive consumption to developing stories and characters using input from their audiences. Instead of performing autopsies in hindsight, every major studio could put out drafts and iterate with audiences, the way creators do. Kane Parsons started Backrooms with one YouTube short and expanded it into a series when audiences responded positively. Vivienne Medrano released a fan-financed YouTube pilot of Hazbin Hotel; A24 ordered a full series after it drew a huge following.

Refining work using audience input doesn’t mean asking fans to vote on every creative choice. Strong directors have always filtered feedback, taking notes that tighten their narrative and discarding what dilutes their core vision. Borrowing a page from tech, studios could also be much more rigorous with data sourcing, A/B testing, and analysis to understand their audience’s behavior.

This audience-centered approach can be applied to all parts of moviemaking. Films that perform well after an inexpensive tease, such as an animatic (storyboards that are cut together), can secure financing quickly, because financiers aren’t being asked to gamble many millions of dollars on an untested idea. Marketing happens in parallel with production. Your audience becomes not just fans but ambassadors, because they feel they are part of the process. The production becomes part of the story. Markiplier, a YouTuber whose real name is Mark Fischbach, created and financed a film, Iron Lung, which he planned to release in a small number of independent theaters. His fans emailed exhibitors until national chains picked it up.

Vision is not replaced by data; instead, data can help studios find the audiences for visionary work, position it well, and understand which ambitious projects have real demand before committing hundreds of millions of dollars to them. If Orson Welles could have shared his early ideas with audiences in the 1940s, while making Citizen Kane, data wouldn’t have diluted the ambition of his deep-focus shots, broken timeline, and montages. Analyzing the public feedback could have helped him find his audience faster.

Hollywood has a finite window to make these changes. Creators who already operate this way, such as MrBeast, are building their own production studios and will have less incentive to partner. Others will follow.

[Rose Horowitch: The film students who can no longer sit through films]

Studios are experimenting by launching short phone series on YouTube and TikTok, signing deals with top creators, and hiring creator-platform experts into executive positions. This is smart, but these initiatives still prioritize bringing existing franchises to new screens over developing stories alongside audiences. Dropping a digital executive into a traditional, risk-averse studio pipeline doesn’t change the entire studio’s culture. The business model, people, and creative culture that make a great film studio differ from those that are best for building a YouTube series or Roblox game.

Hollywood doesn’t need to become a tech company, and Silicon Valley shouldn’t arrogantly assume that tech is superior to a story’s ability to move culture. Legacy studios still have what many creators need: capital, global marketing, distribution know-how, and deep story expertise. Just as Disney secured its future by partnering with and acquiring Pixar, today’s legacy studios need to collaborate with a new class of studios, ones that know how to build on creator platforms, are rooted in Hollywood storytelling, and treat technologists and artists equally.

The Disney-Pixar relationship worked because Bob Iger gave Pixar co-founder Ed Catmull real power over Disney Animation. Other Hollywood acquisitions of gaming studios or creator talent have struggled when the acquired company lacked similar authority. A real partnership would allow legacy media to originate ideas on new platforms instead of being licensing arms for new players.

AI will have its Toy Story moment. The studios that achieve it will know how to reach audiences on newer platforms, in formats that bring the audience along for the ride.

In late October, I met a Yemeni military officer at a high-end mall in Riyadh. He wore a dishdasha and had the gruff, weary manner of an aging career soldier. His unit had been fighting the Houthi militia in southern Yemen, more than 800 miles from the immaculate, marble-floored refuge at the center of the Saudi capital where we ordered coffee.

He was mapping the battlefield on the table with his hands, describing how the latest round of fighting had played out, when cellphones began beeping simultaneously all around us: an incoming-missile warning. Moments later, loud booms echoed through the mall, followed by audible gasps from nearby tables and a child’s cry of fear. Shoppers froze in their tracks, some glancing anxiously toward the glass dome above us.  

The Yemeni officer looked at me without speaking. A grim smile played on his lips, as if to say: They are all learning about the Houthis now.

The missiles were intercepted, but the moment stayed with me. A new reality was intruding on a population coddled for years by visions of a frictionless post-oil future, when new technologies would keep everyone happy and entertained.

Saudi Arabia is at war. The Houthis have demonstrated, by means of recurring missile barrages, that they can shut down much of the country’s economy at will. A deadly strike on the main airport in Riyadh killed at least 14 people in mid-October and injured hundreds. More than a month later, few flights have resumed.

“The kingdom today faces the most dangerous crisis in its history,” the Saudi journalist Abdulrahman al-Rashed told a Saudi podcaster last month.

That kind of candor is extremely hard to come by in Saudi Arabia. None of the Saudi or Yemeni officials I met in Riyadh in October would speak with me on the record. They told me that the monarchy is now taking the Houthi threat far more seriously than in the past. Riyadh has struck deals to bring thousands of soldiers from Pakistan and Syria to help defend the country’s border with Yemen. But these efforts may not be enough. The Houthis are building up their forces too, with help from Iran, China, North Korea, and Russia.

[Graeme Wood: The only thing the Houthis have done successfully]

The Houthis are no longer the rebel upstarts of a decade ago. They are well on their way to becoming the North Korea of the Arab world: an insular, well-armed, and fanatical statelet that poses a threat to the entire region. They have an AI-assisted weapons industry and have studied the Ukraine war; their information operations are way ahead of their Saudi and Yemeni rivals’. The Houthis could soon become more dangerous than Iran, their long-standing patron.

The Saudi military was not designed for this kind of war. The country’s rulers have long been reluctant to build an army that could one day pose a threat to the monarchy. Even now they have dismissed the notion of sending their own troops, apart from elite special forces already deployed in Yemen, to fight the Houthis. But they may soon find that you cannot rely on foreigners when your country is facing a very real threat of invasion.  

The current Houthi military offensive began in September, when the group captured the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, on the Red Sea. Saudi Arabia’s leaders believe that this plan was largely devised by Iran. In October, I met with a senior Saudi official who described a strategy coordinated by Tehran and its allies in the “Axis of Resistance” over the past several months. The latest chapter, the official said, began in the spring, when President Trump ordered the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. That left Tehran with limited options to retaliate, so Iranian officials met with their Houthi allies and persuaded them to launch an offensive on the Bab el-Mandeb.

The Iranians, the official said, agreed to help make it happen. A Houthi delegation attended the funeral of Ayatollah Ali Khamenei in Tehran in July, he told me, and when it returned, the Mahan Air flight that carried it also conveyed a whole team of Axis military technicians and commanders, including two North Koreans, a Syrian who had been part of the Assad regime, and members of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and of Lebanese Hezbollah, the Shiite militia. The plane was loaded with cash and weapons, including drone parts and missile-guidance systems, the Saudi official told me. (Some details of the Mahan Air flight have been reported previously, including the weapons and Iranian officers on board.)

The Saudis had gotten word of the plane’s cargo and bombed the runway of the airport in the Yemeni capital to prevent it from landing. But the flight was diverted to Hodeida, a city on the Red Sea coast that is also under Houthi control, and landed there. Separately, the Saudi official told me, Russia delivered a large shipment of weapons and technology, concealed under thousands of tons of wheat.

If the Saudi official’s account is accurate, it would suggest that the Houthis are receiving more international support than ever. Even if the report was exaggerated for the benefit of an American journalist—Saudi officials are desperate to get more military assistance from the United States—it still points to a larger truth about what the Houthis have become.

The Houthis are the world’s problem. They have proved that they can block a waterway that carries about 15 percent of the world’s trade. They now control about a third of Yemen’s territory and most of its population, and aim to conquer it all. Their use of child soldiers has been well documented for a decade, as has their frequent use of torture, arbitrary detentions, and disappearances. Their rule is rooted in a revolutionary ideology that does not stop at Yemen’s borders and that thrives on constant war. They have challenged Saudi control of Mecca and Medina, Islam’s holiest cities, and they often boast that they will liberate Jerusalem.

[Thomas Wright: The price of defeat in Iran]

These are not empty ambitions. The Houthis are constantly expanding their missile and drone production, using fiber optics and other materials supplied by China. Evidence recently came to light that they have used the AI tool Claude to improve their guided-missile technology. They have developed pragmatic relationships with al-Qaeda and the al-Shabaab terrorist group, despite sectarian differences (the Houthis adhere to an offshoot of Shiite Islam; the other two groups are Sunni). And, in a show of political dexterity and ideological fervor, they have been recruiting and providing training to ethnic and tribal subgroups throughout the Horn of Africa, in Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Sudan. These outreaches appear to be modeled on Iran’s efforts to build up regional allies, and could add more tinder to an already volatile region of Africa.

For now, Saudi Arabia lies on the front line of any effort to contain the Houthis’ power. The United States is too deep in its war with Iran to provide anything more than minimal help, and the Europeans are focused on Ukraine. The other Gulf countries have good reasons to fight the Houthis, but for various reasons, they will not.

The Omanis have been hedging their bets for years—allowing the Houthis to smuggle arms across their border with Yemen and providing some intelligence—in case the Houthis end up as the winners. The United Arab Emirates fought the Houthis inside Yemen for years alongside the Saudis, but late last year, long-running tensions between the two countries reached a breaking point, and the Saudis ordered the Emiratis to withdraw. The Emiratis’ absence has damaged the broader war effort.

The Saudis, who have long backed the internationally recognized Yemeni government in Aden, organized a counteroffensive after the Houthis captured the Bab el-Mandeb. They embedded Saudi commandos with Yemeni government forces across the country. Some were killed in a devastating Houthi strike on October 19. The Saudi official I met in Riyadh told me that the near-term goal is to recapture all of the territory that was lost in September, and then secure some kind of truce. The Saudi-backed Yemeni forces have not yet achieved that, and it is not clear that they will. “I don’t see a way out unless the U.S. agrees to provide direct air support,” Nadwa al-Dawsari, an associate fellow at the Middle East Institute, told me.

Even if the Saudis are able to achieve a respite from the fighting, the Houthis will retain the whip hand in the conflict, because they will still be able to strike the kingdom’s airports and oil facilities. And they will surely use any truce to continue expanding their military, as they have in the past. The Saudi official told me that the kingdom’s long-term plan is to build a larger and more unified Yemeni military that is capable of pushing the Houthis back and “eliminating their military capabilities.”

That is a tall order. The Yemeni military officer I met in Riyadh last month described a fighting force that is hamstrung by clashing political and regional loyalties. “There are forces loyal to Islah”—the Yemeni Islamist party—“and they hate us,” he said, referring to his own more secular faction. (He did not need to add that the feeling is mutual.) In the south, many of the soldiers are more interested in creating a separate state than in fighting the Houthis. “The Houthis know all about the divisions on our side,” the officer said, “and they take advantage of it.”

The Saudis bring problems of their own to the conflict. One is the kingdom’s culture of entitlement and corruption, which has bled into its behavior in Yemen. For decades, the Saudis maintained their influence in Yemen through a patronage system of stipends paid to tribal sheikhs and military figures, and they took a quasi-imperial view of Yemenis as primitive underlings. They had a reputation for paying their proxy soldiers so poorly or so late that some had to sell their weapons in order to feed their children.

This pattern of behavior generated deep resentment, partly because the Yemenis got a glimpse of a different approach. The Emiratis spent decades fostering a culture of discipline and accountability in their own country, and in Yemen, they had a reputation for training their allies, paying them higher salaries, and demanding results in return. The Emiratis—who run a small but highly effective military of their own—therefore had far more success with their Yemeni recruits. The Saudis say they are reforming their system, but that will take time.

The Saudis and their allies are now struggling to relieve the month-long Houthi siege of Taiz, one of Yemen’s largest cities. If the Houthis capture it, nothing but open roads will separate them from Aden, the base of Yemen’s internationally recognized government and its military headquarters. “There is nothing going on to defend Aden,” Henry Thompson, a Yemen expert who has worked with the United Nations, told me. (Just in the past few days, the threat to Aden has grown, and some signs of defensive preparations have become evident there.)

The Houthis, meanwhile, have taken measures in the territories they control that could pull Yemeni troops into protracted conflict even if they succeed in retaking ground. Some recent seizures of cargo destined for Houthi ports have intercepted equipment for digging tunnels, including excavation tools and ventilation systems. The Houthis appear to have successfully imported large quantities of this kind of gear in recent years.

[Robert F. Worth: Saudi Arabia is running out of options]

“In Hodeida, the Houthis have built a city underneath the city,” Mohammed al-Basha, a Yemen analyst and the founder of the Basha Report, a geopolitical-risk advisory firm, told me. “For anyone trying to capture that place, it would be like going into Gaza.”

For the moment, the war is not going well. After making some gains in October, the Saudi-backed forces announced on November 15 that they had been forced to withdraw from the area around the Bab el-Mandeb. I met several members of Yemen’s internationally recognized government in Riyadh—their rear base—and they tried to put on a brave face, with varying degrees of success. Some seem resigned to deeper losses. “The Houthis will take Aden, and they will keep going,” one told me.

The current battlefield is in southern Yemen, but everyone I spoke with agreed that the Houthis will eventually open a new front in the north and attempt to invade Saudi Arabia. That would make it impossible for the Saudis to deny that they are at war. Until now, they have held firmly to the claim that this is an intra-Yemeni conflict, and that Saudi Arabia is a third party. But the Houthis have made clear for years that they consider themselves to be at war with Riyadh, and they frequently mock Yemen’s internationally recognized government as a Saudi fig leaf.

Almost no one in Saudi Arabia wants to acknowledge the possibility that they could soon be forced to defend their country. “Why should we send our young men to defend the Bab el-Mandeb?” one official asked me in Riyadh. “The conflict is inside Yemen.” He went on to describe the Houthis as nothing more than an “annoyance.”

None of the ordinary Saudis I spoke with in Riyadh, whether old friends or people I approached in cafés, seemed comfortable talking about the war. Saudi Arabia is an autocratic society where dissent can land a person in jail—but the reticence may also reflect the dissonance people feel between the war and everything their ruler has been telling them for the past decade. Saudi Arabia’s problems are supposed to be solved through technology and innovation. Being dragged into a bloody war with a radical militia is not part of that plan.

It is not part of America’s plans, either—especially not since the Trump administration waged an expensive and largely futile air war against the Houthis lasting seven weeks in early 2025. But that could change. The Houthis, like their Iranian allies, believe that they are waging a metaphysical battle against America, and they are determined to topple every outpost of American influence in the region.
