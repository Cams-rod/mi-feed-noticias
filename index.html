Updated at 6:16 p.m. ET on November 1, 2026

Haley Stevens nearly won the Democratic nomination for Senate in Michigan in August, then immediately endorsed the winner, Abdul El-Sayed. She has spent the months since then vacillating, expressing doubts about his campaign while maintaining her support. But last week, her fiancé said that he intends to vote for El-Sayed’s opponent, Mike Rogers, and now she has followed, endorsing the Republican just two days before the election.

Stevens’s dramatic reversal met with immediate cries of opportunism or political stupidity. But Stevens seems instead to be following through on a principled objection to El-Sayed’s rhetoric and associations that threaten the state’s Jewish population. The trouble is that her application of those principles is misguided, undermining the values she claims to uphold. Before this weekend, Stevens could credibly accuse El-Sayed of overlooking the bigotry of his allies out of political expedience. Now she, too, is guilty of that very sin.

I grew up in West Bloomfield, Michigan, a heavily Jewish suburb of Detroit, and have many friends and family who still live in the state. Every Jewish Michigander I know has serious concerns about El-Sayed. Their issue is not only that he refuses to accept Israel’s right to exist; it’s that he elevated anti-Zionism into the centerpiece of his primary campaign, treating the Israel lobby as the singular force lying behind every economic and political problem afflicting America.

During the campaign, he has energized an activist cohort whose rhetoric habitually goes beyond mere criticism of Israel, or even anti-Zionism, and extends into conspiratorial anti-Semitism. He campaigned with Amir Makled, a candidate for University of Michigan’s Board of Regents who had reposted some of Candace Owens’s anti-Semitic tweets. More notorious, he attached himself to Hasan Piker, a Marxist-Leninist who has repeatedly employed anti-Semitic rhetoric. When a network of anti-Israel protestors was charged with plotting to intimidate local Jews, El-Sayed questioned the indictment, telling reporters, “It’s a lot more about what you’re advocating for that gets you indicted or not indicted, rather than what you did.”

El-Sayed has made some concessions to the Jewish community. He apologized for his response to a terrorist attack on a Jewish nursery school (“hurt people hurt people”), which he acknowledged minimized the pain felt by the attack’s targets. But for the most part he has tried to strike a very careful balance, assuaging liberal Jews without alienating any elements of his anti-Zionist base. After the left-wing media personality Cenk Uygur released an advertisement blaming Israel for the cost of groceries, Stevens demanded he renounce it. El-Sayed took days to reply, finally stating, “I do not agree with the contents of that ad,” without describing it as offensive.

As the American left has grown more fixated on Gaza, it is becoming more common for progressive organizations and cultures to treat Zionism in any form—including liberal Zionism that harshly criticizes Israel’s government—as a racist ideology, and to ostracize anybody who refuses to renounce it. American Jews rationally fear that El-Sayed is not only willing but eager to import this norm into the Democratic Party, functionally excluding the overwhelming majority of the Jewish population.

Jewish Democrats who have a defter touch than Stevens have tried to work through their reservations, with little success. Josh Whinston, a rabbi in Ann Arbor, wrote an op-ed in the Detroit Free Press conveying his fears about El-Sayed’s rhetoric and political alliances, and El-Sayed’s apparent lack of interest in allaying them. Whinston wrote that he has “said, again and again, that there is a way forward together that does not ask him to abandon his values. He has not yet responded.” Jordan Acker, a Michigan regent whose home was vandalized in the middle of the night by anti-Israel activists, told The New Yorker, “My top issue is the safety of my children, and nothing I’ve heard from the El-Sayed campaign suggests they take that seriously at all.”

Both Whinston and Acker, despite their reservations about El-Sayed, have ruled out endorsing Rogers. Stevens did too, before abruptly reversing course after El-Sayed disinvited her from a preelection unity rally. This decision by El-Sayed, which undercuts the most essential function of a unity rally, is characteristic of his preference for refusing to break ranks with his most left-wing, fanatically anti-Zionist supporters. Whether or not you agree with El-Sayed’s ethic—I do not—one must acknowledge that he is willing to follow it even at some risk to his chances of winning. In this way, El-Sayed, like Stevens, appears to care more about putting his own stamp on the Democratic Party than defeating the Republicans.

Stevens seems to have principles as well. The trouble is that her principled opposition to religious and ethnic intolerance does not extend to Muslim and Arab communities.

During the same period of time that El-Sayed has been winking at a political movement inflected with anti-Semitism, he has been targeted by a political message that is not merely inflected with racism but saturated with it. Donald Trump has made hysterical attacks on El-Sayed, such as “I’m here to keep a jihadist Muslim Brotherhood member from becoming a United States senator,” and accused him of planning to impose Sharia law, among other wild charges. Rather than use his first name, he has sneeringly called El-Sayed “Muhammed,” as if to erase all doubt that his objection is not to any position El-Sayed has taken but to his religion.

Rogers has not repudiated any of this rhetoric. Instead, he has charged El-Sayed with refusing to condemn the Muslim Brotherhood, even after El-Sayed did precisely that.

Perhaps the party of Trump is so deeply corrupted by racism that hoping a Republican would renounce it is absurd, like asking a shark to apologize for mauling a swimmer. But Stevens is not a Republican or a Trump supporter. And she has thrown herself behind Rogers without making any demands of him.

Tolerance cannot be the ethical rationale for doing so. It’s impossible to construct any standard of behavior toward religious minorities that El-Sayed fails but Rogers passes.

And so, whatever message Stevens imagines she is conveying, it is not what is coming across. Perhaps she is expressing a less than exalted notion—Nobody puts Haley Stevens in a corner—lashing out in humiliated defiance of El-Sayed for marginalizing her in the campaign’s final days. Or, even worse, she is communicating that prejudice against Jews is unacceptable but prejudice against Muslims and Arabs is not.

Updated at 5:43 p.m. ET on November 1, 2026

President Trump is deciding whether a resumption of strikes in Iran will finally deliver the victory he has already claimed he’s achieved. He also has two other questions to answer closer to home: Who gets the blame for a war his advisers assured him would end well before the midterms? And can he trust those same advisers when they tell him that the new plan will succeed where previous plans have failed?

For weeks, Trump has been contemplating a renewal of hostilities aimed at forcing Iran to negotiate, and advisers told us that a new round of attacks could come soon after Tuesday’s midterms. Trump has also been questioning allies about the job performances of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and General Dan Caine, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, two outside advisers close to Trump told us. Hegseth and Caine have been two of Trump’s key counselors on the war. In its earliest stages, they were also among the most publicly optimistic about its prospects—although they haven’t held a press conference since May. Trump often solicits opinions from a kitchen Cabinet of friends outside his administration before making any personnel moves.

[Read: Trump’s gamble to end the Iran war]

Hegseth is not a popular figure in much of the White House. Some close to Trump suggested to us that the defense secretary has not been honest about the state of the war, which he consistently paints in rosy terms to keep Trump happy. “Hegseth is good at telling the president what he wants to hear,” said one of the outside advisers. Sean Parnell, a Pentagon spokesperson, told us in a statement that “Secretary Hegseth always provides the President and White House senior leaders the full operational picture,” calling any suggestion otherwise “dead wrong.”

The defense chief has also sought to burnish his standing with the president by pursuing those suspected of leaking to the press, one of the worst transgressions on Trump’s list of personnel offenses. Last week, Hegseth abruptly fired one of the Joint Staff’s most senior officers, Army Lieutenant General Brett Sylvia, the director of planning. Officials who work for Hegseth said Sylvia failed lie-detector tests related to the disclosure of information to reporters. Sylvia hasn’t commented and couldn’t be reached.

Hegseth had ordered Sylvia to undergo at least three polygraphs as part of a broader leak investigation after news reports earlier this year described White House conversations and details about the shortage of munitions caused by the Iran war, the officials told us. Hegseth has decided that the Joint Staff, which reports to Caine, has a problem with leaks, a contention that Caine has not forcefully pushed back on, officials told us. Hegseth’s self-portrayal to Trump allies as having the president’s back resonates with a commander in chief who, since his first term, has suspected senior Pentagon officials of insufficient loyalty.

The White House spokesperson Anna Kelly, in a statement to us, gave an endorsement of Hegseth and Caine, praising their work in reshaping the Pentagon and declaring that “President Trump and everyone at the White House have full confidence in both Secretary Hegseth and General Caine, who are fully aligned with the President’s mission.”

Caine has appeared largely out of the loop over the alleged misdeeds of one of his own senior staff. When The Atlantic first reported that Sylvia was out, the Joint Staff issued a prewritten statement that thanked Sylvia for his “32 years of distinguished service to our nation.” Caine then learned additional details from the secretary’s team about what exactly had led to Sylvia’s dismissal, while Hegseth returned from a trip from Asia, the officials told us. Hours later, Caine’s office issued a new statement that said the original “did not appropriately reflect the full circumstances.” The Joint Staff said in a statement that the chairman offers “his best military advice and recommendations, along with all associated risks” but “does not publicly discuss these necessarily private conversations with civilian leadership.” That confidentiality protects service members “who are endangered by unacceptable unauthorized disclosures,” the statement added.

As the war has dragged on, Trump’s anger has risen over the conflict’s damage to Republicans’ midterm chances. A realization has set in at the White House that Trump is unlikely to pull out another remarkable political comeback in the midterms. The House appears gone, and the Senate may be too. Trump has raged in recent weeks that his messaging is not breaking through, and he now believes that the Iran war is at least in part to blame for his political predicament, the two advisers and an official told us. Not one to take responsibility himself, Trump is searching for a scapegoat, and has focused on those who had told him that a war with Iran would be quick and decisive.

Hegseth and Caine were both at the White House last week to present Trump and Vice President Vance with a series of options designed to create the conditions for Trump to declare victory. Admiral Brad Cooper, the top commander at U.S. Central Command, which is responsible for the military’s Middle East operations, was also at the White House last week. Trump now has to decide whether those options, and the advisers who offered them to him, are capable of bringing about a better outcome.

The military options before the president represent a series of unpalatable choices, current and former defense officials familiar with the plans told us. They give the president the chance to order strikes that would last a few hours, a few days, or several weeks. The president could also opt to maintain the U.S. naval blockade in the Strait of Hormuz while dangling the threat of a return to action. Or he could walk away.

Advocates for a resumption of strikes believe that the economic pressure campaign is working and that additional military activity would force Tehran to the negotiating table. A lengthy campaign would target Iran’s defenses, as in the war’s early days, before the U.S. shifted its strikes to concentrate on Iranian forces around the Strait of Hormuz. Iran’s infrastructure could be hit. The U.S. is expected to argue that the military is targeting infrastructure that serves both civilian and military purposes. But civilians would still be at risk. And such attacks could violate international humanitarian law. Another scenario calls for a days-long campaign against Iran’s nuclear program.

[Read: Trump is preparing for a long war]

Many within the administration expect that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, whose political fate remains uncertain after Israel’s election, would support a resumption of strikes, given his enthusiasm for the war and his belief that it would help him retain power.

Even a devastating campaign, though, might not induce concessions from Tehran. The regime could survive the attacks and celebrate its resilience after absorbing another round of U.S. firepower. After the U.S. and Israel staged a weeks-long campaign that began in late February, subsequent short strikes have only hardened Iranian resolve and prompted Tehran to lash out at U.S. assets and allies in the region. And any strike campaign would further deplete munitions stockpiles.

“If those strikes don’t work, you’ve now sort of exposed the limits of those strikes,” Michael Cohen, the director of an initiative at the Stimson Center think tank that studies whether U.S. military interventions justify their costs. “You keep hitting them and they don’t back down. So what are you going to keep doing? Keep escalating?”

Some in Trump’s orbit hope even the threat of strikes would nudge Iran back to negotiations, though that strategy has failed many times before. Last Sunday, Vance sent a letter to Mohsen Rezaei, Iran’s security chief. He proposed a resumption of direct peace negotiations, stressing that Iran’s nuclear program would have to be addressed from the outset. Vance did not say what, if anything, Tehran would get in return. The international mediators who delivered the letter pushed for a meeting later this week, around the time Trump is expected to decide on the resumption of military operations.

None of the military options appears to address threats from Iran’s proxies in the region, principally the Houthis in Yemen, who have recently launched attacks on Saudi Arabia. Eliminating those proxies was one of Trump’s stated goals for the war. New strikes also could drive oil prices higher, imposing further economic costs on the United States and its allies. One official told us that Trump’s recent turn against Ukraine was in part motivated by his desire for more Russian energy to reach international markets and offset the impact of possible U.S. military action in the Middle East. Tehran also gets a say; retaliation could escalate the conflict.

Legislators have been briefed on parts of the plans. Some were unimpressed. Senator Chris Murphy, a Democrat from Connecticut, described what he had seen as “insane.” “It will lead to devastating escalation, and the likely destruction of our commitment to the rule of law,” Murphy said last week at the Middle East Institute, a Washington think tank.

Trump has not yet given the order to resume strikes, the outside advisers and the administration official told us. If he does, he could keep the decision closely held until the last moment. In the run-up to the war, the White House didn’t inform its closest allies or seek the input of senior administration officials at the Departments of Energy and Commerce, who might have flagged the economic damage that would result from Iran’s seizure of the Strait of Hormuz, a vital waterway for global energy supplies.

At the Pentagon, planning for a possible resumption of the war is now taking place amid renewed angst over Hegseth’s purge of senior commanders. He has fired or forced out more than two dozen generals and admirals. Commanders often say their job is to keep drama away from the troops so they can focus on the mission. But Sylvia’s firing and the accusations against him prompted another round of intense chatter over what happened, who might be next, and the message the firing was intended to send. Some officials at the Pentagon told us they suspect Hegseth was seeking to weaken the Joint Staff’s standing as the president assesses what the war has wrought. Hegseth in the past has not been shy about passing the buck for the outcome of a war he promoted. Earlier this year, he reportedly told Trump at Camp David that Deputy Secretary Stephen Feinberg was responsible for the munitions shortages. (Feinberg remains in the job.)

Parnell, the Pentagon spokesperson, said Hegseth is committed to “promoting the right senior military officers to leadership positions and retiring those who are unwilling to effectively serve the only person in government elected by the whole of the American people: President Donald J. Trump.” (Service members take an oath to defend the Constitution rather than pledging allegiance to a president.)

A devastating night for Republicans on Tuesday won’t necessarily reduce the chances of a resumption of hostilities, despite the war’s political toxicity. In the 2006 midterms, Democrats gained 31 seats in the House and six in the Senate, taking control of both chambers of Congress for the first time since 1994, a result widely interpreted as a repudiation of President George W. Bush’s war in Iraq. Immediately after the election, Bush blamed Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld for the losses and forced him out. Two months later, Bush ordered a surge of forces that ushered in one of the war’s deadliest periods.

For many travelers, flying has become an indignity to endure rather than an experience to enjoy. Seats are more cramped than ever; flight delays are becoming normalized; somehow, airlines have invented an even worse version of Economy. (Welcome to Basic!) Perhaps most unsettling, though, is the unpredictability of other people on board.

Videos of unhinged passengers attempting to open the plane door midair, fighting over reclining seats, shouting slurs, or otherwise disrupting flights have become routine viewing online. This fall, a first-class passenger on an American Airlines flight became violent; he was duct-taped to his seat while the plane made an emergency landing so he could be arrested. Social media tends to make extreme behavior seem more common than it is—although perhaps not in this case: FAA data show that the number of incidents involving unruly fliers has risen sharply since 2019.

On last night’s Saturday Night Live, hosted by Inde Navarrette, a layered spoof imagined how a major airline might finally address the stubborn issue of problem passengers in a way that would still benefit its bottom line. In the sketch, a Delta spokesperson, played by Ashley Padilla, acknowledged that these incidents are ticking up. No traveler, she said, should have to deal with a volatile seat neighbor. Padilla then proposed a solution: Delta Disruptive, “a new section of the plane exclusively for crazy people,” available “on all domestic flights within Florida.” This new airline class, she promised brightly, would welcome fliers who refused to sit in their assigned seat, as well as those who insist on smoking, Disney adults who want to chug beers, and passengers yelling their theories about the Illuminati to the entire cabin.

Delta Disruptive, Padilla pledged, would provide a space for the “basic, inconsiderate a-hole” as well as the “paranoid lunatic” endangering everyone on board, with a four-inch-thick slab of plexiglass separating it from the rest of the plane. In one testimonial, a passenger played by Mikey Day approvingly noted that the section didn’t make him check his bag, which was clearly never going to fit in the overhead compartment. Scenes depicting growing anarchy showed one passenger rolling a joint and another insistent about licking the toes of a man clipping his toenails in his seat. But don’t worry, Padilla said: To ensure that nothing spun too out of control, the section would be staffed by the “toughest, gayest ex–Spirit Airlines employees who have seen much worse.” One flight attendant, played by Kenan Thompson, threw a man’s “comfort iguana” out of the plane after it wouldn’t stop biting people.

[Read: The only thing worse than Spirit Airlines]

The sketch animated the hellish experience of, say, sitting next to an inappropriate passenger who won’t stop yelling about shadow people. But its deeper and more cynical humor came from slyly hinting at how airlines have evaded recognizing their own complicity in the deterioration of the flying experience. Not that Padilla—whose brittle cheeriness clearly masked an exasperation lurking under the surface—acknowledged any of this. Rather, she saw the ongoing issue of unruly in-flight behavior as yet another opportunity to make a buck. “You see, Delta didn’t want to deal with the constant problems these passengers create, but we still wanted their money,” she confessed.

This moment of candor reflects the way that real-life air carriers squeeze  money out of every possible scenario on board. On many airlines, even a modicum of comfort now comes at a premium—wanting to travel with a carry-on, for instance, or just drinking a glass of water—a circumstance that may be helping push travelers over the edge, especially as inequality in the air mirrors a sense of economic injustice on the ground. Earlier this year, The Wall Street Journal reported that airlines are reducing the size of economy cabins to make room for expensive lie-flat seats and rows with a few more inches of leg room, giving these companies still more opportunities to charge passengers extra. Additionally, soaring jet-fuel costs, exacerbated by the war with Iran, have caused airfare prices to spike an average of 23 percent since January, according to recent federal data.

[Read: Airlines are just banks now]

Even those who want nothing more than to reach their destination with zero frills may not be safe from price squeezes. A lawsuit filed earlier this year alleged that JetBlue gathered passenger data to set different prices for airfare based on personal information. Passengers who can’t trust their airline are less likely to trust anyone they encounter on their flights—including other passengers. It would take more than a plexiglass partition to solve that problem.

For more than 60 years, the Department of Justice has sent its attorneys and staff to polling places across the country to ensure that Americans can vote free of discrimination or intimidation. They’ve watched to see if Black voters were illegally hindered in any way, if those who use a wheelchair or walker could get into facilities, and if those who couldn’t read the ballot for any reason—blindness, lack of English proficiency, or simply not having the skill—had help. Officials selected locations that had had problems in the past or where people had reported concerns.  

When the feds would show up in the Tucson area of Arizona, where Spanish speakers and retirees are as numerous as the cacti dotting the Sonoran Desert, local officials considered the monitors partners in protecting the right to vote. But ahead of the primary election this past July, when Pima County Elections Director Constance Hargrove learned that DOJ was sending six representatives—most of whom were criminal prosecutors—she became worried. Would they just witness the vote, as in years past, or were they planning to gather evidence to later try to undermine the results? Would they know the basic rules? She told DOJ’s coordinator during a phone call: “Remind them that they cannot take a gun or weapon into the vote centers.”

On Primary Day, the monitors’ activities seemed normal, Hargrove told us. But she’s still concerned about the plan to send 1,000 of them to polling locations in every state for Tuesday’s election. The number itself is “a tall order,” Hargrove told us, as most previous administrations—Republican and Democratic—have detailed far fewer. And then there’s this department’s track record on elections. DOJ under President Trump has taken up his years-long quest to prove that the 2020 presidential election was stolen from him. Officials have seized ballots in Georgia, gathered material from Arizona, and questioned election officials in Wisconsin—but still have not found evidence of the widespread voter fraud the president has alleged. In recent days, DOJ reminded all state election officials to preserve materials from their elections.

“I’m not going to freak myself out before Election Day, or my poll workers, or the voters,” Hargrove said. She has reminded workers to follow their training should the monitors—or anyone else—be disruptive: “You can always call 911.”

Her concerns and suspicions are widely shared among the dozen-plus local and state election officials we spoke with across the country. Some worry that a long-standing program created during the civil-rights era to prevent intimidation, particularly of nonwhite voters, could be used as an extension of the administration’s attacks on the democratic process. The officials also said they fear that monitors could misinterpret what they see and then share incomplete or inaccurate information with DOJ that could be used to undermine the results.

Kiersten V. Pels, a Justice Department spokesperson, strenuously objected to these concerns in a written response to our questions about the program. Pels noted that the election officials we spoke with did not cite an example of “a single voter who was intimidated by a Justice Department monitor.” Should local officials see monitors acting improperly, Pels said, they should report them to DOJ.

“The Department has monitored elections for decades, under administrations of both parties. Monitors observe. They do not interfere with voting, and they follow state rules on polling place access,” Pels wrote. “Every monitor has the same role: to observe for potential violations of federal law and report concerns to the Department. The Department will review those concerns after Election Day and act where appropriate.”  

This year’s program is overseen by Harmeet Dhillon, the assistant attorney general for the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division, who has described monitors as a way “to increase voter confidence, to troubleshoot problems.” Dhillon’s critics have noted her ties to prominent election deniers and her support of legislation limiting mail-in voting. Her division, meanwhile, has sued dozens of state election officials, seeking—so far unsuccessfully—access to voter rolls containing partial Social Security numbers and other sensitive information. The suits are part of a nationwide search for noncitizens who might be illegally voting. “We see a problem, frankly, with some election officials refusing to clean up their voter rolls and knowingly allowing noncitizens, for example, to stay on the voter rolls,” she told Bloomberg. “This is a federal crime.” In a July post on X, Dhillon described the monitor program as aimed at ensuring equal access to the ballot, as it has in the past. But, she said, it will also help make sure that no American’s vote is “cancelled out by someone who shouldn’t be voting.” (Voting by noncitizens is extremely rare, analysis after analysis has found.) Pels, the DOJ spokesperson, told us that its monitors “will not be questioning voters about their citizenship status at the polls.” But Pels added that “if our monitors uncover evidence of such crimes while monitoring, we will use it.”

[Read: The election deniers’ long game]

The Justice Department has recruited criminal prosecutors to participate as monitors. Their inclusion overturns a longtime practice of not using those who have the power to charge people with crimes, and has raised questions about how the Justice Department might use their observations, particularly in places central to criminal investigations into the 2020 election, such as Arizona and Georgia. A former DOJ Civil Rights Division official, who requested anonymity to speak with us out of fear of professional retaliation, told us that previous administrations “never sent prosecutors, because we never wanted to do anything that might scare people.” Pels told us that monitors will not identify themselves as prosecutors.

Eric Neff, the acting chief of the Civil Rights Division’s Voting Section, said in a sworn declaration released as part of ongoing litigation that no FBI or ICE employees would serve as monitors. However, employees from the Federal Bureau of Prisons; the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives; and the Drug Enforcement Administration would be involved—a departure from long-held norms, three former DOJ attorneys told us. Pels said that their inclusion is “not new policy” and that they are “not being sent as law enforcement.”

To try to understand how the monitors might act on Tuesday, we looked at how they operated during the primaries. Although they watched without incident in many states, that wasn’t the case in deep-red Wyoming. On Primary Day in August, two monitors showed up at a Cheyenne polling place and asked the poll workers “highly unusual” questions that were outside their purview, including about how electronic poll books were acquired and whether they could see the tabulators, according to one local official, who spoke on the condition of anonymity because of safety concerns. Pels said the monitors’ questions were “routine” and disputed that they’d asked about the procurement of equipment. The local official told us that workers described the monitors as “very assertive,” and that they walked around “like they owned the place.” She added: “If you’re not expecting big, tall, beefy men, it could be rattling.”

Wyoming Governor Mark Gordon, a Republican, criticized the monitors’ activities at a public meeting, explaining that just as he doesn’t like the idea of the federal government “taking our guns,” he also dislikes the notion of it “taking our votes.” Pels told us that the Justice Department disagreed “with the public framing” that the monitors were “‘aggressive’ or did anything improper,” and said that Dhillon “had a cordial meeting” after the primary with the governor, the state attorney general, and a county election clerk.

[Read: The supporters urging Trump to declare martial law for the midterms]

We submitted roughly 200 public-records requests to states, counties, cities, and towns over the past several weeks, seeking to learn more about DOJ’s plan and the people carrying it out. Those records, along with our conversations with election officials, reflected that nearly all of the monitors’ visits during the primaries were professional. Some monitors asked poll workers about their training; some talked with voters outside polling locations. DOJ coordinated with local officials, advising when the monitors would arrive and what they could expect from them. A DOJ lawyer recently wrote to the lieutenant governor of Utah with an assurance that the program would run “the same way as in the past,” according to correspondence that we obtained. “Monitors follow all rules of the state and jurisdiction. Monitors dress in business casual clothing. No uniforms or pins identifying as DOJ are allowed.”

But a meeting a few days ago between DOJ and Arizona Secretary of State Adrian Fontes, a Democrat, reflected fears that this year’s election won’t be business as usual. During the conversation, which state officials told DOJ they were recording, Fontes asked why he should believe that the monitors are working in a nonpartisan manner, given the department’s recent lawsuit seeking to decertify Alaska’s Senate results if the Democratic candidate wins, according to audio of the meeting we reviewed. “It’s pretty patently obvious that a lawsuit filed based on a political outcome puts the department in a compromised position,” Fontes told the DOJ attorneys, who said they are sending roughly 20 monitors to the Phoenix area, plus more around the state. The DOJ officials replied that the monitors are, and would, remain nonpartisan. Fontes also asked if the monitors could be called to testify about their observations in court—as witnesses. Neff, the acting chief of DOJ’s Voting Section, responded that such a “speculative” scenario would be “assessed on a case-by-case basis.” Pels, the DOJ spokesperson, told us that this stance is “nothing new” and not newsworthy. “That’s how the law works,” she said.

States can decline to let the monitors into polling sites and instead require them to stand outside at a distance. Some red states have a history of doing so, such as Missouri and Florida, which banned monitors during the 2022 midterms. Hennepin County, in Minnesota, asked monitors to stay 100 feet away from one voting location during the August primary election and will do so again on Tuesday. One video in August captured a local election judge debating with two men dressed in polo shirts about how close they could get. “I’m assuming you don’t want to interfere with our ability to check for your compliance with federal law,” one of the monitors told the judge.

Most of the monitors’ names and assigned locations are not publicly known, but through our public-records requests, and from research conducted by Democracy Forward, a legal nonprofit, we identified some of the more than 80 monitors who were sent to polling locations during the primaries. Among the DOJ employees sent to monitor the primary were: Chris Gardner, who represented the Fulton County Republican Party in 2024 as it supported an election-board member who sued after refusing to certify the results of that year’s primary; Joseph Voiland, who represented the Trump campaign when it tried to overturn the 2020 election results in Wisconsin; and Alexander Keely, now a senior adviser to Dhillon, whose Republican National Lawyers Association member profile says he served as a “Trump 47 Election Integrity Captain” for a Pennsylvania county in 2024. DOJ did not respond to questions about whether these employees would serve as monitors on Tuesday, and Pels stressed in a statement that all monitors go through the same training.

In Arizona’s Maricopa County, local officials were alarmed when we described to them in July the biographies of some of the monitors assigned to their county for the primary, including a DOJ violent-crime-section chief, a federal prosecutor who specializes in public-corruption criminal probes, and a man who pleaded guilty in 2016 to crimes involving unauthorized computer intrusions after hacking two election websites in Florida. David Levin spent 20 days in prison for those crimes. Levin did not respond to our requests for comment, but he has previously claimed that he had been attempting to show the Florida county its own security vulnerabilities and called his actions “one of the bigger mistakes I’ve made in my life.”

[Read: The election system wasn’t built for this]

Records show that Maricopa County officials told DOJ last week they would not allow Levin to monitor their elections. The county attorney’s office, led by a Republican, also asked DOJ to commit in writing that it “will not send any election monitors to Maricopa County whom the DOJ knows has a felony conviction of any kind, or a conviction for any election-related criminal offense, in any state in the Union.”

Pels told us on Saturday that Levin is “no longer with the department” and “will not be a monitor on Election Day.” She said that DOJ hired and trained “qualified individuals to serve our country in a non-partisan manner” and that staff are expected to carry out their duties ethically and legally, “no exceptions.”

Internal DOJ materials we reviewed show that monitors have been trained to focus on voter-access laws—and not to interfere with the election. At least two longtime attorneys in the Civil Rights Division’s Voting Section are helping coordinate the monitors, emails show. Former DOJ officials told us their involvement, and that of other career attorneys who have prior experience with the program, makes them confident in it.

Although 1,000 monitors is an unusually high number, the country has close to 100,000 polling locations, and millions of people vote by mail in the weeks before Election Day. The deployment is unlikely “to actually change voters’ experiences in very many places,” Anna Baldwin, a former monitor who is now the voting-rights-litigation director at the Campaign Legal Center, told us.

Still, the election officials we spoke with said they have been fielding questions from staff, poll workers, fellow officials, and voters who are worried about the federal presence. One Boston official told us that time normally devoted to the nuts and bolts of preparing to run an election has been spent fielding questions from poll volunteers such as, What would you do if the federal government intervenes in your election in some way? Boston’s election administrators have developed new procedures, such as assigning workers to take an inventory of ballots, should federal agents seize them. In Wisconsin, election clerks have been “getting hundreds of emails” from voters and advocacy groups, asking what the plan is if federal law enforcement shows up, a state election official said at a public meeting.

Isaac Cramer, the executive director of the Charleston County Board of Elections, in South Carolina, said he’s worried that the monitors may intensify anxieties at the polls. “When you hear DOJ is coming,” he told us, “I think that can bring some fear. It can bring questions.” When monitors went to his county for a primary runoff this summer, false conspiracy theories immediately sprang up online.

Many city and county clerks told us that in all likelihood, the monitoring won’t interfere with their ability to fairly and accurately run the election. But the precautions they’ve had to take, and the questions they’ve had to answer, show how a program meant to protect the right to vote is being seen as a way to undermine it.

In the first months of Donald Trump’s first term, as one moral outrage followed another, I often found myself repeating, in conversation, a seven-word phrase: Where there’s no bottom, there’s no bottom. However bad things got, they were bound to get worse. That applied above all to Trump, but also to his party, which was fusing with him.

We’ve since seen endless examples of what “no bottom” looks like. The latest is an ad from Vivek Ramaswamy, who is running for governor of Ohio. It features the estranged mother of Ramaswamy’s opponent, Amy Acton, calling her daughter a liar about her childhood and urging Ohioans not to vote for her.

Here is what we know about that childhood. In 1978, when Acton was 12, her stepfather, Cledith Arthur, was indicted for raping her. He fled to Pennsylvania, where he died as a fugitive in 2004. Acton and her brother were removed from their mother’s custody, and she has not spoken with her mother since. Her mother, Donna Arthur, stayed married to the man charged with raping her daughter until he died.

The Ramaswamy campaign pledged to spend $4 million to air the ad on television and online. Some Republicans objected; Ohio’s current governor, Mike DeWine, called the ad “deeply, deeply troubling.” But the two most important Republicans in the country, Trump and Vice President Vance, defended it. Trump called it “very powerful” and wrote, “Who wouldn’t use such an Ad? I would, it’s called, ‘hitting pay dirt.’” Vance said he was “a little bit disgusted”—not by the ad but by the Republicans “whining” about it.

This is what family values look like to Republicans in the Trump era.

As a young conservative in the 1980s, among my formative influences were Gertrude Himmelfarb and Irving Kristol, Allan Bloom and Leon Kass, James Q. Wilson and William J. Bennett, Peter Berger and Richard John Neuhaus, and Michael Novak. I was especially shaped by George Will’s 1983 book, Statecraft as Soulcraft: What Government Does, which argued that statecraft is inherently “soulcraft,” the cultivation of public spirit and moral sensibilities. These thinkers shared the conviction that self-government depends on what Kristol called “republican virtue.”

They believed, too, that norms are fragile and that the membrane separating civilization from barbarism is thin. Character and conscience need to be cultivated. Public discourse either elevates or degrades, moving us away from or closer to a will-to-power ethic. The conduct of leaders, including political leaders, is educative. And when deviancy becomes widespread, we grow used to it. It was a Democrat, Daniel Patrick Moynihan, who coined the phrase defining deviancy down, by which he meant that a society overwhelmed by destructive behavior lowers its standards rather than confronting it.

None of this means that conservatives, or the Republican Party, were paragons of virtue. These thinkers and those who were influenced by their work often fell short of what they preached. But their aspirations were ennobling, and some of them lived lives that matched their words.

Today the American right is not merely indifferent to character formation; it is in the business of character deformation. It takes pride in its transgressiveness. The Ramaswamy ad embodies the “no bottom” ethic of the Trump-era GOP, and its wickedness comes in layers.

Cruelty and coarseness have momentum, but they also have limits. Political leaders who relentlessly push brutality do lower a nation’s standards, at least for a time. But if we’re fortunate, they also awaken its conscience. The revulsion comes first, almost physical, like bile rising in the throat. It is what Kass, in a different context, called “the wisdom of repugnance.”

The reckoning follows.

The anti-MAGA reckoning may have finally begun, though much later than it should have. Trump has lost most of the country. His hold on his own base is weakening; its members, like everyone else, are suffering because of the staggering incompetence of the president. And many of those who continue to support Trump are exhausted. In Ohio, a GOP poll shows that the Ramaswamy ad is backfiring. Axios reports that by a 2-to-1 ratio, those who said they were familiar with it said it made them more likely to vote for Acton.

But Ohio alone won’t be enough. A true repudiation of the politics of savagery will require a rebuke from voters in every region of America. It should come, and in a good society it would.

The Republican Party, after all, is led by a man found liable for sexually abusing a woman and then defaming her. The president has rallied behind an ad featuring a mother who, rather than stand by her 12-year-old daughter, sided with the man charged with raping the girl. Trump mocked the murder of one critic and openly cheered the death of another. He has called his political opponents “vermin,” said immigrants are “poisoning the blood of our country,” disparaged Haiti and African nations as “shithole countries,” and mocked a disabled reporter. His misogyny is undisguised; so is his bigotry. As president, he has enriched himself and his family by billions of dollars. He swoons over dictators while treating Canada as an enemy. He lies and distorts reality on a scale that was once unimaginable. He incited a violent attack on the Capitol in an effort to overturn an election. The list goes on. No American president has matched Trump’s limitless corruption or his cruelty.

The election, and especially the reelection, of Donald Trump caused me, for the first time in my life, to question the fundamental decency of America. I could not understand how anyone, especially my co-religionists, could look at such a squalid figure and vote for him, let alone support him enthusiastically. How could they defend his indecency time after time after time? Doesn’t partisanship have limits? Don’t honor and integrity matter? Isn’t Trump’s peculiar form of madness an obvious disqualifier?

For tens of millions of Americans—more than 77 million in 2024 alone—the answer has been no. That stain is now a permanent part of the American story. But the story isn’t over. We can move beyond this grotesque chapter to something better, though the path won’t be smooth. Even if Democrats sweep the midterms, Trump will still be president, and his closing act will likely involve trying to tear the country apart. We can’t allow him to do that. We need to get to the other side of this era with our institutions, our bonds to one another, and our commitment to justice and the rule of law intact, and then begin the work of reclaiming American ideals. A wipeout of the party of Trump on Tuesday would be an awfully helpful step in that direction.
