Today’s AI agents have become exceptional hackers, and they’re already posing a threat to some of the most important infrastructure in modern life: banks.

In both finance and tech, industry leaders are well aware of the dangers, and they’re gearing up for a fight. Earlier this year, as Anthropic prepared to release a powerful new model called Mythos, the firm gave an early preview of the AI to a select group of companies, aiming to help them shore up their defenses. JPMorganChase was on the list, and more banks were reportedly added as Anthropic expanded the program. After the rollout, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and then–Fed Chair Jerome Powell met with the heads of Bank of America, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, and other major financial institutions to discuss the potential implications of the new model, which Anthropic claimed could “surpass all but the most skilled humans at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities.”

What makes AI agents different from past cybersecurity threats is that they move at machine speeds and can be summoned in groups. These bots can attack in swarms, hunting for cracks in companies’ back-end systems. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, which oversees many U.S. banks, wrote in a report to Congress this summer that AI has increased the “speed, scale, and sophistication of cyber-attacks targeting financial institutions.”

Research from last year suggests that finance is likelier than other industries to face AI-enabled cyberattacks, but we can already see these threats playing out in the tech sector. Tech companies typically send out weekly software patches—updates and bug fixes, essentially—on what’s known as Patch Tuesday. In the age of agentic threats, the volume of those updates has ballooned. Microsoft patched nearly 1,000 issues earlier this month, an all-time record. The trend was accelerating before Mythos arrived, but, per J.P. Morgan’s analysis, the most advanced models have dramatically changed the cyber-risk landscape, leading to a “tsunami of patches.” Insiders have developed a word for it: “Patchmaggedon.”

There’s also the risk that AI companies might lose control of the agents they send out into the world. Earlier this year, OpenAI revealed that its agents had defied instructions, breaching containment and hacking the servers of a company called Hugging Face. They also stole employee data and conspired to hide the evidence from their human managers. It was an incident that “exposed the speed of AI’s development,” as my colleague Josh Tyrangiel noted, “but also the lax oversight of the humans supposedly keeping tabs on it.” OpenAI has since disclosed several other lapses, and similar AI hacking incidents have occurred at Google, Anthropic, and Meta.

What might happen if AI agents manage to identify substantial weaknesses in a bank’s code base? It’s hard to say with any certainty. But because banks underpin nearly every aspect of modern life, outages or disruptions to back-end systems could ripple across digital commerce. That some of the dangers associated with frontier AI are inherently unknowable can make preparing difficult. The companies building these tools don’t always understand the extent of a model’s capabilities until it’s been fully trained—OpenAI’s chief scientist has written that “AI is grown more than designed.”

In spite of all this, the cybersecurity experts I spoke with made clear that the financial-services industry isn’t panicking. That’s in part because these agents and frontier models can also be used to prevent cyberattacks. Today’s engineers understand that defending against machine intelligence might itself require machine intelligence; firms are already deploying these tools for fraud detection, risk management, and more. Mike Silverman, the head of strategy and innovation at the Financial Services Information Sharing and Analysis Center, told me that companies are using AI to beef up what’s known as anomaly detection—early-warning systems for potential threats—as well as other kinds of data monitoring. A cybersecurity executive at one of the largest banks in the United States told me that the industry’s Security Operations Centers—defense hubs traditionally managed by human beings—are exploring the possibility of developing their own agents, and that engineers across the sector are now designing systems with AI’s capabilities in mind.

Because the financial sector has long been a target of scams, frauds, and data breaches, it’s already on high alert. But not every institution is equally capable of handling new threats; some smaller banks may lack the resources and security expertise of larger firms. As AI models advance, these tools may expose the difference between those who can afford to prepare for what’s coming, and those who can’t.

Why I’m Sending My Black Son to Private School

By Jenisha Watts

Since before he could even talk, I’ve been worrying about where to send my son to school. I know that having a choice to make at all is a mark of status: I am part of a group of parents who have enough money, time, and education to decide what is best for our children. And the fact that I can make a choice means that I can make the wrong one. So whenever I meet an educated Black parent, I inevitably ask for their advice about school. I remember one person answering, “We took the Nikole Hannah-Jones approach.”

I knew exactly what he meant. A decade ago, I read the cover story Nikole wrote for The New York Times Magazine about her and her husband’s struggle to decide where to send their daughter, Najya, to school. They ended up putting her in a “high-poverty, all-black school” because they believed that middle-class families had a responsibility to such neglected institutions …

I have always admired Nikole’s writing, and over the years, she has become a confidant and friend. Although many on the internet have accused Nikole of sacrificing her daughter for her ideals, I think she’s courageous. Still, as my son approaches school age, I realize that I could never make the choice she made.

China Has No Use for AI Doomerism

AI is everywhere in China. In Hangzhou, humanoid police robots wearing fluorescent-yellow vests patrol tourist hot spots, gently admonishing jaywalkers. The Chinese AI chatbot Doubao is such a popular source of medical advice that citizens have begun to call the service “Doctor Doubao.” The authoritarian country is marshaling the state’s resources behind building a world-leading AI industry and pushing for the technology to be adopted across crucial sectors, including manufacturing, energy, and agriculture. Meanwhile, leaders are calling for a nationwide effort to nurture AI breakthroughs, adopting Cold War language last used when the country sought to develop a nuclear weapon.

Encouraged by a steady drumbeat of state propaganda, the Chinese population has largely come to see AI, like previous waves of digital disruption, as a means of easing everyday life while also creating new jobs and industries. Polls indicate that the country is among the nations most excited about the prospects of AI, and doomers are tough to find.

[Read: What AI anxiety looks like in China]

So when the American tech sector reached a new level of AI panic last week, across the Pacific Ocean, the Chinese AI community largely shrugged—including the very people working on the technology’s safety.

Chinese engineers and AI-safety experts I spoke with told me that although they recognize the existential safety risks of AI, they feel that losing control of the technology is a distinctly American challenge—for now. They’re far more preoccupied with catching up to U.S. AI models, which, despite the best efforts of Chinese companies such as DeepSeek and Moonshot AI, continue to lead in overall benchmarks. With the United States and China in a race to develop the most cutting-edge technologies, many in China also saw Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s proposal calling for an intentional slowdown in AI development as self-serving and driven by the geopolitical rivalry between the two countries.

Amodei’s post, which fueled the AI panic across Silicon Valley, capped a whirlwind two months of cybersecurity breaches by agentic models from top U.S. and Chinese labs. It also attracted rare consensus from Amodei’s biggest rivals—OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and the SpaceXAI founder Elon Musk—and ignited a debate among industry executives, national-security hawks, and lawmakers. President Trump dismissed the warnings as a “SICK conspiracy,” while Republicans and Democrats clashed over proposed regulation.

In China, the loudest voices commenting on the AI panic have come from state media and nationalist news commentators, who framed the outrage as just another ploy to delay China’s AI progress. Global Times, a state-owned media outlet run by the Chinese Communist Party, called the Anthropic executive’s warnings part of a “Cold War playbook” in a global AI race and singled out Amodei’s call for greater export controls to keep advanced chips out of China’s hands. “From the Chinese perspective, Dario’s proposal that China ‘pace the frontier’ seems hypocritical,” Kwan Yee Ng, an analyst at Concordia AI, a Beijing-based consultancy focused on AI safety, told me. “The country views itself as already being held back by the very export controls Amodei endorses.”

The Chinese technology giant Huawei’s rotating chairperson, Xu Zhijun, told reporters that Chinese AI might not be powerful enough to encounter the same type of catastrophic safety risks that U.S. companies fear. Xu’s view is commonly held within the industry. One Chinese AI-software entrepreneur, who asked for anonymity because of the sensitive nature of the topic, told me that although cybersecurity is a constant concern, businesses are less concerned about losing control of AI models. “It’s so competitive here, and everyone is focused on bettering their own product. No one has the time to dwell on those issues,” he said. China is still playing catch-up, he added. If anyone’s going to lose control of AI first, he claimed, it will be the Americans.

Another Chinese developer I spoke with who also asked for anonymity in order to speak openly, echoed the broader sense of AI optimism within China. A Stanford University industry report published this year cited polls showing that 84 percent of Chinese respondents said products and services that use AI made them excited. That’s compared with 53 percent of the global average, and 38 percent of people in the U.S. The developer told me that many of his peers tended to be “tech optimists” and didn’t subscribe to the same timelines for artificial general intelligence as Silicon Valley. Amodei’s warnings on AI misalignment are valuable insider signals, he said, but without more empirical evidence, it’s hard to get worked up.

Part of the dismissive attitude is driven, it seems, by a broadly held suspicion of Amodei. The Anthropic CEO has repeatedly pushed for tight export controls to hold back China’s AI development and lobbied U.S. policy makers to clamp down on “unauthorized distillation” practices by major Chinese labs. That’s made him a controversial figure in the AI-developer community in China, despite the fact that Claude Code is widely used in the industry there. Last week, the DeepSeek engineer Liu Shengyu went viral on Chinese social media for a post that called on frontier AI to remain open and affordable, while also taking a personal shot at Amodei’s company. The prospect of Anthropic monopolizing the most advanced AI or AGI, Liu argued, was as severe as “Hitler acquiring atomic weapons before the Allies.” (Liu has since clarified that his views were personal and not representative of DeepSeek.)

Another reason for the subdued reaction in China has to do with how AI companies there are structured. For the most part, they tend to be leaner and more resource-constrained than their U.S counterparts, which have teams devoted to long-term alignment and AI safety. To the extent that they have AI-safety resources, Chinese teams devote them to satisfying regulatory requirements, such as data security and filtering politically sensitive material. “The dynamics of AI safety is different in the U.S. and China,” Zilan Qian, a research scholar at GovAI, a think tank focused on helping governments navigate advanced AI, told me. “In the U.S., the labs and companies mainly drive the AI-safety discussion. In China, the government plays a central role in setting the AI-safety agenda and putting out regulation. Then the tech companies comply.”

That’s not to say Chinese officials are completely unaware of AI risks. In recent months, multiple top Chinese politicians have sounded the alarm. Last week, Chen Yixin, the head of China’s state security agency, wrote in a public letter that AI was a serious threat to regime stability and required tighter scrutiny and control. And in July, President Xi Jinping warned about the risks of AI and pushed for multilateral cooperation in AI governance. China’s cyberspace regulator recently released an annual update of its AI Safety Governance Framework to include a hefty section about the risks from autonomous agents and the increased capabilities of frontier models.

For all of the problems associated with America’s laissez-faire approach, China illustrates the opposite concern. Chinese officials worried about misinformation and losing control of the public discourse introduced some of the world’s tightest restrictions on the nascent technology. Generative-AI models in China are trained on data aligned with the party’s political ideology to ensure that the answers generated hold its worldview. China has also harnessed AI to create the world’s largest digital police state, with millions of surveillance cameras mounted on streets scanning faces or analyzing citizens’ every move.

Compare that with the U.S., where both the Trump administration and prominent Silicon Valley investors and businessmen have pushed against the notion of a comprehensive federal AI law. As a result, various states and agencies have filled the gap with laws to govern the use of the technology, including rules covering deepfakes, data-governance statutes, and algorithms that dictate health-care decisions.

Misalignment and loss of control of AI might not be a concern in China now, but in six months to a year, things could shift, Malo Bourgon, the head of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, told me. As China catches up, the country’s tech scene might face similar moments of panic.

In the meantime, Trump and Xi will meet this week in Washington for talks on topics including AI. A notification system for AI safety between the two tech superpowers has been proposed. Ultimately, the geopolitical and economic competition between the U.S. and China means that each side is looking at the other with suspicion. It’s unlikely the two of them will agree on much, other than a belief that no slowdown is needed.

Food is like dance: a language, but not one you’d want to use in an emergency. Food can say I love you or Congratulations (it helps if you are able to use icing on top of the food to make its message clearer) or even something slightly more subtle: I am proud of the seafood offerings of my region. It cannot say Look out! There is a projectile flying at your head! or Please stop trying to invade Greenland. Or can it?

I went to “Taste of the Arctic” last week at the Danish ambassador’s residence to find out. The evening, billed as a celebration of “the culinary heritage and enduring cultural ties connecting Alaska, Greenland, and Denmark across the Arctic and North Atlantic,” came the week before the signing of an agreement about the U.S.’s presence in Greenland, though I am not sure I would have been able to tell you any details of the agreement being hashed out based on the available cuisine.

The food was made by two chefs, Miki Siegsted and Joaquin Guzmán, representing Greenland and Denmark respectively. Guzmán, from Argentina, is the head chef of a Danish restaurant that cooks exclusively with Danish ingredients, meaning that if he wants chocolate, he cannot have chocolate. He has to engineer something like chocolate himself by using the by-products of beer brewing, which he reports have a delicious, chocolate-like flavor. Siegsted makes a point of introducing Greenlandic cuisine to a wider world, and is serving us reindeer sausage! (“Poor Rudolph,” says the waiter passing it around.)

Fortunately, speeches let us know what the food is trying to tell us. “Food is one of the most honest languages we have,” Danish Ambassador Jesper Møller Sørensen says. “It tells you about a place, its water, its weather, and its people better than any briefing note could do.” He adds that “we have a very constructive dialogue with our American friends to achieve a framework that addresses the real security concerns that the United States has, that we share. These are serious conversations. But they are made easier and more human when we have them over a shared meal, rather than across a conference table.” Maybe we will find a little security framework in our salmon?

Jacob Isbosethsen, of the Greenland delegation, says, “From hunting polar bears, reindeer, musk ox, whales, walruses, and seals to catching fish and birds, our relationship with nature has always been deeply connected to our way of life.” However, the chefs will not be serving “most of the proteins or animals I mentioned earlier.” But we will still get to enjoy other arctic delights, the product of “56,000 people on the world’s largest island” with the “most modern shrimp-fishing fleet in the Arctic.”

Senator Lisa Murkowski acknowledges that “some will say, well, I don’t know, when I think about the Arctic, I don’t think about really exotic or great foods or Michelin-starred restaurants,” but she is not included in that misguided “some.” “When you begin with honest, incredible seafood that comes from these cold waters, you can make just about anything fabulous.”

The speeches also have the effect of postponing any access to these flavors until almost 8 p.m. Having made the unacceptable rookie mistake of thinking that, at an evening event at an embassy themed around food, there would be enough food for dinner, served at dinnertime, I am quite hungry and (after a Hygge In a Can) perhaps excessively convivial. (This shows how little time I spend going to Events at Embassies, which is why I never know what is Really Happening in Washington.) I post myself in front of the two tasting stations like a ravenous anteater squatting outside an anthill, or, to use a more arctic animal, a polar bear resting on an ice floe. I do such a good job of this that I entirely deny a man access to reindeer sausage!

The parade of arctic food runs the gamut from one seafood to another seafood to the first seafood again, but fermented this time. The seafood is fresh and delicious! The reindeer sausage is maybe the best sausage I’ve ever eaten in my life? It tastes like a Slim Jim, if that Slim Jim were gourmet and assembled by Santa under desperate circumstances.

I should preface all of my descriptions of food by saying: Once, I had the enormous privilege of getting to visit Sicily with my in-laws. We went to a very fine pizza restaurant. The waiter brought me the hot, pillowycrusted pizza with melted cheese and mouth-watering sausage on board (“on board”? Already my descriptive powers are flagging) and set it in front of me. It was incredible. I devoured several slices. “This tastes like DiGiorno!” I exclaimed. I was trying to compliment the pizza, but my in-laws were horrified. This is why I am a humor columnist and not a food critic.

All right, now that you are warned, on to Taste of the Arctic.

My favorite taste of the Arctic was the reindeer sausage, by a mile. I must have eaten five. There was a mustard sauce on top, and the sausage engaged in a sort of dialogue with it over the course of the bite (I am trying to describe the food! How do you think it’s going so far?), saying things like, I am spicy! to which the sausage responded, You think you are the only thing that is spicy? I, too, am spicy! Diplomatically, I have no idea how this translates.

There was a tasty, fresh shrimp. It arrived in a little boat, as though it were ready to row out in Greenland’s defense, should it be called to.

I also very much liked Lox on a Cucumber. It tasted like lox on a cucumber, but with, I think, a hint of citrus?

There was also something called a cod-and-salmon ballotine, where cod (white) and salmon (pink) were curled together into a small dumpling and garlanded with a green sauce so that it looked like an edible Italian flag. Unclear what the diplomatic significance of this was. Should Italy be more involved in the Arctic? This tasted like salty mashed potatoes, which is a compliment.

I also tried salmon with salmon eggs on top, and a sort of brown crumble. A WHOLE FISH FAMILY PERISHED AT MY BEHEST! And I would do it again. I wrote in my notes that there was “guacamole” on this, but I think I was mistaken. Maybe it was the Danish equivalent of guacamole, made from lingonberry leaves or something.

The one thing I’ll say about the food at Taste of the Arctic is that there was not enough of it. These dishes were all bite-size, the kind of thing a mouse would lug home to his family, who would then say, in a disappointed tone, “But you said you were bringing dinner for all of us.” At one point, I perched forlornly at the edge of the tasting tables. Their white, empty tablecloths with no food on them evoked the arctic tundra, frozen, empty of food for the winter. Perhaps deliberately?

The strange thing about this event was not the food, which was delicious. Or the Hygge-inspired beer, which was excellent. Or the fact that the Danish ambassador’s residence was hospitable and full of bicycles, which it also was. What was odd was that we were in a geopolitical situation where this diplomacy via cod and reindeer innards felt warranted at all. What was odd were those harrowing months when it looked as though the president wanted to annex Greenland, destroying our relationship with Denmark and ending the post–World War II international liberal order.

If your president goes rogue and decides that he wants to annex Greenland because the Mercator projection makes it look impressively big, or because he thinks the United States is not cold enough on average (I guess that’s one solution to climate change!), it is not the fault of the honest, incredible seafood. But when the going gets tough, the tough serve shellfish.

As of yesterday, there is a deal. Denmark and Greenland are both party to it, and it will allow us to build bases there, which we already do, and have “undersea access,” which, based on the tastiness of the cod, is important.

Perhaps I should have known from this food that a deal was coming. If food were trying to say something like Keep out! We are enemies now! it would have tasted different. There would have been small glass shards in the salmon, perhaps. If food is a language, I think this food was trying to say, If you don’t like fish very much, you should leave us well enough alone.

It is good, I think, that the treaty was made with words instead.

The Makings of a Prewar World

Donald Trump offered perhaps the clearest summation yet of his worldview in an otherwise discursive address to the United Nations General Assembly yesterday. Defending the American takeover of Venezuelan oil, he invoked an old maxim: “To the victor belongs the spoils.” He then presented Iran with a choice between accepting his terms and being driven “into hell with no chance of survival.”

These remarks are the latest sign that world politics is entering an era when might makes right. Yet Trump is only one of the leaders driving this rupture from the post–World War II international order. Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping have also broken the order, although their methods differ profoundly—morally, legally, and otherwise. American policy may change when Trump leaves office in January 2029, but Putin and Xi may remain in power well into the next decade.

A new American president will likely not be able to restore the old order or create a better one, even if they want to. Their greatest challenge will be to arrest the slide.

International order is a notoriously vague and politically contested concept. Many leaders in the global South, along with Western critics, reject the idea that the United States ever consistently abided by a rules-based system. They point to Western military interventions, double standards, and the unequal distribution of power within international institutions. But the old order’s failures do not mean that its restraints were meaningless.

In his first book, A World Restored, Henry Kissinger argued that a stable and legitimate order rests on a limited consensus: an understanding among the major powers about what he called the permissible aims and methods of foreign policy. Governments can compete intensely and violate rules without seeking to overturn the system itself. The danger arises when a major power becomes so dissatisfied with the limits imposed upon it that it adopts a revolutionary foreign policy.

[Vivian Salama: America’s allies are no longer waiting for Trump to leave]

Under the old international order, major powers did not, as a general rule, conquer and annex the territory of their neighbors. The global trading system was based on reciprocity, tariffs were historically low, and markets were relatively open. And, although it was the world’s strongest country, the United States exercised enough self-discipline to allow its allies to grow into genuine partners, not vassals to be constantly squeezed for concessions.

Over the past decade, and decisively since the pandemic, that consensus about permissible conduct has collapsed. Putin launched the largest war in Europe since World War II with his invasion of Ukraine in 2022. He more than anyone has revived the idea that countries can expand their borders through the use of force. Combined Russian- and Ukrainian-military casualties (those killed, wounded, and missing) have reached approximately 2 million people. European governments have accused Russia of an expanding campaign of sabotage, cyberattacks, arson, and drone incursions below the threshold of conventional war. North Korea has intervened directly in this war, and many other nations are indirectly involved—China and Iran on the side of Russia; Europe, the United States (at least under Joe Biden), and Asian democracies on the side of Ukraine.

Under Xi, China has broken the global trading system. Xi has used the power of the Chinese state to establish dominance in advanced manufacturing, reduce China’s imports as much as possible, and aggressively grow its exports. The result is that China has enormous excess capacity. Its goods flood into the rest of the world at prices impossible to compete with, leaving other countries to choose between deindustrialization and protectionism. More and more of them are selecting the latter. This policy is not as morally egregious as Putin’s invasion of Ukraine, but it has toppled a key pillar of the international economic order.

Since taking office for a second term, in 2025, Trump has turned American power against U.S. allies and partners. He imposed the highest tariffs in nearly a century on Canada, Japan, South Korea, India, and the European Union and used the threat of more to extract hundreds of billions of dollars in investment pledges. He mused about making Canada the 51st state and seizing Greenland. He pushed Ukraine to sign over a share of its mineral wealth in the middle of a war for its national survival. Under Trump, the United States sees itself not as the guarantor of an alliance system of like-minded democracies, but as a dominant power that wields its influence for singular, transactional gain.

Trump has broken the restraint that made American primacy tolerable to most of the rest of the world. Reports suggest that he may meet Xi and Putin for a “big three” trilateral on the margins of the Asia Pacific Economic Summit in Shenzhen in November, which could mark the formal end of the old order.

The rupture has produced an international environment that feels ever more like that of a prewar period. This does not mean that a major conflict is inevitable. But if one occurs, future historians will not find its causes sudden or mysterious. They are in plain sight.

[Listen: Trump battles against an economy of his making]

The major powers are building up their militaries and racing to master drone warfare and artificial intelligence. Trump’s lack of commitment to alliances, especially in Europe, raises the prospect that an aggressor may calculate that it can act without triggering much of a U.S. reaction. Meanwhile, America’s rivals are more and more aligned as they integrate their military production, share sensitive technology, and assist one another in battle.

The shift has generational and psychological drivers. Putin, Xi, and Trump are elderly, sometimes erratic, insulated in information bubbles, and animated by ideological visions of national revival. The moment may feel like a closing window—a now-or-never opportunity to remake the world while they still can.

A future American president may promise to build a new and improved international order. But Xi and Putin will have little reason to accept the restraints that Washington wants to restore, and although American allies may welcome a return to a more internationalist U.S. foreign policy, they will harbor doubts that it will survive the next election.

Rather than promising what they cannot deliver, aspiring presidents should level with the American people. The international order has come to an end. The next president will inherit one of the most unforgiving international environments since 1945. Their task will be to stop the deterioration, inject some stability into world affairs, and begin to lay the foundation for a better future, even if that is not achievable in the short term.

When CNN’s Kaitlan Collins approached President Trump with a question about the Iran war at the United Nations General Assembly yesterday, the president scolded her for being there at all. “I’m surprised that CNN is here covering me,” Trump said, adding, “You should not be here covering me.”

Presumably, he said this because CNN is among the three outlets, along with MS NOW and Politico, that Trump has barred from White House grounds because he didn’t like their coverage. But, to state the obvious, the United Nations is not the White House. If a journalist is denied a press pass from one entity, she’s not precluded from getting credentialed by another. As Collins said to Trump, “The United Nations allowed us in, sir.”

The exchange highlights Trump’s warped understanding of journalism—in which stories are either good or bad, reporters are “friendly” or “terrible,” and a president decides who covers him and who doesn’t. “There’s something wrong with a country that can allow people to write purposely negative stories,” he said when he banned CNN and the other two outlets. “Now, if they want to write them, that’s fine. But I don’t have to let them into my—into the people’s house.”

As Trump noted when he corrected himself, the White House serves the American people—but so does the press. Since the days of FDR, a cadre of rotating print, TV, and radio reporters have traveled with the president wherever he goes, including on weekends and holidays, to provide an unbroken and independent record of everything the president says and does in public. This press pool exists because not every reporter can constantly trail the president; neither can every citizen. Political reporters stand in for people who want to know what their most important elected representative is doing, but who have day jobs.

The importance of the pool became especially visible this week when, in solidarity, the other four networks that typically share pool duties with CNN—ABC, CBS, Fox News, and NBC—suspended their pool coverage of the president. This led to an unintentionally Veep-like moment during Trump’s unveiling of his new helipad, when no networks provided audio as Trump appeared flanked by smiling construction workers, speaking without sound. (A Newsmax host gingerly described the scene as a sign that the CNN ban was perhaps “backfiring just a little bit.”)

Trump has a long history with the press—and of seeming not to understand how it works. (As with many things pertaining to the president, however, determining whether his ignorance is genuine or strategic can be difficult.) At times, he has appeared to feign confusion in an effort to get reporters to do what he wants. In a 1997 New Yorker profile, for instance, Trump repeatedly told the reporter following him that his words were “off the record, but you can use it”—guidance that makes no sense, because “off the record” is an agreement that means a reporter can’t use a piece of information in a story. When he was campaigning for president in 2016, Trump frequently railed against “libel laws” and promised to “loosen them up” but struggled to describe what that meant. (“I’d have to get my lawyers in to tell you,” he told The Washington Post.) He suggested that late-night comedians should devote “equal time” to jokes about Democrats and his administration, seemingly invoking a law that doesn’t apply to either presidents or late-night jokes.

A fact that seems to bedevil Trump most is that the press doesn’t take orders from him, and that therefore almost every reporter he encounters will at some point seem “unfriendly.” In his current term, one of Trump’s first orders of business was to decree that the White House would choose the reporters in the press pool, breaking with the tradition of news organizations choosing. He’s trying to ban the Associated Press from parts of the White House because the wire service continues to refer, against Trump’s wishes, to the body of water south of the United States as “the Gulf of Mexico.” Trump regularly interrogates pool reporters about which network they’re really with, and dubs the answer “fake news” if it’s not one that he likes. As evidenced by the exchange at the United Nations, CNN’s Collins seems to provoke his disdain to an unusual degree: Over the years, Trump has attempted to bar her from White House press events, tried to make her sit in the back of the press room, called her “stupid” and “nasty,” implored her to smile, and told her she should be ashamed of herself because she used to be “a conservative from Alabama.” To which Collins replied, “I’m still from Alabama, sir.”

Presidents have always had testy relations with the press, of course, and have tried to minimize negative coverage. The behavior of Trump’s administration, however, often shades into something more sinister. His FBI raided the home of a Washington Post reporter whose coverage he found objectionable, and Trump has sued and defunded news organizations whose coverage he deemed biased. MS NOW reported in May that the FBI had opened a criminal investigation focusing on my colleague Sarah Fitzpatrick, related to an article she wrote about FBI Director Kash Patel. (The FBI has denied the existence of such an investigation.)

As a reporter, I spend a lot of time explaining to sources—many of whom have never interacted with the media—the basics of journalism: that we don’t accept or grant payment for interviews, that reporters don’t interact with advertisers, and that most mainstream outlets pride themselves on not affiliating with any given political ideology. Some degree of media illiteracy is understandable at a time when thousands of local newspapers have closed, and when influencers—some of whom secretly accept payment for their branded videos—compete for attention on social media with journalists reporting the truth. Journalists shouldn’t have to explain their jobs, though, to someone who has lived his entire adult life in the public eye, and who is now America’s most important public servant. But as Collins showed at the UN, apparently, they must.
