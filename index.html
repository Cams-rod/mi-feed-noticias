In the weeks heading into next week’s midterm election, President Trump has been crisscrossing the country trying to gin up enthusiasm among his base at rallies—where he’s gone on Islamophobic rants and inexplicably praised a candidate’s backside. Meanwhile, spending on races across the country has been through the roof: His super PAC has been pumping hundreds of millions into campaigns to help vulnerable Republicans, as has Elon Musk; and Mike Bloomberg is spending tens of millions to help Democrats. It’s a sprint to the first Tuesday in November. .

But enthusiasm is not really the word to describe most Americans’ sentiments heading into this election. Economic concerns about the price of goods such as gas and groceries, an unpopular war with Iran that has driven that rise in everyday costs, and angst from Democrats about an unchecked Trump administration is fueling the conversation ahead of Election Day.

This week on Radio Atlantic, my colleagues Ashley Parker and Michael Scherer join me to discuss the closing messages from Democrats and Republicans, the campaign spending blitz, and the issues voters will be taking with them to the polls.

The following is a transcript of the episode:

Ashley Parker: You may recall, one of the key things Donald Trump promised was no more forever wars, no more quagmires in the Middle East, et cetera, et cetera. And the war in Iran, which people already don’t like on its merit, is so deeply tied with gas prices in a way that people can make that one-to-one connection. So it’s just a nest of what everyone hates.

Adam Harris: This is Radio Atlantic, I’m Adam Harris, and we’re a week away from the midterm elections. The stakes have been clear for a while: The election will determine whether President Trump spends his last two years in office with a Congress that continues to allow him to enact his policy agenda with few, if any, checks—or as a lame duck.

The president has been zigzagging the country trying to excite his base, as early-voting numbers are already surging well ahead of 2018 and 2022 midterm cycles.

My colleagues Ashley Parker and Michael Scherer are with me to discuss the closing messages for Republicans and Democrats, where they’re spending in the last week, and how voters are responding.

Let’s get to questions. Welcome back.

Ashley Parker: Thank you.

Michael Scherer: Yeah, thanks for having me.

Harris: Ashley, I want to start with the news that the Justice Department sued to block Senate results if Republicans lose in Alaska. What do we know about what’s happening there?

Parker: In Alaska, you have this amusing situation, where you have two candidates named Dan Sullivan. One is the Republican incumbent, and one is an independent. And Republicans and the Justice Department are trying to say that the results may be invalid because of confusion between the two names.

They wanna make sure that someone who goes into the voting booth and votes for Dan Sullivan, the independent, actually meant to vote for him and not Dan Sullivan, the Republican. But my understanding, from what legal experts have said, is that this is a ridiculous case that almost certainly will not hold up in court.

Scherer: But it does serve the purpose of getting this in the news in Alaska, which is—I think the goal of the case is to elevate it, to make people recognize again, Wait, there are two Dan Sullivans. I gotta figure out which is the right one.

It’s also interesting there are races across the country where both parties have been trying to create new parties to get fourth candidates on the ballot, or to take over the Green Party ticket in places to bleed off voters. So that trickery is advancing.

Harris: And in Alaska, it’s particularly interesting because it’s ranked-choice voting. So how is that factoring in?

Scherer: There’s a lot of Republican education going on now to try and convince people not to put Mary Peltola, the Democrat, as the second person anywhere. So they’re trying to tell their voters, Just do the real Dan Sullivan, one, and then leave the rest blank, because they’re worried on subsequent ballots she’d pull ahead.

Harris: There’s been haze over the election that the president might try to contest results elsewhere if Democrats were successful. And I wonder: Is this attempt from the DOJ the sort of foreshadowing of the possibility of the administration contesting the election in other states if things don’t go their way?

Parker: That was a point I was gonna make. Michael is right that part of this lawsuit—that, I think, any DOJ lawyer worth their salt knows is not likely to advance or be successful—is helping Republicans in Alaska understand that there is one Republican, Dan Sullivan, and that’s who they should vote for. That’s valuable for Republicans.

The flip side is, as you point out, there is a tremendous angst going back to January 6, where Democrats and a lot of the electorate is terrified that Donald Trump and his team will not accept the results of a free and fair election. You talk to the reasonable people in Trump’s inner circle, and they say—and Michael did a great story on this that he can talk about—they say, Yes, there is a contingent in the president’s ear trying to get him to declare martial law, but that’s a very tiny contingent. That’s not what’s gonna happen. But I do think the concern is this shows, for instance, that the Justice Department is willing to go forward with something that is objectively, on its face, according to lawyers, absurd, because the president wants them to do it. Which fairly raises the question: What else might they be willing to do simply because the president wants them to do it?

Scherer: I think there’s two categories of things, and the problem is you can’t easily define them ahead of time. One is: You very often have litigation after elections. And so I think there will be races that are close; there will be races where lawsuits are filed, where the DOJ might intervene. But I think the American people can rest assured, at least at the moment—the courts have been almost unanimously rejecting the president’s efforts to intervene in this election.

He’s tried five or 10 different ways—stripping voter rolls, banning mail-in ballots, all kinds of things. And the courts have just struck them down consistently. And so I think it’s likely that the litigation that follows a close race will be the way litigations follow[ing] close races have been in the past.

Now, the concern that Ashley mentioned is this other idea that the president tries to do something that’s never been done before, which is to declare, say, The election machines are fraudulent and we can’t trust the vote; or to say, We have to seize these ballots; or even the martial-law idea.

Another concern is that Republicans refuse because of a dispute to seat the winner of a race. So you could have an effort that would have to be litigated and fought, where Republicans try and prevent a Democrat who was elected from taking power next year. And that would be a mess.

That said, I think it’s unlikely that that second category of things goes forward right now. And the reason is that there’s very little appetite in the Republican Party, and there’s very little appetite in the senior ranks of the White House. So to do it, the president would have to basically overrule his own advisers and pick a fight with the Republican Party in a way that he probably comes out worse from, not better. And the bottom line is he’s been expecting to lose this race since the beginning of the year. So it’s not like it was in 2020, where his entire reputation, his entire legacy, was on the line.

Parker: Right. And I was gonna say, he might be willing—that’s the sort of thing he was clearly, in 2020, willing to do on behalf of himself to try to stay in office for four more years. I don’t know that he’s going to expend that sort of reputational capital for a random House Republican.

Scherer: Although, the other thing we should say is it’s certain that, after this election, he’s gonna continue to demand changes to the voting system, and he’s gonna continue to launch investigations into the voting system.

And so the noise around this is gonna continue. He wants the voting system we have now changed. And he basically wasn’t able to do it before the midterms, but he has another window here to try and do it before the next presidential.

Harris: Michael, I want to talk about the spending. Trump’s super PAC has spent nearly a quarter-billion dollars to help Republicans this election cycle. What do we know about where the president is spending now in this last week, and why is he spending in those spaces?

Scherer: So the president, he claims that he will spend $400 million, and that includes some money—it’s on his C4, his dark-money side; we don’t have easy ways of tracking it when all is said and done—which is an enormous amount of money. This is basically additive money that didn’t exist in other cycles. Presidents don’t usually raise money separately to do this.

And so the result has been, in the key Senate races around the country, something like 30 or 40 percent more spending on the Republican side than on the Democratic side, all-in. Now, that doesn’t translate to 30, 40 percent more ads, because Democratic candidates are raising more money and candidates get to pay less money for their ads. So every outside dollar that comes in has to pay a different rate than the candidate has to pay.

In the House races, it’s closer to even. There’s a slight Republican edge, but again, Democratic candidates tend to be raising more, so on points, they’re doing better. There’s differences in spending by race, but those seem to be intentional strategic differences; different parties have different places. There’s not a huge margin there.

In terms of how the president is spending his money, there was concern—we reported back in August—concern that he was gonna spend all this money on himself. And it’s true: He has spent a portion of the money on ads about how great he is, which is not something Republicans wanna see. It’s something Democrats love to see. It really doesn’t clearly affect in positive way these races. That message works if you narrowcast it to Trump supporters. It doesn’t really work on broadcast TV when Trump is polling at 30 percent in the state.

But in terms of the other spending, you can’t really separate the Trump spending from the ecosystem spending, and that’s because it’s all being coordinated. So what the Senate Leadership Fund, Congressional Leadership Fund, those are the outside groups tied to the Republican leaders in Congress; what Elon Musk is spending—all of that’s being coordinated. And so they’re basically divvying it up amongst themselves and operating as a single unit.

So to say, Well, the president’s priorities are here, and other people’s priorities are there—with the exception of that money that he spent on his own vanity ads, it’s all part of a single strategy.

Harris: Thinking about the spending from Musk, the spending from the president, the spending from the various PACs that are out there, spending from candidates themselves, Bloomberg sort of giving $80 million to Democrats. The money is becoming almost exorbitant to the point of nonsensical. And I wonder: Does having more money at this point even really matter?

Parker: The airwaves, at the end of the day, can get saturated. In terms of TV broadcast, at the end of the day, there is a finite amount of ads you can buy. And if you’re traveling to cover a race, and you are in Maine, or you are in Iowa, or you are in Texas, it is possible you turn on the TV and all you see are political ads. Gone are the somewhat sketchy prescription-drug ads, and it’s just candidate ads.

But I think the money does matter.

If you look at how much money they’re pouring into Texas to help prop up a pretty mediocre Republican Senate candidate in Ken Paxton, there is a sense that if if Elon Musk had not put in the money he did, if Trump had not put in the money he did, that Ken Paxton would be floundering. And it’s also an open question about: If they weren’t putting all that money into Texas, could that money be more helpful in some of these other states that are close?

So I think at the end of the day, money still matters tremendously.

Scherer: The political science on this is that if two sides spend roughly equal amounts of money, they kinda cancel each other out. And generally, that’s true, unless you have new information being presented in the ads that really changes people’s mind, and I think that’s why you’ve seen a lot of data-center ads. It’s new information that’s being added to some of these races, and that’s mostly benefited Democrats.

What’s interesting is the late spending. So, the Senate Leadership Fund had just bought a bunch more ads, and they were buying ads in Mississippi, Kansas, South Carolina, and Nebraska—all deep-red states. There wasn’t very large sums of money, but that’s a situation where they’ve seen things in their polling that has made them panic at the late stage. They’re diverting money into deep-red areas, and I think spending in places where there is not a lot of spending, or where people aren’t used to it, could have a better, bigger effect. The real thing Republicans are after right now is not persuasion; it’s turnout. So that’s the other question here. There’s a lot of advertising that’s going on TV, but there’s also a lot of advertising that the Trump team is doing, for instance, on Facebook, using the list they have of Trump voters saying, You really have to get out to vote. Go vote; go vote; go vote. A lot of the Elon Musk–spending is that. All the polling we have says those voters don’t wanna vote right now. They’re just not as excited as they were in 2024. But if they’ve found a new magic formula to do that, and that turns up on Election Day, it could have an impact.

Harris: Is that also why you’re seeing the president go and host these sort of boots-on-the-ground rallies in these really deep-ruby-red states, where people might say, Your energies may be spent better elsewhere, in states where races are more competitive?

Parker: That is precisely why. I was with the president in Grand Island, Nebraska, which is a very rural part, and red part, of an already red state. And it seemed confusing. At first, the thinking was Trump is just going on a glory days tour. He just likes the adulation and these are places he can go. And that’s all true. He does like the adulation. But in talking to his advisers, they say they track the numbers before he goes into the state and when he leaves, and those trips are all about turnout.

Because you have to keep in mind that the Trump coalition that got him elected was a very unlikely coalition of people who specifically liked him, and so that’s why you see him saying, Pretend that I’m on the ballot. And those are people who don’t regularly vote in presidential elections, let alone in off-year midterms. And so, if Trump coming to your city and you standing in line and going to a rally can make you remember to mail in your ballot or go early or show up on Election Day, they believe that’s worth it.

Scherer: The problem is that if you’re in a state like Nebraska or Kansas, more than half the people are Trump supporters, naturally. If you’re in a state where it’s less than half, and only 30 percent of the people approve of Trump at this moment—which is a lot of states right now—and he says, Pretend I’m on the ballot, that means something very different for the 70 percent who don’t like him and the 30 percent who like him. So there’s a risk to that. Really what they wanna be doing is narrowcasting that message just to the hardcore believers.

Parker: Right. They don’t wanna turn out Democrats.

Scherer: But Trump doesn’t always get that nuance and is not convinced that he needs to be cautious.

Harris: And actually, to that point—where it could turn out Democrats—we are seeing in the early turnout numbers that they’re outpacing 2018, outpacing 2022 in places like Georgia and among Latino voters overall, who are largely dissatisfied. That coalition that the president had built in 2024 is deteriorating to an extent, if the poll numbers are taken at face value. And historically Democrats vote early; Republicans typically vote day. What are we learning from the early-voting numbers at the moment?

Scherer: I have a rule I adopted after the 2020 or 2016 race to never count early-vote numbers to mean anything. And the reason is that, like you said, different populations vote at different rates. Every election is very different. Midterms are very different from presidentials, and every district is reporting their own early votes. And so what you end up getting is like this barrage of numbers. It is clear that Democrats right now are voting with more enthusiasm and higher numbers than the past, but I don’t know what that means. Those Republicans can still go to the polls on Election Day. It doesn’t mean they’re not gonna vote. So I don’t know what any of it means. You just can’t. If person X has not yet voted, you don’t know whether they’re going to vote. And that’s the problem with relying too heavily on the early-voting numbers.

Parker: And good campaigns have lists and rolls that tell them person X hasn’t voted yet but probably supports you. So there’s a world in which, three days before Election Day, they get a phone call from their neighbor, and the knock on the door from the canvas, and someone from their church offering to drive them to the polls. So I’m with Michael.

Harris: When we come back, we discuss the issues driving voters to the polls.

There was an AP story that I was reading where they were talking about the F-series truck, the F-150, and it’s the most purchased car in America, and it has been for some while. And then you have the Iran war that’s really unpopular with voters as well. As we’ve reported, the president is considering another escalation in the Middle East. What is the play there? Why is the president considering this sort of reescalation in Iran when it is already a deeply unpopular war with voters?

It was blown up by the Iran war. If gas goes up, Democrats do better, Trump does worse, and reverse.

And gas became very expensive in September, which totally explains the drop in polls. It’s gotten slightly better since then. It’s not a lot better, but it’s not getting worse, at least right now. If you were to open your reporter’s notebooks, what are some of the things that you will be thinking about and looking for from the president and the administration, and from Democrats as well, in the last couple of days?

Parker: So two things. I think on the investigations front you’re absolutely right. That is the biggest reason Donald Trump wants Republicans to win—is because he wants to shield himself and his staff from what will be myriad investigations. But I think one thing on the Democratic side is there is a risk of overreach. Remember, this is an affordability election. So there is a risk that Democrats can potentially go too far in their investigations in trying to impeach everyone. So that’s a debate the Democrat caucus will have to have themselves.

I’m also interested—there is this sense that if Democrats retake control of both chambers of Congress, that Congress will finally again be a check on Donald Trump because Republicans have fundamentally abdicated that role.

But my counterintuitive take is: I just don’t know how realistic that is. If Democrats win, especially in the Senate, it is not going to be by huge margins. It is going to be incredibly tight, and Donald Trump has mastered the art and the willingness of doing things without congressional approval, in the face of congressional approval, through executive orders, and other creative levers of power, and sort of saying, I’m gonna smash through everything until a court or someone stops me.

Harris: And once it’s broken, it’s harder to put back together.

Parker: Right. Once you’ve taken a wrecking ball to the East Wing and a court ruling says you weren’t allowed to do it—well? So I am skeptical of how effective Congress may be in Trump’s last two years if he chooses to try to bulldoze them.

Scherer: Trump needs money from Congress. He’s gotta pay for this war; he’s gotta fund his government. The more Democrats in power, the harder that is for him to get his priorities. They’re gonna have more leverage over him. He’s gonna have to eventually bend on some things. Parker: Yeah, exactly.

He’s got the Olympics coming up. He’s got tariffs still he can play with, and he has an ability to pick fights all the time that have nothing to do with legislation, which he’ll continue to do.

They say that patriotism is the last refuge of a scoundrel, but President Trump and his allies have found a new low: open bigotry against Muslims.

The bar for what can shock has been raised steadily over the past decade, but even by Trump-era standards, remarks that the president made yesterday at a rally in Port Huron, Michigan, are astonishing: a straightforward assertion that a Muslim candidate for office is a terrorist by virtue of his faith.

Trump was campaigning for Mike Rogers, the Republican candidate for U.S. Senate in Michigan, when he attacked Rogers’s opponent, the Democrat Abdul El-Sayed. “I’m here to keep a jihadist Muslim Brotherhood member from becoming a United States senator,” Trump said. “If Abdul and the Democrats win, they will open the floodgates and invite hordes of jihadists to our shores, creating a nightmare of violence and terror until you cannot even have a Christmas.” Trump also said that “anyone with that record of support for jihadists would be screened and unable to get a seat on an airplane.” He added that if elected, El-Sayed “would be the only senator that will have to go through a metal detector before he’s allowed to go into the Senate.” (In fact, the only time that members of Congress have had to pass through metal detectors in the Capitol was on the House side, as a security precaution after Trump supporters sacked it on January 6, 2021.)

To be clear: El-Sayed has disavowed the Muslim Brotherhood, and no one has produced any evidence that he has ties to the group. Neither has anyone produced any evidence that El-Sayed has ties to any jihadists or is one himself. If he did, the Justice Department would presumably be prosecuting him—it certainly hasn’t shied away from politicized cases under Trump.

The president and anyone else are free to critique El-Sayed’s views on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, or any other policy matter, but that’s not what this is. Asking a candidate to disavow a foreign political group that has no American arm is a nonsensical and prejudiced thing to do, but Republicans have been trying hard in the past couple of weeks to draw some connection between El-Sayed and the group, despite a lack of evidence. (They have been assisted by allies in the press, including Bari Weiss, the editor in chief of CBS News, whose staffers complained to Semafor that she is disproportionately focused on candidates such as El-Sayed because of her own views on Israel. CBS News called the report “baseless.”) Perhaps this is because they believe “Muslim Brotherhood” sounds shadowy and scary to voters. The new tack is also silly because the GOP previously spent months trying to connect El-Sayed to the Democratic Socialists of America, another group that he says he is not a member of. Historically, the Brotherhood, an Islamist organization, has typically clashed with socialists in the Muslim world.

Trump’s idea of what a senator should be is an older white man. That’s not a guess—it’s what he said about Rogers, the Republican candidate. “I call him apple pie,” Trump said. “He’s like, perfect, this guy. He’s like central casting. He’s what your state is all about.” As I’ve written in the past, it’s not a dog whistle if everyone can hear it. Trump has also made light of El-Sayed’s full name, Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed, mocking the prevalence of Muhammad in Muslim names. (The president’s own middle name is John.)

“Donald Trump and J. D. Vance, their whole campaign, Mike Rogers’s whole campaign, is to point at me and my name and my faith,” El-Sayed said in response at a rally yesterday evening. “But my name and my faith are not the reason you can’t afford your gas. My name and my faith are not the reason you can’t afford your groceries. My name and my faith are not the reason you can’t see a doctor.”

Trump’s latest remarks about El-Sayed are of a piece with a series of racist and Islamophobic remarks. Two frequent targets of late are Representative Ilhan Omar, the Minnesota Democrat, and the Somali community in Minnesota. He has called Omar “garbage” and said that Somalia is “filthy, dirty, disgusting, dirty.” He has labeled Somalis “low-IQ” but also complained that they are highly effective grifters.

Politicians allied with Trump are also getting in on the hate. Earlier this year, I wrote about a spree of bigoted remarks by GOP members of Congress. More recently, Representative Tom Emmer, a Minnesota Republican, said that Somali Americans should assimilate or “go the hell back.” (He added that he wasn’t afraid of being called an Islamophobe.) And Texas Governor Greg Abbott, who is in a tough reelection race, falsely claimed that his Democratic opponent, Gina Hinojosa, “supports Sharia law” because she received an endorsement from the Council on American-Islamic Relations. You don’t have to think too hard to guess why CAIR opted to back someone running against Abbott.

With this kind of talk, it’s no wonder that Muslim voters who backed Trump in 2024, largely out of frustration with the Biden administration’s policies toward Israel, are now angry and disillusioned, as The New York Times reports. (Anyone is entitled to cast a protest vote, but given Trump’s long record of anti-Muslim bigotry, those who lined up behind Trump shouldn’t be surprised.)

Islamophobic rhetoric deserves condemnation on its own terms, but the attacks don’t just affect El-Sayed, Omar, or even Muslim communities in the United States. During the same speech, Trump—who has previously said that Jewish Democrats are “disloyal to Jewish people”—called for ostracizing any Jewish Michiganders who vote for El-Sayed. “Any Jewish person that supports this guy should be ashamed of themselves,” he said. “You should never speak to them again.” Once bigotry against one community enters the mainstream, it tends to spread quickly to other groups.

By Elaine Godfrey

At 2 o’clock on Sunday afternoon, I found myself in a corner booth at the Cheesecake Factory, emerging from the fog of war.

Before me were a dozen half-empty plates, a cluster of dirty napkins, and several depleted beverages. The food had come in waves, unrelenting, like Normandy. Maybe you’ve seen the ad: A man and a woman are loading a shopping cart. The woman tells the man he must return a carton of eggs. “We have to set that $7 aside for killing Palestinians.”

The woman explains that grocery prices are rising because the United States gives so much aid to Israel. “It’s costing us a lot of money to drop bombs on Gaza,” the woman explains. “Don’t they get universal health care too?” the man asks. “We’re paying for that, too, probably,” the woman responds, at which point the man puts back the bananas.

The ad was produced by an independent political-action committee to support the Senate campaign of the Michigan Democrat Abdul El-Sayed. During the Democratic primary, El-Sayed regularly denounced American supporters of Israel as a shadowy, sinister special interest. Since winning the nomination, he has distanced himself from his earlier language. Now a super PAC is reviving it for him only a week before the general election.

The legendary journalist Hunter S. Thompson explained the ad half a century before it was produced. In his book Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail ’72, he described “one of the oldest and most effective tricks in politics,” which may have played a role in one of Lyndon Johnson’s early campaigns in Texas:

The race was close and Johnson was getting worried. Finally he told his campaign manager to start a massive rumor campaign about his opponent’s life-long habit of enjoying carnal knowledge of his own barnyard sows.

“Christ, we can’t get away calling him a pig-fucker,” the campaign manager protested. “Nobody’s going to believe a thing like that.”

“I know,” Johnson replied. “But let’s make the sonofabitch deny it.”

In the case of the ad supporting El-Sayed, the sonofabitch targeted by the false accusation is not El-Sayed’s theoretical opponent, the Republican Mike Rogers—not really. The ad concludes, “Mike Rogers is for”—ironic chuckle—“they/them.” We all know who they are, and they are the real target of the ad.

[James Loeffler: The hatred that broke American politics]

The accusation that Israel is to blame for America’s economic problems is not some unique quirk of El-Sayed supporters. During his campaign to become New York City’s mayor, Zohran Mamdani told Politico in 2025 that the dilapidated state of the city’s housing was the fault of the same villain: “The government refuses to fund public housing, all while we continue to find billions of dollars to drop bombs that kill tens of thousands of Palestinians over more than a year now.”

That same year, Marjorie Taylor Greene drew a similar link. American aid, she charged, typically accounts for up to a fifth of Israel’s defense budget, “even though Israel, nuclear-armed Israel, has universal healthcare for their citizens and subsidized college for their citizens. Here in America, we’re $37 trillion in debt.”

On Meet the Press in August, El-Sayed insinuated that U.S. aid for weapons in Israel was somehow an impediment to health care at home. “This campaign was a referendum on whether or not we want health care here or bombs somewhere else.”

Do supporters of Israel have to deny the false accusation? Okay, here goes. U.S. military aid to Israel is set at $3.8 billion a year from 2019 to 2028—about one-20th of 1 percent of the total federal budget. In 2024, Congress approved a $95 billion aid package for Ukraine, Israel, and other allies—of which, nearly two-thirds went to Ukraine, about $15 billion went to Israel, and about $9 billion was directed to humanitarian relief, including assistance for Gazan civilians displaced by the war that Hamas started. (The rest went to Taiwan and other allies in the Indo-Pacific region.)

Almost all U.S. assistance to Israel takes the form of credits that Israel can use only to buy American-made equipment. This assistance provides at least two direct and immediate benefits to Americans. First, it buys the United States access to Israeli military technology. By partly underwriting the development of Israel’s Iron Dome missile-defense system, the U.S. has gained the use of battle-tested systems that also protect American lives. Second, this assistance lowers the per-unit cost of U.S. systems. The United States buys up to 48 F-35 fighters a year. Israel has bought 75 and is considering an estimated 25 more. Foreign orders allow U.S. arms manufacturers to improve their economies of scale. At a time when other NATO allies are canceling their orders of American equipment because they no longer fully trust the U.S. as an ally or supplier, Israeli orders enhance the efficiency of American defense production.

[Helen Lewis: The firebrand]

Meanwhile, all U.S. aid to Israel amounts to a fraction of what Israel’s critics wish to spend domestically. Despite the implications of that El-Sayed ad: Americans spend $1.1 trillion a year on groceries. Canceling aid to Israel would deliver the equivalent of about 30 hours of that spending. Per Mamdani’s complaint that Israel is to blame for bad housing in New York City: The city’s housing authority estimates its backlog of deferred maintenance at almost $80 billion, or about 20 years’ worth of U.S. assistance to Israel at present levels. Expanding Medicare to cover all American citizens would cost perhaps more than $32 trillion in additional federal spending over the first decade. Covering even one month of this plan would take 70 years’ worth of aid to Israel.

By blaming the Jewish state for the price of bananas in Michigan, the super-PAC ad calls to mind political tricks older and more disturbing than the one attributed to Johnson. In 1899, Karl Lueger, the mayor of Vienna and one of the most notorious anti-Semites of the 19th century, gave a speech blaming the Habsburg empire’s economic troubles on its Jews. “Here in our Austrian fatherland the situation is such that the Jews have seized a degree of influence which exceeds their number and importance,” he argued. “And in this respect the Jews operate a terrorism of a kind that could hardly be worse. For us, in Austria, it is a matter of liberating Christian people from the hegemony of Jewry.”

As with the rising cost of living in the U.S., the problem Lueger was concerned with was real. His voter base of artisans and craftspeople was coming under immense pressure from factory competition. There was not much Lueger could do about his voters’ distress. But he could point them toward a politically useful target. Now, with a modest update of Lueger’s language, an outside PAC’s closing argument in one of the most watched Senate races of 2026 blames a familiar “they/them.”

How Long Until the Internet Breaks?

On Wednesday, July 18, 2001, a freight train derailed in Baltimore’s Howard Street Tunnel. Flammable liquid ignited, causing a fire that raised the temperature in the tunnel to 1,000 to 1,500 degrees. “We’re told some of the boxcars are actually glowing,” a fire-department spokesperson told the Associated Press. More than 100 firefighters were called to fight the blaze.

The National Transportation Safety Board investigated the fire but never figured out what went wrong. No one was killed, and other trains were rerouted to avoid the inferno. But more than trains navigated the tunnel’s brick-­lined walls: Several internet-service providers and telecommunications companies were running cables through it. The internet couldn’t be so easily retransmitted. The cables that wound their way through the tunnel were damaged, and web traffic as far away as Atlanta and Los Angeles slowed to a crawl. One company that measures web performance said“the internet delays were the worst it had seen,” reported the Associated Press. A single tunnel was responsible for much of the country’s web traffic, and Americans paid the price.

The internet isn’t what we think. It is physical, subject to the vagaries of nature and time, just as our bodies are. Like us, it will flicker and fade. The internet’s fragility crops up everywhere: in the sudden disappearance of a relied-­upon website, and in the endless thrum of posts, images, and videos.

In the case of the Howard Street Tunnel, the fragility became, without warning, quite obvious. As the writer Maria Farrell and the technologist Robin Berjon note in their 2024 essay titled “We Need to Rewild the Internet,” WorldCom—a ­major phone-and-internet company at the time—played an important role in the outages that followed the inferno. “WorldCom had spread web traffic over different fiber networks,” they write, but “the region’s narrow physical topography had concentrated all those different fiber networks into a single chokepoint, the Howard Street Tunnel. WorldCom’s resilience was, quite literally, incinerated. It had technological redundancy, but not diversity.” Despite dealing in the physical world—in the fiber-­optic cables that make up the real, material internet—WorldCom had somehow forgotten that the land makes its own rules.

Bottlenecks can have their benefits. Efficiencies can be found in transmitting data through companies that consolidate the services we need to be online. In 2021, Fastly, a cloud-utility provider, stopped working for an hour, taking down the websites of major news outlets including CNN, The New York Times, and The Guardian. The Verge, a technology-news site, ended up publishing updates about the outage via Google Docs while its website was down. The day of the disruption, Fastly’s stock price actually rose. “Investors were delighted by headlines that informed them of an obscure technical service provider with an apparent lock on an essential service,” Farrell and Berjon write. “To investors, this critical infrastructure failure doesn’t look like fragility but like a chance to profit.” Fragility and profit go hand in hand, which makes some sense when you remember that corporate redundancy always carries inefficiency. Brittleness is big business, a feature and not a bug of the cloud economy.

But the cloud obscures the very real collection of companies, people, and processes that create and structure the internet. When we put something in the cloud, we don’t just offload the responsibility of maintaining our data to fickle chunks of magnetic metal alight with electricity; we also offload that responsibility to a rather limited set of fickle human institutions. You might want your family photos to live in the cloud for centuries, but “most companies do not last long,” Maxwell Neely-­Cohen wrote for the Harvard Law School’s Library Innovation Lab. “They get acquired or split up into pieces or go bankrupt or decline into something much smaller or are upended by catastrophic geopolitical events.”

[Read: Inside the dirty, dystopian world of AI data centers]

The cloud also shrouds the cables, data centers, and transmission towers that compose the internet itself. “The cloud is made possible by a relatively small number of undersea cables that require constant maintenance,” continued Neely-­Cohen. For a medium so often praised as decentralized, for a metaphor that calls to mind something diffuse, that’s an awful lot of centralization. A small number of companies own the data centers, connected by a small number of cables also owned by a small number of companies, through which data, yet again owned by a small number of companies, flow. The internet, in this telling, looks much more like the Howard Street Tunnel than a cloud.

And now artificial intelligence has gusted in, revealing the physical infrastructure that the cloud used to hide. Ordinary people can see more clearly than ever the precarity of the supply chains and the power of the chips that fill the water-guzzling, electricity-hungry data centers that so many have come to loathe.

Just as AI reveals the physicality of our digital lives, it has also brought into sharp focus the crucial pieces of software that undergird the internet. Last week, an AI model found security vulnerabilities in PHP, a coding language used in 70 percent of websites. Despite its centrality to the modern web, I’d hazard a guess that PHP isn’t a household name. Originally named as an acronym for personal homepage, it was built to help host its creator’s résumé. As PHP grew in popularity, it shed some of that DIY spirit—and that acronym—but, like so many essential systems that keep the web online, volunteers remain an essential part of its maintenance.

[Read: ‘If anything happens to this guy … everyone is screwed’]

In a classic comic about the state of modern infrastructure, a precarious Jenga tower is held up by a single block labeled “a project some random person in Nebraska has been thanklessly maintaining since 2003.” AI—swarming onto rarely used Wikipedia pages, probing poorly run government websites, discovering zero-day exploits—has shown the web to be the frail, uncanny place that it has always been. Indeed, the most cloudlike part of this state of affairs is the absence of human agency.

As users of the web, we have to pay attention to the data and infrastructure that the cloud cloaks. Hordes of flesh-­and-­blood engineers, sitting in glass-­and-­steel office buildings and water-­and-­air-­cooled data centers, attend to the systems that hold our words, pictures, and videos. What we store and what we transmit are acts of attention to a world that remains resolutely material, despite the seeming immateriality of our digital lives. Human institutions remain central to the practice of storage and transmission—an inconvenient fact, because human institutions fade, just as human bodies do. The cloud is refreshed every moment of every day by the fresh attention of humans. Just as manure, in balance, can encourage the dogwood to flower, so, too, does our care—over generations—make the internet bloom.

Updated at 2:27 p.m. ET on October 26, 2026

Nothing makes me more nauseated than a row of multiple-choice questions and empty bubbles. After I turned 18, I thought I’d never encounter that feeling again, outside of stress dreams about forgetting my No. 2 pencils. And yet there I was, hiding in a cubicle at work to take a test intended for high schoolers, demonstrating my understanding of apostrophe placement and verb tense, and comparing the definition of truth in passages from Nietzsche and JFK.

I was taking the Classic Learning Test, an alternative to the SAT and ACT. The test is only 11 years old, and was originally taken mainly by kids who were homeschooled or had attended private religious academies. But use of the test is growing at an astonishing pace in red states where there’s a belief that the SAT and ACT have neglected the Western canon in an attempt to become “woke.” In the past three years, public universities in Florida, Arkansas, Texas, Georgia, Indiana, and Iowa announced that applicants could submit their CLT scores in place of SAT and ACT results. Oklahoma, Louisiana, and Wyoming now allow students to use their CLT scores to qualify for state scholarships. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth recently called it the “gold standard” exam, and starting this year, military-service academies will accept CLT scores too. Some 200,000 kids will take the test this year. The problem is that the test is too easy.

The CLT was created by Jeremy Tate. As a remedial-English teacher at a public high school in New York, and then a college-admissions counselor in Maryland, he had become disillusioned with the American education system and what he saw as its emphasis on preparing students for the workforce. “What every other generation was doing in education was fundamentally a different kind of project,” he told me. They were doing two things: “moral formation” and the “transmission of this cultural, intellectual, Western inheritance.” College-entrance exams inform high-school curricula. Because the SAT and ACT emphasize rote reading and math skills, that’s much of what schools teach. But if students had to take a classical-learning test, Tate believed, schools would be forced to teach the Western canon.

In 2015, Tate opened the for-profit Classic Learning Initiatives and started administering the CLT. Students are recorded as they take the two-hour test online, to prevent cheating. The exam is divided into three sections: verbal reasoning, grammar and writing, and math. Most of the texts are taken from a list of classics beginning with The Epic of Gilgamesh and ending with Toni Morrison. The math section covers algebra, geometry, trigonometry, and mathematical reasoning.

Tate presents the CLT as more rigorous than the SAT and the ACT. The CLT is the only one that doesn’t allow calculators, and its reading passages are longer than those on the SAT. But his biggest sell is on the substance of the texts. The other tests, he argues, have altered their content to push “anti-Western left-wing ideology.”

(Representatives for the ACT and SAT denied that the tests had any ideological leaning, or any agenda beyond assessing college readiness. Granted, it’s been several years since I took either one, but I recall more questions about the definition of words such as zenith and benediction than any progressive polemics.)

[Idrees Kahloon: Actually, the SAT was necessary]

Tate’s objection is a bit surprising when you remember that, for years, criticism of the SAT and ACT has mainly been a left-wing phenomenon. Some progressives oppose all standardized testing as inequitable, giving an unfair advantage to children from wealthy backgrounds and cutting students with lower scores off from future opportunities, while also failing to measure qualities such as creativity and grit. During the beginning of the pandemic, in 2020, many universities, including all of the Ivy League schools, made the SAT and ACT optional for applications. But since then, most top schools have reinstated the test requirements, finding that they’re the most reliable way to predict students’ abilities. Now, suddenly, the tests are being attacked from the other side.

Eventually, Tate intends for his test to dwarf the SAT and ACT. He has a long way to go; last year, 2 million graduating seniors had taken the SAT at some point during high school, and 1.4 million had taken the ACT. More than two dozen states, about half of which have Republican leaders, have all public-school students take the SAT or ACT. But Tate believes that Americans are waking up to the testing companies’ “hollowing out and politicization” of schools. “I think word is getting out more and more among students that the SAT and ACT just aren’t really serious,” he told me. Although Harvard and MIT do not accept the CLT, Tate said that a growing number of students are submitting CLT scores to impress their admissions offices. (An MIT spokesperson told me over email that CLT scores are not considered in admissions decisions. The not was underlined.)

Tate claims that the CLT, in contrast to the SAT and ACT, is not political. Yes, Christopher Rufo, the anti-critical-race-theory activist, is on the board of Classic Learning Initiatives. But so is Cornel West, the public intellectual who calls himself a “non-Marxist socialist.” “He’s a good friend,” Tate told me. (West did not reply to a request for comment.) There’s nothing partisan, Tate argues, about valuing rigor and revering old books. Recently, the CLT’s X account posted a collage celebrating what it called “arguably the 50 most influential books in history.” The image appears to be AI-generated; it includes The Aeneid by “Virmer.”

For the first eight years of its existence, only about 20,000 students took the CLT. But in 2023, Florida Governor Ron DeSantis got into a fight with the College Board over its AP African American Studies course, which he accused of indoctrinating students in left-wing ideology. DeSantis seized on CLT as an alternative. Florida began to allow high-school students to use their CLT score to apply to the state’s public universities and its biggest scholarship program. Among students who weren’t destined for college, those who failed Florida’s end-of-semester exams could use a CLT score to fulfill their English and math graduation requirements. Over the next two years, Florida’s high-school graduation rate increased by four percentage points.

In school-board meetings, administrators credited the CLT for the boost. The superintendent of Hillsborough County, which serves more than 200,000 public-school students, called the CLT a “pretty big bullet.” More than 70 percent of the county’s seniors who could not pass the state’s math exam were passing the CLT’s math section. For the English section, that number was more than 30 percent. Many school officials think this is a good thing, presumably, because it makes the schools look better. The CLT “allows students who never before would have gotten a high-school diploma to get a high-school diploma,” a Monroe County school-board member said in a meeting earlier this year. “It doesn’t have them college-ready, but typically those students aren’t heading to college, so this is enormous.” It’s unclear exactly how much of the rise in graduation rates can be attributed to the CLT; Florida’s Department of Education did not respond to a request for comment. But officials in at least seven other districts also claimed that the test helped students graduate, USA Today reported.

The graduation jump clearly suggests that the test is easier than the state’s own exams, but how the CLT measures up against other assessments is surprisingly hard to figure out. The Classic Learning Initiatives recently gave a team of independent researchers access to the test and the evidence behind it. The researchers—who work at Arizona State University, the University of Cincinnati, the University of Alabama, and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign—compared the CLT’s content with the SAT and ACT. The group didn’t set out to measure rigor overall, but it found that of the three tests, the CLT contains the highest proportion of questions about number sense and pre-algebra, which are typically taught in middle school. (The CLT had about double the proportion on the ACT and six times the proportion on the SAT.)

[From the September 2026 issue: Bring back the blue-book exam]

Noah Tyler, the CLT’s chief financial officer, told me that this finding doesn’t mean the test is easier. The math section emphasizes mathematical reasoning, he said. For example, one question asks students to determine how many numbers between zero and 400 are both odd and a perfect square. “So even if it is some quote, unquote ‘middle-school content,’ it’s extremely hard. It’s dialed up to 11.” (The independent researchers confirmed that the CLT contained a higher proportion of reasoning questions.)

The researchers found that the verbal-reasoning section was also less cognitively complex than the SAT and ACT. Some students might struggle with the exam’s classic texts, which can contain arcane references and convoluted sentence structures. But the questions themselves tend to ask for a lower level of inference and interpretation than the SAT and ACT, according to Mark Sulzer, an associate professor at the University of Cincinnati and a member of the research team. Compared with the others, the CLT has more questions that direct students to a specific point in the passage and ask them to rephrase the information that’s already there, Sulzer told me.

My experience with the test confirmed this. It was daunting to start the timer and immediately plunge into a passage by Charles Dickens. But the questions—asking, for example, if a character  delivered a letter with enthusiasm, annoyance, or another emotion—didn’t demand much understanding of the author’s work.

Tyler argued that the Florida graduation rate isn’t a fair measure of the test’s difficulty. Although many students pass Florida’s graduation cutoff, few earn a high score. A perfect score on the test is 120—40 on each section. Only 43 students got a 40 on the CLT math section in 2025, Tyler said. And none of them also aced the reading and grammar. “We wanted to make sure there were plenty of differentiators at the top. And so we left it hard,” Tyler told me. “We want to set an anchor and say: Let the whole world rise to the occasion.”

That may be true, but most aren’t taking the test to aim high. The company’s internal data indicate that about two-thirds of test-takers are using the exam not to apply to college at all but to satisfy Florida’s high-school requirements. To do that, they have to earn only a combined score of 36 on the verbal reasoning and grammar sections, and a 14 on the math.

The biggest problem is that it’s not clear what a good score on the test even is. Most companies that administer standardized tests go through a standard-setting process to determine which scores classify students into different proficiency levels. The company that administers the CLT hasn’t done that, the researchers said.

The company has put the test through a “concordance” process—basically, a way to determine what, say, a score of 85 on the CLT would be roughly equivalent to on the ACT (which has 36 total points) or the SAT (which has 1,600). The Classic Learning Institute told Florida officials that a 420 on the SAT math section—considered its passing score—was equivalent to an 11 on the CLT. That’s why Florida first adopted a score of 11 as the threshold necessary for high-school students to graduate.

But researchers say there were major weaknesses in the company’s process. How the institute determined the score was “so methodologically flawed, it’s, like, shocking,” said Audrey Amrein-Beardsley, a professor of teaching and learning innovation who heads the group of researchers studying the CLT. For one, Tate’s organization used fewer than 5,000 students to determine which SAT and CLT scores are equivalent, a sample size that is 100 times smaller than the one the SAT and ACT used. The company could access official SAT and CLT scores for only about half of that group. The rest were self-reported. When the organization studied whether official scores lined up with self-reported ones for a group of students in that sample, it found that about a quarter did not match. “If they were to put any of these technical reports up for peer review, the peer reviewers would literally eat them alive,” Amrein-Beardsley told me.

Florida seems to have recognized that it set the bar too low. This year, the state raised the threshold to pass the math section to 14. The state, Amrein-Beardsley said, is just throwing spaghetti at the wall: “If it doesn’t stick again, they’ll try again,” she told me. “That’s not the appropriate way to do it, but that is the way it’s being done.”

Tyler objected to the researchers’ findings, saying that his organization hasn’t yet been given the chance to review their work. “Before really doing her homework or even releasing a draft, she’s got these grand conclusions about our project and whether we ought to cease and desist,” he told me. “That seems totally inappropriate to me.”

[Walt Hunter: Stop meeting students where they are]

Amrein-Beardsley emphasized that nothing is inherently wrong with the content of the CLT. And more standardized-testing options could be a good thing. Her conclusion is “not that the CLT can never become a good alternative, but that the current evidence does not justify placing it on equal evidentiary footing with the SAT and ACT, especially for high-stakes uses.”

The way to know for sure if a test is rigorous enough is to see how it predicts students’ college performance. But because the CLT is only about a decade old, little evidence exists to show how test-takers do once they matriculate. Currently, only three studies have examined the question. Two compared CLT scores with first-year GPAs at Grove City College and Franciscan University, private Christian schools that prepare many of their graduates for the clergy. The third looked at how 259 students did in their first year at five small Texas universities. A team of Texas A&M researchers who analyzed the CLT reported that, while there was enough data for the scores to be factored into admissions decisions, more evidence was needed before the CLT could be considered as reliable an assessment as the SAT or ACT.

A confession: I breezed through the test’s reading and grammar sections, as well as through the beginning of the quantitative-reasoning section: If there are seven male and nine female chimpanzees, and six male chimpanzees are removed, what is the probability of selecting a female chimpanzee from the remaining group? But then I got to trigonometry and realized that I had forgotten everything I’d learned in high school. I already had a degree, I remembered, and was free to quit at any time. And so I did.  

Critics on the left say that all standardized testing is tiring and time-consuming. Critics on the right say that these tests are still the best way to determine what students are capable of. I think they’re both correct. But if we’re going to require students to take these tests, we should at least make sure they’re worth the effort.

For years, red-state politicians have criticized progressives for eroding the education system in service of an ideological agenda. They’ve promised a return to high standards and accountability. And yet, more and more seem to be jettisoning their states’ best measures of accountability in order to win an ideological argument. Does anyone think this is going to work out well for students? The correct answer is no.
